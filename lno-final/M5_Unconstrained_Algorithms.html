Notation used (one letter = one meaning on this page):

  • $\nabla f(x)$ = the gradient (vector of partial derivatives); $g_k=\nabla f(x_k)$, the gradient at step $k$. ($g$ always means a gradient here.)
  • $d_k$ = the direction we walk in at step $k$; $\alpha_k\gt 0$ = the step length (how far we walk along $d_k$).
  • $\phi(\alpha)=f(x_k+\alpha d_k)$ = "$f$ along the line" (a function of one number $\alpha$); $\phi'$ = its derivative. (His Ch 2 notes call this $g(\alpha)$; we use $\phi$ because $g$ is the gradient.)

0. Why this topic exists

We want the lowest point of a function $f(x)$, with no constraints on $x$. Chapter 1 told you how to recognise a minimiser: $\nabla f(x^*)=0$ and the Hessian is positive semidefinite. But "solve $\nabla f(x)=0$" is a system of $n$ nonlinear equations. For real problems you can't solve it by hand, and $n$ may be in the thousands.

So instead we walk downhill. Start anywhere, look at the slope, take a step that goes down, and repeat until the slope is (nearly) zero. Every method in this module is a version of that walk. They differ in only two choices:

  • Which way to step (the direction $d_k$): straight down the slope (steepest descent), a curvature-corrected direction (Newton), or a direction that doesn't undo earlier progress (conjugate gradient, CG).
  • How far to step (the step length $\alpha_k$): the best possible along the line (exact line search), a fixed number, or anything "good enough" (Armijo, Wolfe).

Why quadratics get so much attention. Near a minimiser, every smooth function looks like a quadratic (that's Taylor's theorem). So how fast a method finishes is decided by how it behaves on $f=\tfrac12x^TQx-b^Tx$. On a quadratic everything can be computed exactly, which is why the exam questions are on quadratics.

A. The descent template

A1. Which directions go downhill?

Plain words. Stand at a point $x$. If you take a tiny step $t$ in direction $d$, the height changes by about "slope along $d$" × $t$. The slope along $d$ is the dot product $\nabla f(x)^Td$. That comes from first-order Taylor:

$$f(x+td)\approx f(x)+t\,\nabla f(x)^Td\qquad(t\text{ small}).$$

So if $\nabla f(x)^Td\lt0$, small steps along $d$ go down.

With numbers (this function is used all through A and B)

$f(x)=x_1^2+4x_2^2$ at $x=(2,1)$. Then $f(x)=4+4=8$ and $\nabla f=(2x_1,\,8x_2)=(4,8)$.

Definition: descent direction

$d_k$ is a descent direction at $x_k$ if $\ \nabla f(x_k)^Td_k\lt0$. Then $f(x_k+\alpha d_k)\lt f(x_k)$ for all small enough $\alpha\gt0$.

Write $\theta_k$ for the angle between $d_k$ and $-g_k$: $\ \cos\theta_k=\dfrac{-g_k^Td_k}{\|g_k\|\,\|d_k\|}$. Descent ⇔ $\cos\theta_k\gt0$ ⇔ $d_k$ is less than 90° away from $-g_k$.

Level curves of $f=x_1^2+4x_2^2$ (ellipses). At $x=(2,1)$ the gradient (red) points uphill, perpendicular to the level curve. Every direction pointing into the green half-plane (the side of the dashed tangent line away from $\nabla f$) is a descent direction. $-\nabla f$ (blue) is the steepest one.

Why $-\nabla f$ is "steepest". For a unit-length $d$, $\nabla f^Td=\|\nabla f\|\cos(\text{angle between them})$. That is most negative when the angle is 180°, i.e. $d=-\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$.

The template every method in this module follows

$$x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_k\,d_k,\qquad\text{with }d_k\text{ a descent direction and }\alpha_k\gt0.$$

Repeat until $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|$ is tiny. The methods differ only in how they choose $d_k$ (sections D–H) and $\alpha_k$ (section B).

A2. Turning the step-length choice into a 1-D problem: $\phi(\alpha)$

Once $x_k$ and $d_k$ are fixed, the only unknown is the number $\alpha$. So look at $f$ along the line:

$$\phi(\alpha)=f(x_k+\alpha d_k),\qquad \phi'(\alpha)=\nabla f(x_k+\alpha d_k)^Td_k\ \ \text{(chain rule)},\qquad\phi'(0)=g_k^Td_k\lt0.$$

$\phi(0)=f(x_k)$ is where we stand. $\phi'(0)$ is the starting slope, negative because $d_k$ is a descent direction.

With numbers

Same $f$, $x_k=(2,1)$, $d_k=-\nabla f=(-4,-8)$. Then $x_k+\alpha d_k=(2-4\alpha,\ 1-8\alpha)$ and

$$\phi(\alpha)=(2-4\alpha)^2+4(1-8\alpha)^2=(4-16\alpha+16\alpha^2)+(4-64\alpha+256\alpha^2)=8-80\alpha+272\alpha^2.$$

$\phi'(\alpha)=-80+544\alpha$, so $\phi'(0)=-80$, the same as $g_k^Td_k=-80$ from the table ✓. We use this $\phi$ for every rule in section B.

B. Choosing the step length $\alpha$ (Chapter 2)

B1. Exact line search: the best $\alpha$ along the line

Idea. Pick the $\alpha$ that makes $\phi$ smallest: $\alpha_k=\arg\min_{\alpha\gt0}\phi(\alpha)$. In our example $\phi'(\alpha)=-80+544\alpha=0$ gives $\alpha^*=\frac{80}{544}=\frac{5}{34}\approx0.147$, with $\phi(\alpha^*)=\frac{36}{17}\approx2.12$ (down from 8).

For any quadratic $f=\tfrac12x^TQx-b^Tx$ (here $Q$ is a fixed symmetric matrix with all eigenvalues positive and $b$ a fixed vector; its gradient is $g=Qx-b$; section C studies these properly) there is a formula. Expand: $\phi(\alpha)=f(x_k)+\alpha\,g_k^Td_k+\tfrac12\alpha^2\,d_k^TQd_k$. Set $\phi'(\alpha)=g_k^Td_k+\alpha\,d_k^TQd_k=0$:

$$\boxed{\alpha_k=-\frac{g_k^Td_k}{d_k^TQd_k}}\qquad\text{and for }d_k=-g_k:\quad\boxed{\alpha_k=\frac{g_k^Tg_k}{g_k^TQg_k}}.$$

Check: $x_1^2+4x_2^2=\tfrac12x^T\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&8\end{pmatrix}x$, so $d^TQd=2\cdot16+8\cdot64=544$ and $-g^Td=80$: $\alpha=80/544=5/34$ ✓.

Why not always do this? For a non-quadratic $f$ there's no formula. Finding the exact minimiser of $\phi$ takes many evaluations of $f$, and the next iteration throws the line away anyway. So in practice we accept any step that is "good enough". The rest of B says what "good enough" means.

B2. "Just make $f$ go down" is not enough. Armijo: not too long

Two ways to fail while $f$ decreases every step (take $f(x)=x^2$, minimiser 0):

  • Steps too short: $x_k=1+2^{-k}$: $2,\ 1.5,\ 1.25,\dots$ Every step lowers $f$, but the steps shrink so fast that you stop at 1, not 0.
  • Steps too long: $x_k=(-1)^k(1+2^{-k})$: $2,\ -1.5,\ 1.25,\dots$ Every step lowers $f$, but you bounce across the valley and end up oscillating between $\pm1$.

So we need a rule that demands a real decrease (stops long, bouncing steps) and a rule that forbids tiny steps.

Armijo (sufficient decrease). The tangent line at $\alpha=0$ "promises" a decrease of $\alpha|\phi'(0)|$. Demand that you get at least a fraction $c_1$ of that promise:

$$\boxed{\phi(\alpha)\le\phi(0)+c_1\,\alpha\,\phi'(0)},\qquad 0\lt c_1\lt1\ \text{(typically small, e.g. }0.1\text{ or }10^{-4}).$$

Picture: the right side is a straight line starting at $\phi(0)$, less steep than the tangent. Armijo asks the curve to lie below this line.

With numbers ($c_1=0.1$)

Armijo line: $8+0.1\cdot\alpha\cdot(-80)=8-8\alpha$. Condition: $8-80\alpha+272\alpha^2\le8-8\alpha\iff272\alpha^2\le72\alpha\iff\alpha\le\frac{72}{272}=\frac{9}{34}\approx0.265$.

So Armijo accepts every $\alpha\in(0,\,9/34]$. It caps the step from above. But it also accepts $\alpha=0.0001$, which barely moves: Armijo alone does not stop too-short steps.

B3. Not too short: Wolfe's curvature condition, strong Wolfe, Goldstein

Idea. If at your chosen $\alpha$ the curve is still going down steeply, you stopped too early: going further would clearly help. So demand that the slope has flattened enough:

$$\textbf{Wolfe curvature:}\quad\boxed{\phi'(\alpha)\ge c_2\,\phi'(0)},\qquad 0\lt c_1\lt c_2\lt1.$$

Both sides are slopes; the right side is a negative number. "≥" means the slope at $\alpha$ is less negative than $c_2$ times the starting slope. Near $\alpha=0$ the slope is still about $\phi'(0)$, which is below $c_2\phi'(0)$, so tiny steps fail. Wolfe conditions = Armijo + curvature. ($c_1\lt c_2$ guarantees that some $\alpha$ passes both, for any $f$ that is bounded below along the line.)

Strong Wolfe replaces the curvature condition by $\ |\phi'(\alpha)|\le c_2|\phi'(0)|$. This also forbids landing where the slope is steeply positive, i.e. far past the bottom of the dip.

Goldstein uses two straight lines and no derivative at $\alpha$ (needs $0\lt c\lt\tfrac12$):

$$\phi(0)+(1-c)\,\alpha\,\phi'(0)\ \le\ \phi(\alpha)\ \le\ \phi(0)+c\,\alpha\,\phi'(0).$$

The right inequality is Armijo with $c_1=c$. The left one is a lower fence: near $\alpha=0$ the curve hugs its tangent, which lies below the steeper fence line, so tiny steps fail. (Drawback: on a non-quadratic $\phi$ the fence can cut off the true minimiser of $\phi$.)

With numbers: all four rules on $\phi(\alpha)=8-80\alpha+272\alpha^2$

Take $c_1=0.1$, $c_2=0.5$, Goldstein $c=0.25$. Recall $\phi'(\alpha)=-80+544\alpha$, $\phi'(0)=-80$.

  1. Armijo (B2): $\alpha\le9/34\approx0.265$.
  2. Curvature: $-80+544\alpha\ge0.5\cdot(-80)=-40\iff\alpha\ge\frac{40}{544}=\frac{5}{68}\approx0.074$. Wolfe interval: $[5/68,\ 9/34]$.
  3. Strong Wolfe: $-40\le-80+544\alpha\le40\iff\frac{5}{68}\le\alpha\le\frac{120}{544}=\frac{15}{68}\approx0.221$.
  4. Goldstein: upper: $8-80\alpha+272\alpha^2\le8-20\alpha\iff\alpha\le\frac{60}{272}=\frac{15}{68}$; lower: $8-80\alpha+272\alpha^2\ge8-60\alpha\iff\alpha\ge\frac{20}{272}=\frac{5}{68}$. Interval $[5/68,\,15/68]$ (equal to strong Wolfe here only because of these constants).
Which $\alpha$ each rule accepts for our $\phi$ ($c_1=0.1$, $c_2=0.5$, $c=0.25$). Armijo alone allows steps near 0. The curvature condition removes them. Plain Wolfe still allows big overshoots past $\alpha^*$ (up to $9/34$, where the slope is $+64$); strong Wolfe cuts those off.

Why you need both Wolfe conditions (he stressed this, see "What he said"). Armijo alone accepts tiny steps. Curvature alone accepts huge ones: $\alpha=0.3$ has $\phi'(0.3)=83.2\ge-40$, so it passes curvature, yet $\phi(0.3)=8.48\gt8$, so $f$ went up.

His Exercise 2.3: the general intervals for a quadratic $\phi$

$\phi(\alpha)=\phi(0)-\sigma\alpha+\tfrac{\omega}{2}\alpha^2$ with $\sigma,\omega\gt0$ (so $\phi'(0)=-\sigma$ and $\phi'(\alpha)=-\sigma+\omega\alpha$). Our example is $\sigma=80$, $\omega=544$.

  1. Exact minimiser: $\phi'(\alpha)=0\Rightarrow\alpha^*=\sigma/\omega$.
  2. Armijo: $-\sigma\alpha+\tfrac{\omega}{2}\alpha^2\le-c_1\sigma\alpha\iff\alpha\le\dfrac{2(1-c_1)\sigma}{\omega}$. So $\alpha\in(0,\,2(1-c_1)\alpha^*]$.
  3. Curvature: $-\sigma+\omega\alpha\ge-c_2\sigma\iff\alpha\ge(1-c_2)\alpha^*$. Wolfe interval: $[(1-c_2)\alpha^*,\ 2(1-c_1)\alpha^*]$.
  4. Strong Wolfe adds $-\sigma+\omega\alpha\le c_2\sigma\iff\alpha\le(1+c_2)\alpha^*$. Interval: $[(1-c_2)\alpha^*,\ \min\{1+c_2,\,2(1-c_1)\}\alpha^*]$.
  5. Goldstein: $\alpha\in[2c\,\alpha^*,\ 2(1-c)\alpha^*]$. Since $c\lt\frac12$, it always contains $\alpha^*$.
  6. Lesson: ordinary Wolfe can accept steps up to almost $2\alpha^*$ (a big overshoot); strong Wolfe limits the overshoot to $(1+c_2)\alpha^*$.

B4. Backtracking: the practical Armijo rule

Try $\alpha=1$. If Armijo fails, multiply by a fixed $s\in(0,1)$ and try again: $\alpha=1,\,s,\,s^2,\dots$ Accept the first $\alpha$ that passes Armijo.

Why no curvature check is needed: you start big and shrink by a fixed factor, so you can't end up absurdly small. The accepted step is at least $s$ times a step that failed. Starting at $\alpha=1$ matters for Newton's method (section G), where $\alpha=1$ is the natural full step.

With numbers ($c_1=0.1$, $s=\tfrac12$)

$\alpha=1$: $\phi(1)=200\gt8-8=0$ ✗. $\alpha=\tfrac12$: $\phi=36\gt4$ ✗. $\alpha=\tfrac14$: $\phi=8-20+17=5\le6$ ✓. Accept $\alpha=0.25$. Note that it overshoots $\alpha^*\approx0.147$ and fails strong Wolfe ($\phi'(0.25)=+56$): Armijo only caps the step.

  • Armijo bounds $\alpha$ from above only; curvature/Goldstein-left bound it from below. You cannot drop either Wolfe condition in general.

B5. Golden section: a 1-D search with no derivatives

Setting. $\phi$ is unimodal on a bracket $[a,b]$: it goes down, then up, with one dip. You can only evaluate $\phi$.

Idea. Put two interior points in the bracket and compare $\phi$ there. The dip can't be beyond the worse point (unimodality), so throw that outer piece away. To save work, place the points so that the surviving interior point can be reused in the next round: one new evaluation per round.

Deriving the golden fraction
  1. Bracket $[0,1]$, points at $\rho$ and $1-\rho$ (symmetric, $\rho\lt\tfrac12$).
  2. Say $\phi(\rho)\le\phi(1-\rho)$. Keep $[0,\,1-\rho]$ (length $1-\rho$). Its right interior point should sit at fraction $1-\rho$ of the new length: $(1-\rho)^2$.
  3. Reuse means that point is the old $\rho$: $(1-\rho)^2=\rho\iff\rho^2-3\rho+1=0\iff\rho=\dfrac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\approx0.382$.
  4. Each round multiplies the bracket length by $1-\rho=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}\approx0.618$, with one new evaluation.

Numbers. On $[0,10]$: points $3.82$ and $6.18$. If $\phi(3.82)\le\phi(6.18)$, the new bracket is $[0,6.18]$, whose points are $0.382\times6.18=2.36$ (new) and $0.618\times6.18=3.82$ (old, reused). After 4 rounds the length is $10\times0.618^4\approx1.46$.

B6. Zoutendijk's theorem in plain words

Question. If we only use "good enough" steps, do we still reach a point where $\nabla f=0$? Answer: yes, provided the directions don't become nearly perpendicular to the gradient.

Zoutendijk

If $f$ is bounded below, $\nabla f$ is Lipschitz (it can't change arbitrarily fast), the $d_k$ are descent directions and every $\alpha_k$ satisfies the Wolfe conditions (B3; strong Wolfe also works, since it implies Wolfe), then $$\sum_k\cos^2\theta_k\,\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2\lt\infty.$$ So if $\cos\theta_k\ge\delta\gt0$ for all $k$ (the angle stays bounded away from 90°), then $\|\nabla f(x_k)\|\to0$.

The Ch 2 notes state the theorem with strong Wolfe steps. The standard theorem needs only the ordinary Wolfe conditions (Armijo + curvature); strong Wolfe is a special case. So if an MCQ asks what is needed: Wolfe steps (not exact line search).

Reading it: each step makes "useful progress" of about $\cos^2\theta_k\|g_k\|^2$. The total progress is finite (because $f$ is bounded below), so these terms must shrink to 0. If the cosine can't shrink, the gradient must.

The standard way to guarantee the angle

Use $d_k=-D_kg_k$ where $D_k$ is symmetric with eigenvalues in $[m,M]$, $m\gt0$. Then

  • $-\nabla f^Td=\nabla f^TD\nabla f\ge m\|\nabla f\|^2$ (smallest eigenvalue), so $d$ is a descent direction;
  • $\|d\|=\|D\nabla f\|\le M\|\nabla f\|$ (largest eigenvalue);
  • so $\cos\theta=\dfrac{-\nabla f^Td}{\|\nabla f\|\|d\|}\ge\dfrac{m\|\nabla f\|^2}{\|\nabla f\|\cdot M\|\nabla f\|}=\dfrac{m}{M}\gt0$.

Steepest descent is $D=I$: $\cos\theta=1$. Newton with a PD Hessian whose eigenvalues stay in $[m,M]$ is $D=H^{-1}$: also covered.

C. Quadratics: the test case for every method

C1. The quadratic, its gradient, its minimiser, its error

$$f(x)=\tfrac12x^TQx-b^Tx,\qquad Q=Q^T\succ0.$$

$Q\succ0$ (positive definite) means $v^TQv\gt0$ for every $v\ne0$; equivalently, every eigenvalue of $Q$ is positive. It makes $f$ a bowl with exactly one bottom.

Gradient by hand on a 2×2, so you trust the formula

$Q=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: $\ \tfrac12x^TQx=\tfrac12(3x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2)$, so $f=\tfrac32x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2-b_1x_1-b_2x_2$.

$\partial f/\partial x_1=3x_1+x_2-b_1$, $\ \partial f/\partial x_2=x_1+2x_2-b_2$. That's exactly the vector $Qx-b$.

Facts about the quadratic (used everywhere below)
  • Gradient $g(x)=Qx-b$; Hessian $=Q$ (constant).
  • Minimiser: $Qx^*=b$, so $x^*=Q^{-1}b$ (unique since $Q\succ0$).
  • Error $e=x-x^*$. Then $g=Qx-b=Qx-Qx^*=\boxed{Qe}$: the gradient is $Q$ times the error.
  • $f(x)-f^*=\tfrac12e^TQe=\tfrac12\|e\|_Q^2$. (Expand $f(x^*+e)=f^*+(Qx^*-b)^Te+\tfrac12e^TQe$ and use $Qx^*=b$.)
Reading $Q$ off a polynomial

$f=3(x_1^2+x_2^2)+4x_1x_2+\dots$: the Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\4&6\end{pmatrix}$. The diagonal is twice the coefficient of $x_i^2$ (because of the ½), and the off-diagonal is the whole coefficient of $x_1x_2$ (here 4, not 2): $\partial^2(4x_1x_2)/\partial x_1\partial x_2=4$. Safest: take the second derivatives directly.

C2. Eigen-directions: the natural axes of the bowl

$Q$ symmetric has $n$ perpendicular unit eigenvectors $q_1,\dots,q_n$ with $Qq_i=\lambda_iq_i$. Write any error as $e=\sum_i\varepsilon_iq_i$ (its components along the eigenvectors). Then:

  • $Qe=\sum_i\lambda_i\varepsilon_iq_i$: $Q$ just multiplies the $i$-th component by $\lambda_i$. No mixing between components.
  • $f-f^*=\tfrac12e^TQe=\tfrac12\sum_i\lambda_i\varepsilon_i^2$. A level set is an ellipse (ellipsoid) with axes along the $q_i$. Along $q_i$ its half-width is proportional to $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$: large eigenvalue = steep, narrow direction; small eigenvalue = flat, long direction.
  • $\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ measures how stretched the ellipses are. $\kappa=1$: circles.

D. Fixed-step gradient descent on a quadratic (Midterm 1 Q II)

The method: $d_k=-g_k$ and the same $\alpha$ every step: $x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha g_k$.

D1. Deriving the error recursion

  1. Subtract $x^*$ from both sides: $e_{k+1}=e_k-\alpha g_k$.
  2. Use $g_k=Qe_k$ (C1): $\ \boxed{e_{k+1}=(I-\alpha Q)e_k}$. (Multiplying by $Q$ gives the same recursion for gradients: $g_{k+1}=(I-\alpha Q)g_k$.)
  3. Apply it to one eigenvector: $(I-\alpha Q)q_i=q_i-\alpha\lambda_iq_i=(1-\alpha\lambda_i)q_i$.
  4. So if $e_k=\sum_i\varepsilon_i^{(k)}q_i$ ($\varepsilon_i^{(k)}$ = the component of the error along $q_i$ after $k$ steps), then $\varepsilon_i^{(k+1)}=(1-\alpha\lambda_i)\,\varepsilon_i^{(k)}$, and after $k$ steps $\ \boxed{\varepsilon_i^{(k)}=(1-\alpha\lambda_i)^k\,\varepsilon_i^{(0)}}$.

In words: the error splits into independent pieces, one per eigen-direction. Each step multiplies piece $i$ by the fixed number $1-\alpha\lambda_i$, its multiplier. Everything (convergence, cycling, divergence, best step, rate) is read off from these multipliers.

D3. Convergence range, best step, rate

  • Converges from every start ⇔ every multiplier has $|1-\alpha\lambda_i|\lt1$ ⇔ $0\lt\alpha\lambda_i\lt2$ for all $i$ ⇔ $\boxed{0\lt\alpha\lt2/\lambda_{\max}}$.   • Worst-case factor per step on $\|e_k\|$: $\max_i|1-\alpha\lambda_i|=\max\{|1-\alpha\lambda_{\min}|,\,|1-\alpha\lambda_{\max}|\}$ (the extremes matter, since $1-\alpha\lambda$ is a straight line in $\lambda$).
  • Best $\alpha$: as $\alpha$ grows, the first term falls and the second rises; the max is smallest where they balance: $1-\alpha\lambda_{\min}=-(1-\alpha\lambda_{\max})$, so $\ \boxed{\alpha_{\rm opt}=\dfrac{2}{\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max}}}$, with factor $\ \boxed{\dfrac{\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}}=\dfrac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}}$ on $\|e\|$ (square it for $f-f^*$).

D4. Cycling, divergence and special starts

  • Multiplier exactly $-1$: that piece flips sign forever: the iterates jump between two points (a 2-cycle). $f(x_k)$ stays constant, but the iterates don't converge (Midterm 1 II(3): objective values converging doesn't mean iterates converge).
  • Multiplier with $|\cdot|\gt1$: that piece grows: divergence unless the start has zero component along that eigenvector. A zero stays zero forever, so from such starts (in 2-D: a line through $x^*$, the "eigen-line") the iteration still converges.
  • Adding a linear term $b^Tx$ moves $x^*$ but not the Hessian, so the range $0\lt\alpha\lt2/\lambda_{\max}$ doesn't change.

E. Steepest descent with exact line search (Midterm 1 Q III)

The method: $d_k=-g_k$, and $\alpha_k$ = the best step along that line (B1), recomputed every iteration. You no longer need to know the eigenvalues to pick $\alpha$.

E1. The step, and why the path zigzags

  1. $\phi(\alpha)=f(x_k-\alpha g_k)$. The gradient at the new point is $g_k-\alpha Qg_k$, so $\phi'(\alpha)=-(g_k-\alpha Qg_k)^Tg_k=-g_k^Tg_k+\alpha\,g_k^TQg_k$.
  2. $\phi'(\alpha)=0$: $\ \boxed{\alpha_k=\dfrac{g_k^Tg_k}{g_k^TQg_k}}$.
  3. At that $\alpha_k$, $\phi'(\alpha_k)=-g_{k+1}^Tg_k=0$. So $\boxed{g_{k+1}^Tg_k=0}$: each new gradient is perpendicular to the previous one. Geometrically: you walk until the line just touches a level ellipse; there the new gradient (perpendicular to the ellipse) is perpendicular to the line you walked. Consecutive steps are at right angles: a zigzag.
By hand: $Q=\mathrm{diag}(1,4)$, $b=0$, $x_0=(4,1)$ (so $x^*=0$, $f^*=0$)
  1. $g_0=Qx_0=(4,4)$. $g_0^Tg_0=32$, $Qg_0=(4,16)$, $g_0^TQg_0=16+64=80$, $\alpha_0=32/80=2/5$.
  2. $x_1=(4,1)-\tfrac25(4,4)=(2.4,\,-0.6)$. $g_1=(2.4,\,-2.4)$, and $g_1^Tg_0=9.6-9.6=0$ ✓.
  3. $f(x_0)=\tfrac12(16+4)=10$, $f(x_1)=\tfrac12(5.76+1.44)=3.6$. Ratio $3.6/10=9/25$.
  4. Next: $\alpha_1=2/5$ again, $x_2=(1.44,\,0.36)=\tfrac{9}{25}x_0$. The pattern repeats: $x_3=(0.864,-0.216)$, $x_4=(0.518,0.130)$, … with $f$ shrinking by $9/25$ every step, forever.

E2. How fast: the Kantorovich bound, in words

Each exact step reduces $f-f^*$ by at least a fixed fraction, and that fraction is worst when the ellipses are very stretched:

$$f(x_{k+1})-f^*\ \le\ \Big(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\Big)^2\big(f(x_k)-f^*\big).$$

The exact one-step formula (from the Ch 3 notes, with $E_k=f(x_k)-f^*=\tfrac12e_k^TQe_k$) is $E_{k+1}=\Big[1-\dfrac{(g_k^Tg_k)^2}{(g_k^TQg_k)(g_k^TQ^{-1}g_k)}\Big]E_k$; Kantorovich's inequality bounds the bracket by $\big(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\big)^2$.

In the example above $\kappa=4$: bound $(3/5)^2=9/25$, and the actual ratio was exactly $9/25$. So that start is a worst case: the bound is attained. Same factor as fixed-step GD with the best $\alpha$, but without needing to know the eigenvalues.

E3. The effective condition number

Suppose $g_0$ has components only along some of the eigenvectors (index set $J$). Then every later gradient also stays there, because $g_{k+1}=(I-\alpha_kQ)g_k$ and $I-\alpha_kQ$ multiplies each eigen-component by a number: a component that is zero stays zero. So the method never "sees" the other eigenvalues. Replace $\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}$ by the smallest and largest eigenvalue within $J$: the effective $\kappa$.

Example: $Q=\mathrm{diag}(1,2,10,50)$, $x_0=(0,1,1,0)$, $b=0$: $g_0=(0,2,10,0)$ involves only λ = 2 and 10. Effective $\kappa=5$, bound $(4/6)^2=4/9$ instead of $(49/51)^2$ (Practice N7).

E4. Killing a known eigenvalue with one fixed step (Midterm 1 Q III)

Write $\eta$ for a fixed step length (the same role as a fixed $\alpha$; Midterm 1 III uses $\eta$). A gradient step with $\eta=1/\mu$, where $\mu$ is an eigenvalue of $Q$, multiplies the $\mu$-component of the error by $1-\mu/\mu=0$ (D1 did this with $\alpha=1/10$). Two such steps with $\eta_0=1/\mu_1$ and $\eta_1=1/\mu_2$ (either order) remove two known outlier eigenvalues $\mu_1,\mu_2$, without knowing the eigenvectors: after both steps the error has been multiplied by $P(Q)$ with $P(t)=(1-\eta_0t)(1-\eta_1t)$, which is zero at $t=\mu_1$ and $t=\mu_2$. (Midterm 1 III, Practice P2, calls the two outliers $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$.)

Traps in E
  • Exact line search reaches $x^*$ in one step iff $g_0$ is an eigenvector of $Q$ (his Ch 3 Ex 2): then $-g_0$ points straight at $x^*$.
  • The Kantorovich factor is for $f-f^*$ (or $\|e\|_Q^2$). For $\|e\|_Q$ take the square root.
  • "Consecutive gradients orthogonal" is for steepest descent; in CG all gradients are mutually orthogonal (H7).

F. The $\|x\|^p/p$ family: when the gradient isn't Lipschitz (Midterm 1 Q V)

Everything in D assumed a quadratic: the gradient changes at a bounded rate (it's "Lipschitz"). This family breaks that, and shows what goes wrong. His Ch 3 Exercise 7 is the case $p=3/2$; Midterm 1 Q V was "a modification of exercise 7".

The function: $f_p(x)=\frac1p\|x\|^p$ with $1\lt p\lt2$, minimiser $0$.

  1. Gradient. $f_p=\frac1p(x^Tx)^{p/2}$, so $\nabla f_p(x)=\frac1p\cdot\frac p2(x^Tx)^{p/2-1}\cdot2x=\|x\|^{p-2}x$ for $x\ne0$, and $\nabla f_p(0)=0$ (since $|f_p(h)-f_p(0)|/\|h\|=\frac1p\|h\|^{p-1}\to0$).
  2. Why it's not Lipschitz. $\|\nabla f_p(x)-\nabla f_p(0)\|/\|x\|=\|x\|^{p-2}\to\infty$ as $x\to0$ (the power $p-2$ is negative). 1-D picture for $p=1.5$: the slope is $\sqrt{|x|}$, so near 0 the slope changes infinitely fast. The bottom is a "sharpened" bowl.
  3. Fixed step. $x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha r_k^{p-2}x_k=(1-\alpha r_k^{p-2})x_k$ with $r_k=\|x_k\|$. So $\boxed{r_{k+1}=r_k\,|1-\alpha r_k^{p-2}|}$: the multiplier now depends on where you are, and blows up as $r_k\to0$.
  • $f$ increases ⇔ $|1-\alpha r_k^{p-2}|\gt1$ ⇔ $\alpha r_k^{p-2}\gt2$ ⇔ $0\lt r_k\lt(\alpha/2)^{1/(2-p)}$. Too close to 0 ⇒ thrown out. (Raising to the negative power $1/(p-2)$ flips the inequality.)
  • $r_0=(\alpha/2)^{1/(2-p)}$: multiplier $-1$, iterates $x_0,-x_0,x_0,\dots$
  • $r_0=\alpha^{1/(2-p)}$: multiplier 0, lands on 0 in one step. Convergence to 0 can only happen by hitting 0 exactly, or not at all.
  • Exact line search along $-\nabla f_p$: $\alpha_k=r_k^{2-p}$, lands exactly on 0.

G. Newton's method (Ch 2 in 1-D, Ch 3 in n-D)

Idea. Steepest descent uses only the slope. Newton also uses the curvature: replace $f$ near $x_k$ by its second-order Taylor model (a parabola / quadratic bowl) and jump straight to the bottom of the model.

G1. One dimension

  1. Model: $q(x)=f(x_k)+f'(x_k)(x-x_k)+\tfrac12f''(x_k)(x-x_k)^2$.
  2. Bottom of the model: $q'(x)=f'(x_k)+f''(x_k)(x-x_k)=0$, so $\ \boxed{x_{k+1}=x_k-\dfrac{f'(x_k)}{f''(x_k)}}$. (This is Newton root-finding applied to $f'$.)
  3. This is the bottom only if $f''(x_k)\gt0$. If $f''(x_k)\lt0$ the model is an upside-down parabola and the formula jumps to its top.
Failure 1: negative curvature sends you uphill

$f(x)=\tfrac14x^4-\tfrac12x^2$ at $x_0=0.5$: $f'=x^3-x=-\tfrac38$ (downhill is to the right), $f''=3x^2-1=-\tfrac14\lt0$.

Newton step $p=-f'/f''=-\dfrac{-3/8}{-1/4}=-\tfrac32$: to the left (the full step would jump to $x_1=-1$). Slope along it: $f'\cdot p=(-\tfrac38)(-\tfrac32)=+\tfrac{9}{16}\gt0$: an ascent direction. A small step $0.1p$ goes to $x=0.35$, where $f=-0.0575\gt f(0.5)=-0.109$: $f$ went up.

Failure 2 (his Exercise 2.6): convex, yet Newton fails. $f(x)=\sqrt{1+x^2}$
  1. $f'(x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$, $f''(x)=(1+x^2)^{-3/2}\gt0$: strictly convex, minimiser $x^*=0$.
  2. Newton: $x_{k+1}=x_k-\dfrac{x_k(1+x_k^2)^{-1/2}}{(1+x_k^2)^{-3/2}}=x_k-x_k(1+x_k^2)=\boxed{-x_k^3}$.
  3. $|x_0|\lt1$: converges to 0 extremely fast ($0.5\to-0.125\to0.00195\to-7.5\times10^{-9}$). $|x_0|=1$: cycles $1,-1,1,\dots$ $|x_0|\gt1$: diverges ($1.5\to-3.375\to38.4\to\dots$).
  4. For $|x|\gt1$ the Newton direction is still downhill (it has the sign of $-f'$), but the full step overshoots and increases $f$. A line search (damping, G2) fixes this.
At $x_0=1.5$ the curvature $f''=0.17$ is small, so the Newton model (purple dashed) is a very flat parabola whose bottom is far to the left. The step lands at $x_1=-3.375$, higher on the curve ($f=3.52$ vs $1.80$).

G2. Many dimensions

Model: $m_k(p)=f(x_k)+g_k^Tp+\tfrac12p^TH_kp$, with $H_k=\nabla^2f(x_k)$. Gradient of the model $=g_k+H_kp=0$:

$$\boxed{H_k\,p_k=-g_k,\qquad x_{k+1}=x_k+p_k}.$$

Solve the linear system; don't form $H_k^{-1}$.

  • One step solves a quadratic. If $f=\tfrac12x^TQx-b^Tx$ with $Q\succ0$, the model is $f$: $x_1=x_0-Q^{-1}(Qx_0-b)=Q^{-1}b=x^*$ from any $x_0$.
  • Descent needs a PD Hessian. $g_k^Tp_k=-p_k^TH_kp_k$ (substitute $g_k=-H_kp_k$). If $H_k\succ0$ this is negative: descent. If $H_k$ is indefinite the Newton direction can point uphill (as in G1), and no amount of damping fixes a non-descent direction.
  • Quadratic convergence, but only nearby. If $\nabla f(x^*)=0$, $\nabla^2f(x^*)$ is nonsingular and the Hessian is Lipschitz near $x^*$, then for $x_0$ sufficiently close to $x^*$: $\|x_{k+1}-x^*\|\le C\|x_k-x^*\|^2$. In words: once close, the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step. Far away there's no guarantee ($\sqrt{1+x^2}$).
  • Damped Newton: $x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kp_k$ with Armijo backtracking starting at $\alpha=1$ (B4). Far away it takes shorter safe steps; near a minimiser with $\nabla^2f(x^*)\succ0$ (and $c_1\lt\tfrac12$) the full step $\alpha=1$ is eventually accepted, so the fast local rate comes back. (Why Newton counts as a "scaled steepest descent": in coordinates $y=H_k^{1/2}x$ the model's Hessian becomes $I$, i.e. the bowl looks round, as in H1. The steepest-descent step there, mapped back to $x$, is exactly $p_k=-H_k^{-1}g_k$.)

Watching quadratic convergence (Practice N6 function)

H. Conjugate gradient (CG), built from zero (Chapter 4)

Throughout H: $f=\tfrac12x^TQx-b^Tx$, $Q\succ0$, $g_k=Qx_k-b$, and every step uses exact line search along its direction.

H1. Why steepest descent zigzags, and the idea that fixes it

The problem. In the zigzag of E1, each line search makes the slope along the current direction zero. But the next step spoils the earlier ones. In the example: after two steps, $x_2=(1.44,0.36)$, $g_2=(1.44,1.44)$, and the slope along the first direction $d_0=-g_0=(-4,-4)$ is $g_2^Td_0=-11.52\ne0$. We'd want to go along $d_0$ again: the work of step 0 was undone. That's why it never finishes.

When does it work? If the level sets were circles ($Q=I$, $f=\tfrac12\|x-x^*\|^2$), a line search along a coordinate axis sets that coordinate exactly right and leaves the others alone. So $n$ line searches along any $n$ perpendicular directions finish exactly. Nothing gets undone.

The trick: make the ellipses round. Change variables $y=Q^{1/2}x$ (where $Q^{1/2}$ is the symmetric matrix with $Q^{1/2}Q^{1/2}=Q$). Then $\tfrac12x^TQx=\tfrac12\|y\|^2$: in $y$-coordinates the level sets are circles. A direction $d$ in $x$ becomes $Q^{1/2}d$ in $y$, and two directions are perpendicular in $y$ exactly when

$$(Q^{1/2}d_i)^T(Q^{1/2}d_j)=d_i^TQd_j=0.$$

So: choose directions that are perpendicular after rounding the bowl. Back in $x$ they don't look perpendicular; they're called Q-conjugate. Line searches along Q-conjugate directions never undo each other, and $n$ of them finish exactly.

H2. Q-conjugacy

Definition

Nonzero vectors $d_0,\dots,d_{k}$ are Q-conjugate if $d_i^TQd_j=0$ for all $i\ne j$.

  • They're linearly independent (so at most $n$ of them). If $\sum_ia_id_i=0$, multiply by $d_j^TQ$: every term dies except $a_jd_j^TQd_j$, and $d_j^TQd_j\gt0$, so $a_j=0$.
  • Eigenvectors of $Q$ are both orthogonal and Q-conjugate ($q_i^TQq_j=\lambda_jq_i^Tq_j=0$). In general, conjugate directions are not orthogonal.
By hand: making a direction conjugate (Quiz 2 Q3)

$Q=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $d_0=(1,0)$, $d_1=(w,1)$ (Quiz 2 calls the unknown $s$). $Qd_1=(3w+1,\ w+2)$, so $d_0^TQd_1=3w+1=0\Rightarrow w=-\tfrac13$. Then $d_0^Td_1=-\tfrac13\ne0$: conjugate but not orthogonal.

H3. The best point of a flat sheet: the gradient is perpendicular to the sheet, not zero

We'll need this: what does it mean to be the minimiser over $\bar x+\mathrm{span}\{d_1,\dots,d_m\}$ (a line, plane, … through $\bar x$)? Put the directions as columns of a matrix $D$. Points of the sheet are $\bar x+Dz$, so define $h(z)=f(\bar x+Dz)$.

  • Chain rule: $\nabla h(z)=D^T\nabla f(\bar x+Dz)$.
  • For convex $f$, $h$ is convex, so the minimiser is where $\nabla h=0$: $\ \boxed{D^T\nabla f(x_S)=0}$, i.e. $\nabla f(x_S)$ is perpendicular to every $d_i$.
  • Not $\nabla f(x_S)=0$: the sheet may simply not contain the true minimiser. No direction inside the sheet goes downhill, but directions out of it might.
  • For the quadratic this is an $m\times m$ system: $(D^TQD)z_S=-D^T\nabla f(\bar x)$.
With numbers

Minimise $f=x_1^2+4x_2^2$ on the line $x=(2,1)+t(-1,0)$: $h(t)=(2-t)^2+4$, $h'(t)=-2(2-t)=0\Rightarrow t=2$, $x_S=(0,1)$. There $\nabla f=(0,8)\ne0$, but $(−1,0)\cdot(0,8)=0$ ✓: perpendicular to the line.

His warning

At the sheet's minimiser $\nabla h=0$ but $\nabla f\ne0$ in general. Writing "$\nabla f(x_S)=0$" is the mistake he said memorisers make.

H4. The expanding subspace theorem (Midterm 1 Q VI asked its steps)

The key formula ("you have to remember"). One step changes the gradient by $\alpha_jQd_j$: $\ g_{j+1}=Qx_{j+1}-b=Q(x_j+\alpha_jd_j)-b=g_j+\alpha_jQd_j$. Adding these up:

$$\boxed{g_i=g_0+\sum_{j\lt i}\alpha_jQd_j}.$$

The theorem in plain words. Take any Q-conjugate $d_0,\dots,d_{K-1}$ and do exact line searches along them one after another. Then after $K$ steps, $x_K$ is the best point of the whole sheet $x_0+\mathrm{span}\{d_0,\dots,d_{K-1}\}$, not just of the last line. Each line search does its own job and never spoils the earlier ones. Consequently $g_K$ is perpendicular to every $d_i$ used so far (H3), and for $K=n$ the sheet is all of $\mathbb R^n$, so $x_n=x^*$.

The proof as a chain of steps (memorise the order; with $c_i=d_i^Tg_0$, $h_i=d_i^TQd_i$)
  1. The sheet objective separates. $\Phi(\beta)=f(x_0+\sum_i\beta_id_i)=f(x_0)+\sum_i\big(c_i\beta_i+\tfrac12h_i\beta_i^2\big)$. (Expand $f(x_0+v)=f(x_0)+g_0^Tv+\tfrac12v^TQv$; the cross terms $\beta_i\beta_jd_i^TQd_j$ die by conjugacy.) Here the $\beta_i$ are just coefficients, not CG's $\beta_k$.
  2. Each piece has one minimiser. $h_i\gt0$ ($Q\succ0$, $d_i\ne0$), so $\beta_i^*=-c_i/h_i$.
  3. Earlier steps don't change the slope along $d_i$. By the key formula, $d_i^Tg_i=d_i^Tg_0+\sum_{j\lt i}\alpha_jd_i^TQd_j=c_i$.
  4. So the line search finds exactly that minimiser. $\alpha_i=-d_i^Tg_i/h_i=-c_i/h_i=\beta_i^*$.
  5. Conclusion. $x_K=x_0+\sum\beta_i^*d_i$ is the minimiser over $x_0+\mathcal S_K$ ($\mathcal S_K=\mathrm{span}\{d_0..d_{K-1}\}$), and $g_K\perp\mathcal S_K$. The order of the directions doesn't matter.

H5. The CG algorithm: conjugate directions built on the fly

We need Q-conjugate directions without computing eigenvectors. CG builds them one at a time from the gradients.

  1. Start with steepest descent: $d_0=-g_0$.
  2. Step length by exact line search (B1): $\alpha_k=-\dfrac{g_k^Td_k}{d_k^TQd_k}$; then $x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kd_k$ and $g_{k+1}=g_k+\alpha_kQd_k$.
  3. Next direction: the new steepest-descent direction plus a bit of the old direction, $d_{k+1}=-g_{k+1}+\beta_kd_k$, with $\beta_k$ chosen to make $d_{k+1}^TQd_k=0$: $\ -g_{k+1}^TQd_k+\beta_kd_k^TQd_k=0\Rightarrow\beta_k=\dfrac{g_{k+1}^TQd_k}{d_k^TQd_k}$.
  4. The surprise (CG theorem; proof not examinable): making $d_{k+1}$ conjugate to $d_k$ alone automatically makes it conjugate to all earlier directions. So H4 applies, and CG finishes in at most $n$ steps.

Simplifying $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (the efficient forms used in exams):

  • $g_k^Td_k=g_k^T(-g_k+\beta_{k-1}d_{k-1})=-\|g_k\|^2$, because $g_k^Td_{k-1}=0$ (the previous line search). So $\alpha_k=\dfrac{\|g_k\|^2}{d_k^TQd_k}$.
  • Gradients are mutually orthogonal: $g_{k+1}\perp d_0,\dots,d_k$ (H4), and each $g_j$ is a combination of $d_j$ and $d_{j-1}$ (since $d_j=-g_j+\beta_{j-1}d_{j-1}$ gives $g_j=-d_j+\beta_{j-1}d_{j-1}$; for $j=0$, $g_0=-d_0$), so $g_{k+1}^Tg_j=0$ for $j\le k$.
  • From $Qd_k=(g_{k+1}-g_k)/\alpha_k$: $g_{k+1}^TQd_k=\|g_{k+1}\|^2/\alpha_k$ and $d_k^TQd_k=\|g_k\|^2/\alpha_k$. Dividing: $\beta_k=\dfrac{\|g_{k+1}\|^2}{\|g_k\|^2}$.
CG (Fletcher–Reeves form) $$\begin{aligned} &d_0=-g_0;\qquad \alpha_k=\frac{\|g_k\|^2}{d_k^TQd_k};\qquad x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kd_k;\qquad g_{k+1}=g_k+\alpha_kQd_k;\\ &\beta_k=\frac{\|g_{k+1}\|^2}{\|g_k\|^2};\qquad d_{k+1}=-g_{k+1}+\beta_kd_k.\qquad\text{Stop when }g_{k+1}=0. \end{aligned}$$

H7. What CG guarantees, and how many steps it needs

  • Gradients orthogonal ($g_i^Tg_j=0$), directions Q-conjugate ($d_i^TQd_j=0$). Never swap these.
  • $g_k^Td_k=-\|g_k\|^2$ and $g_k^Td_i=0$ for $i\lt k$.
  • Krylov space. Recall $g_0=Qe_0$ (C1), so $Q^jg_0=Q^{j+1}e_0$. Each new gradient is the old one plus $\alpha Q\times$(a direction), so one more power of $Q$ enters each step: $\mathrm{span}\{d_0,\dots,d_{k-1}\}=\mathrm{span}\{g_0,Qg_0,\dots,Q^{k-1}g_0\}$. After $k$ steps, $x_k$ minimises $f$ over $x_0+\mathrm{span}\{g_0,Qg_0,\dots,Q^{k-1}g_0\}$ ($k$ vectors, highest power $k-1$, shifted by $x_0$).
  • Decrease per step: $f(x_k)-f(x_{k+1})=\dfrac{\|g_k\|^4}{2\,d_k^TQd_k}$ (put $\alpha_k$ into $\phi(\alpha)=f(x_k)+\alpha g_k^Td_k+\tfrac12\alpha^2d_k^TQd_k$). For Quiz 2's $E=\|x-x^*\|_Q^2=2(f-f^*)$ the decrease is twice that.
  • $f$ always decreases, but $\|g_k\|$ can increase (Quiz 2 Q10: $\|g_0\|=1$, $\|g_1\|=\tfrac32$).

How many steps: count distinct eigenvalues. By the Krylov fact, $x_k-x_0$ is a combination of $g_0,\dots,Q^{k-1}g_0$, i.e. of $Qe_0,\dots,Q^ke_0$. Subtract $x^*$: $x_k-x^*=e_0+(\text{combination of }Qe_0,\dots,Q^ke_0)=R_k(Q)e_0$, where $R_k$ is a polynomial of degree $k$ with $R_k(0)=1$. Conversely, every such polynomial gives a point of the sheet $x_0+\mathrm{span}\{g_0,\dots,Q^{k-1}g_0\}$. By the expanding-subspace result (H4), $x_k$ is the best point of that sheet, so CG automatically picks the best such polynomial (smallest $f$, i.e. smallest error energy). On each eigen-component, $R_k(Q)$ multiplies by $R_k(\lambda_i)$. So:

  • If $e_0$ has nonzero components only on eigenvalues $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_r$ (distinct), the degree-$r$ polynomial $R(t)=\prod_j(1-t/\mu_j)$ has $R(0)=1$ and kills all of them: CG finishes in exactly $r$ steps. Fewer is impossible: a polynomial of degree less than $r$ with value 1 at 0 can't vanish at $r$ distinct points.
  • So: steps = number of distinct eigenvalues whose eigenspaces $e_0$ actually touches (at most $n$).
  • Partial bound: if $m$ outlier eigenvalues lie above an interval $[a,b]$ holding the rest, after $m+1$ steps $E_{\rm after}\le\big(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\big)^2E_{\rm before}$ (use $R(t)=(1-\frac{2t}{a+b})\prod_j(1-t/\mu_j)$ and bound the outlier factors by 1 on $[a,b]$).

H8. Preconditioned CG (PCG)

Idea. CG is fast when the eigenvalues are few or clustered. If they're spread out, change variables to make the problem rounder first. Pick an SPD matrix $B\approx Q^{-1}$ (cheap to apply, e.g. $B$ = inverse of the diagonal of $Q$). With $x=B^{1/2}y$ the Hessian in $y$ is $\hat Q=B^{1/2}QB^{1/2}$, close to $I$ if $B\approx Q^{-1}$. Run plain CG on $y$ and translate back to $x$: every "$g$" inside an inner product becomes "$Bg$".

PCG

$d_0=-Bg_0$, $\ \alpha_k=\dfrac{g_k^TBg_k}{d_k^TQd_k}$, $\ x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kd_k$, $\ g_{k+1}=g_k+\alpha_kQd_k$, $\ \beta_k=\dfrac{g_{k+1}^TBg_{k+1}}{g_k^TBg_k}$, $\ d_{k+1}=-Bg_{k+1}+\beta_kd_k$.

Guarantees: $g_i^TBg_j=0$ (not $g_i^Tg_j$), $d_i^TQd_j=0$, $g_k^Td_k=-g_k^TBg_k$. Speed depends on the eigenvalues of $B^{1/2}QB^{1/2}$. $B=Q^{-1}$ (or $cQ^{-1}$): one step.

Scaling $B$ changes nothing. With $\tilde B=cB$: $\tilde d_k=cd_k$, $\tilde\alpha_k=\alpha_k/c$, $\tilde\beta_k=\beta_k$, so $\tilde\alpha_k\tilde d_k=\alpha_kd_k$ and the iterates are identical. So "eigenvalues of $\hat Q$ near 1" isn't the real criterion: their spread (clustering) is.

Trap (and a disagreement) In class he said that with PCG the original $f(x_k)$ "may not reduce monotonically" (Ch 4 lec 4 [19:30]). Mathematically, $f(x_k)=h(y_k)$ exactly (same point, $x=B^{1/2}y$), and each PCG step is an exact line search for $f$ along $d_k$, so $f(x_k)$ does decrease. What really need not decrease is the Euclidean gradient norm $\|g_k\|$. This is consistent with Quiz 2's key. If it is asked, write this reasoning.

H9. CG on non-quadratic functions (FR, PR)

On a non-quadratic $f$ there is no single $Q$: conjugacy and the $n$-step finish are lost. CG becomes a heuristic that is often faster than steepest descent, but it needs safety checks. His "four things that are different" (write $F(x_k)=\nabla^2f(x_k)$, the Hessian, in place of $Q$):

  1. $\beta_k$ goes back to the original formula with $Q\to F(x_k)$: $\beta_k=\dfrac{g_{k+1}^TF(x_k)d_k}{d_k^TF(x_k)d_k}$.
  2. If $d_k^TF(x_k)d_k\le0$ (no positive curvature along $d_k$), set $\beta_k=0$ (restart with steepest descent).
  3. $\alpha_k$ from a line search (Armijo/Wolfe); there's no closed form. The quadratic-model step $\hat\alpha_k=-g_k^Td_k/d_k^TF(x_k)d_k$ is only a trial value; it can increase $f$.
  4. Descent check: if $g_k^Td_k\ge0$, reset $d_k=-g_k$.

Gradient-only versions: $\beta^{FR}_k=\dfrac{\|g_{k+1}\|^2}{\|g_k\|^2}$ (Fletcher–Reeves), $\beta^{PR}_k=\dfrac{g_{k+1}^T(g_{k+1}-g_k)}{\|g_k\|^2}$ (Polak–Ribière). With exact line search any finite $\beta$ gives $g_{k+1}^Td_{k+1}=-\|g_{k+1}\|^2\lt0$ (descent, not convergence). FR + strong Wolfe with $0\lt c_1\lt c_2\lt\tfrac12$:

$$-\frac1{1-c_2}\le\frac{g_k^Td_k}{\|g_k\|^2}\le\frac{2c_2-1}{1-c_2}\lt0.$$
Plug in (Quiz 2 Q19)

$c_2=\tfrac14$: lower $-\frac1{3/4}=-\tfrac43$, upper $\frac{-1/2}{3/4}=-\tfrac23$. Both negative ⇒ every FR direction is a descent direction.

P2 Midterm 1 Q III (past paper, 20 marks)Descriptive

$f(x)=\frac12x^THx-c^Tx$, $H=H^T\succ0$, $n\ge4$. $n-2$ eigenvalues lie in $[a,b]$; the other two are $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ with $0\lt a\le b\lt\lambda_1\lt\lambda_2$, $b\ll\lambda_1$, known exactly (eigenvectors not given). From any $x_0$ do two gradient steps $x_1=x_0-\eta_0g_0$, $x_2=x_1-\eta_1g_1$, then steepest descent with exact line search $\alpha_k=\frac{g_k^Tg_k}{g_k^THg_k}$ for $k\ge2$. Find $\eta_0,\eta_1$ so that for $k\gt2$ the convergence rate depends only on $a$ and $b$. What is that rate?

Hint

A gradient step multiplies the $\lambda$-component by $1-\eta\lambda$. Which $\eta$ makes that zero?

Show answer
  1. Recurrences (3). $x^*=H^{-1}c$, $e_k=x_k-x^*$, $g_k=He_k$. A step of length $\eta$: $e_{k+1}=(I-\eta H)e_k$ and $g_{k+1}=(I-\eta H)g_k$.
  2. Two preparatory steps (3). $e_2=p(H)e_0$, $g_2=p(H)g_0$ with $p(t)=(1-\eta_0t)(1-\eta_1t)$. The component along an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ is multiplied by $p(\lambda)$. To remove both outliers for every $x_0$ we need $p(\lambda_1)=p(\lambda_2)=0$.
  3. Choice (4). $\boxed{\eta_0=1/\lambda_2,\ \eta_1=1/\lambda_1}$ (or the reverse order). Then $p(\lambda_1)=(1-\lambda_1/\lambda_2)(1-1)=0$, $p(\lambda_2)=(1-1)(1-\lambda_2/\lambda_1)=0$. No eigenvectors needed.
  4. Subspace preserved (3). Let $S$ = span of eigenvectors with eigenvalues in $[a,b]$. Then $e_2,g_2\in S$, and $HS\subseteq S$, so each $I-\alpha_kH$ maps $S$ into $S$: the removed components never come back (exact arithmetic).
  5. Rate (5). On $S$ the effective eigenvalues lie in $[a,b]$, effective $\kappa=b/a$. Exact line search beats the trial step $\bar\alpha=\frac{2}{a+b}$, for which every mode multiplier satisfies $|1-\frac{2\mu}{a+b}|\le q=\frac{b-a}{b+a}$. Hence $$f(x_{k+1})-f^*\le\Big(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\Big)^2\big(f(x_k)-f^*\big),\quad k\ge2,$$ i.e. $\|e_{2+m}\|_H\le q^m\|e_2\|_H$. No $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ in the factor.
  6. (2) If $a=b$ the next exact step finishes; if $g_2=0$ stop.

Checked numerically ($n=8$, outliers 50 and 200, rest in $[1,3]$): after the two steps the outlier components are $\sim10^{-16}$ and the per-step ratios stay below $0.25=((3-1)/(3+1))^2$. In floating point the outliers slowly creep back after many steps; the question assumes exact arithmetic.

P3a Midterm 1 Q V(1)MCQ

Which statement is correct?

  1. (a) $f_p$ is not differentiable at 0.
  2. (b) $f_p$ is differentiable at 0 with gradient 0, but not twice differentiable there.
  3. (c) $f_p$ is twice differentiable at 0, with Hessian 0.
  4. (d) $f_p$ is differentiable at 0 with a nonzero gradient.
Show answer

Answer: (b). $|f_p(h)-f_p(0)|/\|h\|=\frac1p\|h\|^{p-1}\to0$ (since $p\gt1$), so $\nabla f_p(0)=0$. Away from 0, $\nabla f_p(x)=\|x\|^{p-2}x$, and $(\nabla f_p(tu)-0)/t=t^{p-2}u$ is unbounded as $t\downarrow0$ ($p-2\lt0$): no second derivative. (a) fails because $p\gt1$ (it would be true for $p=1$). (c) fails by the blow-up. (d) the gradient is 0 at a minimiser.

Memorise cold: line search (Ch 2)
  • Template $x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kd_k$. Descent direction: $g_k^Td_k\lt0$. Exact step on a quadratic along any $d_k$: $\alpha_k=-\dfrac{g_k^Td_k}{d_k^TQd_k}$; for $d_k=-g_k$: $\alpha_k=\dfrac{g_k^Tg_k}{g_k^TQg_k}$.
  • $\phi'(0)=g_k^Td_k\lt0$. With $0\lt c_1\lt c_2\lt1$:
    Armijo (not too long): $\phi(\alpha)\le\phi(0)+c_1\alpha\phi'(0)$.
    Wolfe = Armijo + curvature (not too short) $\phi'(\alpha)\ge c_2\phi'(0)$.
    Strong Wolfe = Armijo + $|\phi'(\alpha)|\le c_2|\phi'(0)|$.
    Goldstein ($0\lt c\lt\tfrac12$): $\phi(0)+(1-c)\alpha\phi'(0)\le\phi(\alpha)\le\phi(0)+c\,\alpha\phi'(0)$.
    Backtracking: try $\alpha=1,s,s^2,\dots$ ($0\lt s\lt1$), accept the first that passes Armijo.
    Quadratic $\phi$ (Ex 2.3): Armijo $\alpha\le2(1-c_1)\alpha^*$; Wolfe $\alpha\ge(1-c_2)\alpha^*$; strong Wolfe $\alpha\le(1+c_2)\alpha^*$; Goldstein $[2c,\,2(1-c)]\alpha^*$.
  • Golden section: interior points at fraction $\rho=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\approx0.382$ from each end; the bracket shrinks by $\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}\approx0.618$ per new evaluation (one old point reused).
  • 1-D Newton: $x_{k+1}=x_k-f'(x_k)/f''(x_k)$. Needs $f''\gt0$, and can still fail far away ($\sqrt{1+x^2}$: $x_{k+1}=-x_k^3$).
  • Zoutendijk: Wolfe steps (strong Wolfe also fine) + $f$ bounded below + $\nabla f$ Lipschitz ⇒ $\sum_k\cos^2\theta_k\|g_k\|^2\lt\infty$, $\cos\theta_k=\frac{-g_k^Td_k}{\|g_k\|\|d_k\|}$. If $\cos\theta_k\ge\delta\gt0$ then $\|g_k\|\to0$. $d_k=-D_kg_k$ with eig$(D_k)\in[m,M]$ gives $\cos\theta_k\ge m/M$.
Memorise cold: gradient descent and Newton (Ch 3)
  • Fixed step: $e_{k+1}=(I-\alpha Q)e_k$; the eigen-component along $q_i$ is multiplied by $(1-\alpha\lambda_i)$ each step.
  • Converges from every $x_0$ ⇔ $|1-\alpha\lambda_i|\lt1\ \forall i$ ⇔ $\boxed{0\lt\alpha\lt 2/\lambda_{\max}}$. Best $\alpha=\dfrac{2}{\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max}}$, worst-case factor $\dfrac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$ on $\|e_k\|$.
  • Multiplier $=-1$ ⇒ that component flips sign forever (2-cycle, constant $f$). $|{\cdot}|\gt1$ ⇒ diverges, unless $e_0$ has zero component in that eigen-direction.
  • A gradient step with $\eta=1/\lambda$ kills the $\lambda$-component exactly ($1-\lambda/\lambda=0$).
  • Exact line search SD: $g_{k+1}^Tg_k=0$ (zigzag); $f(x_{k+1})-f^*\le\left(\dfrac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^2(f(x_k)-f^*)$ (Kantorovich). If $g_0$ lives only in some eigen-directions, use the effective $\kappa$ over those only. One-step finish ⇔ $g_0$ is an eigenvector.
  • Newton: $H_kp_k=-g_k$, $x_{k+1}=x_k+p_k$. One step on a quadratic with $Q\succ0$ lands on $x^*$. Quadratic convergence $\|e_{k+1}\|\le C\|e_k\|^2$ only for $x_0$ sufficiently close. Descent iff $H_k\succ0$ ($g_k^Tp_k=-p_k^TH_kp_k$). Damped Newton + Armijo ($c_1\lt\frac12$, start at $\alpha=1$) eventually accepts $\alpha=1$.
  • $\|x\|^p/p$, $1\lt p\lt2$: $\nabla=\|x\|^{p-2}x$, not Lipschitz at 0; $r_{k+1}=r_k|1-\alpha r_k^{p-2}|$; $f$ rises for $r_k\lt(\alpha/2)^{1/(2-p)}$; converges only by an exact hit ($r=\alpha^{1/(2-p)}$); exact line search $\alpha_k=r_k^{2-p}$.
Memorise cold: conjugate directions / CG (Ch 4)
  • Affine-sheet minimum ($f$ convex, directions = columns of $D$): $x_S$ minimises $f$ on $\bar x+\mathrm{range}(D)$ ⇔ $D^T\nabla f(x_S)=0$. The gradient of $h(z)=f(\bar x+Dz)$ is zero; the gradient of $f$ is only orthogonal to every $d_i$, not zero.
  • Q-conjugate: $d_i^TQd_j=0$ ($i\ne j$) = perpendicular after rounding the bowl ($y=Q^{1/2}x$). Nonzero Q-conjugate vectors are linearly independent. Eigenvectors of $Q$ are both orthogonal and Q-conjugate.
  • Key formula: $g_i=g_0+\sum_{j\lt i}\alpha_jQd_j$. With conjugate directions: $d_i^Tg_i=d_i^Tg_0$, $\alpha_i=\beta_i^*=-c_i/h_i$, and $g_k\perp d_0,\dots,d_{k-1}$ (expanding subspace).
  • CG: $d_0=-g_0$; $\ \alpha_k=\dfrac{\|g_k\|^2}{d_k^TQd_k}$; $\ x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kd_k$; $\ g_{k+1}=g_k+\alpha_kQd_k$; $\ \beta_k=\dfrac{\|g_{k+1}\|^2}{\|g_k\|^2}$; $\ d_{k+1}=-g_{k+1}+\beta_kd_k$.
  • $g_k^Td_k=-\|g_k\|^2$. Gradients orthogonal ($g_i^Tg_j=0$), directions Q-conjugate ($d_i^TQd_j=0$). Never swap.
  • $x_k$ minimises $f$ over $x_0+\mathrm{span}\{g_0,Qg_0,\dots,Q^{k-1}g_0\}$ (highest power $k-1$).
  • CG steps to reach $x^*$ = number of distinct eigenvalues whose eigenspaces $e_0$ actually touches ($\le n$). Partial bound: $m$ outliers above $[a,b]$ ⇒ after $m+1$ steps $E$ shrinks by $\big(\frac{b-a}{b+a}\big)^2$.
  • Decrease per step: $f(x_k)-f(x_{k+1})=\dfrac{\|g_k\|^4}{2\,d_k^TQd_k}$ (for $E=\|x-x^*\|_Q^2$ the decrease is twice that). $\|g_k\|$ may increase.
  • PCG ($B\approx Q^{-1}$, SPD): $d_0=-Bg_0$, $\alpha_k=\dfrac{g_k^TBg_k}{d_k^TQd_k}$, $\beta_k=\dfrac{g_{k+1}^TBg_{k+1}}{g_k^TBg_k}$, $d_{k+1}=-Bg_{k+1}+\beta_kd_k$. Then $g_i^TBg_j=0$, $d_i^TQd_j=0$, $g_k^Td_k=-g_k^TBg_k$. $B\to cB$ changes nothing in $x_k$. Speed depends on eig$(B^{1/2}QB^{1/2})$. $f(x_k)$ still decreases; $\|g_k\|$ need not.
  • Non-quadratic CG, four changes: (1) $\beta$ back to the original formula with $Q\to F(x_k)$; (2) $\beta_k=0$ if $d_k^TF(x_k)d_k\le0$; (3) $\alpha_k$ by a line search; (4) if $g_k^Td_k\ge0$, reset $d_k=-g_k$. FR + strong Wolfe ($c_2\lt\frac12$): $-\frac{1}{1-c_2}\le\frac{g_k^Td_k}{\|g_k\|^2}\le\frac{2c_2-1}{1-c_2}\lt0$.
