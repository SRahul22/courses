General notation. Vectors are columns; $x^T$ is the transpose. $x\ge0$ means every entry is $\ge0$. $x_j$ = entry $j$ of $x$. "Nonsingular" = square and invertible. Vectors are linearly independent if no one of them is a combination of the others. The null space $\ker A=\mathrm{null}(A)=\{d: Ad=0\}$ (all directions that do not change $Ax$). A symmetric matrix $M$ is positive definite (PD, $M\succ0$) if $v^TMv\gt0$ for every $v\ne0$, positive semidefinite ($M\succeq0$) if $v^TMv\ge0$. $B^{-T}$ means $(B^{-1})^T=(B^T)^{-1}$. A bar, as in $\bar x$, means "the value at the current point".

Part 1 notation (Chapter 8, LP). $n$ = number of variables, $m$ = number of equality constraints. $A$ = the $m\times n$ constraint matrix, assumed to have rank $m$ (no redundant rows); $b\in\mathbb R^m$; $c\in\mathbb R^n$ = cost vector. $a_j$ = column $j$ of $A$. $K=\{x: Ax=b,\ x\ge0\}$ = feasible set. $B$ = basis matrix ($m$ chosen columns), $N$ = the other columns; $x_B,x_N,c_B,c_N$ = the matching parts of $x$ and $c$. $\lambda\in\mathbb R^m$ = multipliers of $Ax=b$ ("prices"); $\mu\in\mathbb R^n$ = multipliers of $x\ge0$; $\mu_N$ = reduced costs. $q$ = index of the entering variable; $u=B^{-1}a_q$; $d$ = direction of motion; $\theta$ = step length; $r$ = the row (variable) that leaves; rows are counted in the fixed order of the basic variables (an ordered basis). $s_i$ = slack or surplus variable. $z_0$ = current cost (tableau).

Part 2 notation (Chapter 9, QP). $f(x)=\tfrac12x^TQx-c^Tx$ with $Q$ symmetric $n\times n$, so $\nabla f(x)=Qx-c$. Equality constraints $Ax=b$ ($m$ rows, rank $m$), multipliers $\lambda$, Lagrangian $\ell(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda^T(Ax-b)$. $K$ = the KKT matrix $\begin{bmatrix}Q&A^T\\A&0\end{bmatrix}$. $Z$ = a matrix whose columns are a basis of $\ker A$; $x_p$ = any point with $Ax_p=b$; $z$ = coordinates along $Z$; $H_r=Z^TQZ$, $g_r=Z^T(Qx_p-c)$; $S=AQ^{-1}A^T$; $P$ = projector onto $\ker A$. Inequality QP: constraints $a_i^Tx\le b_i$, where now $a_i$ is the normal vector (row) of constraint $i$; $\mathcal A(x)$ = active set; $W_k$ = working set; $g_k=Qx_k-c$; $p_k$ = search direction; $\alpha_k$ = ratio-test bound; $i_k$ = the blocking constraint (the notes write $l_k$). In worked runs the iterates are written $x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\dots$ and $p^{(0)},p^{(1)},\dots$, so they can't be confused with the coordinates $x_1,x_2$.

0. Why this topic exists

A linear program (LP) asks: choose numbers $x_1,\dots,x_n$ to make a linear cost (like $-2x_1-3x_2$) as small as possible, subject to linear constraints (like $x_1+x_2\le4$). Shipping plans, production plans and diets are LPs.

The difficulty: the feasible set has infinitely many points. The big discovery of Chapter 8 is that you only need to look at the corners: if an LP has a best point, some corner is a best point, and there are only finitely many corners. The simplex method walks from corner to neighbouring corner, always downhill, until no neighbour is cheaper.

A quadratic program (QP) (Chapter 9) has a quadratic cost $\frac12x^TQx-c^Tx$ with linear constraints. Now the cost is curved like a bowl, so the best point can sit in the middle of an edge or face, not at a corner. The active-set method uses the same "walk and stop at the first blocking constraint" idea, but it may stop before reaching a constraint, because the bottom of the bowl came first.

The whole module in one sentence

Guess which constraints are tight, solve an easy equality problem with only those, then (1) if moving helps, move until the first new constraint blocks you (a ratio test), and (2) if moving doesn't help, check the signs of the multipliers and release a constraint with the wrong sign.

A. What an LP looks like

A1. The standard form

Chapter 8 writes every LP in one shape, the standard form:

$$\min_x\ c^Tx\quad\text{subject to}\quad Ax=b,\quad x\ge0.$$

Read it piece by piece: the cost $c^Tx=c_1x_1+\dots+c_nx_n$ is linear; there are $m$ equations $Ax=b$; and the only inequalities are the simple signs $x_j\ge0$. We assume rank $A=m$: no equation is a combination of the others (a redundant one could just be deleted).

Why this shape? Equations are easy (linear algebra solves them), and inequalities of the form "$x_j\ge0$" are the easiest kind to keep track of: a variable is either 0 (the inequality is tight) or positive. Everything in Part 1 is built on this split.

A2. Converting any LP to standard form

Four tricks, each with a small example first.

  • "$\le$" constraint → add a slack. $x_1+x_2\le4$ says "there is some leftover $s_1=4-x_1-x_2\ge0$". So write $x_1+x_2+s_1=4$, $s_1\ge0$. The slack $s_1$ measures how far you are from the boundary. Its cost is 0, and its column in $A$ is $+e_i$ (a 1 in row $i$).
  • "$\ge$" constraint → subtract a surplus. $x_1+x_2\ge1$ says "there is some excess $s_2=x_1+x_2-1\ge0$". So write $x_1+x_2-s_2=1$, $s_2\ge0$. Cost 0, column $-e_i$.
  • Free variable (any sign) → split it. Any number is a difference of two non-negative numbers: $-3=0-3=2-5$. So put $x_j=x_j^+-x_j^-$ with $x_j^\pm\ge0$ and substitute it everywhere (cost and constraints). The pair is not unique, and that's fine. The column of $x_j^-$ is minus the column of $x_j^+$, and so is its cost.
  • Other fixes. $\max c^Tx$ → $\min(-c)^Tx$. A bound $x_j\ge h_j$ → put $x_j=h_j+v_j$, $v_j\ge0$. A negative $b_i$ → multiply that row by $-1$ (a $\le$ becomes $\ge$). The notes then assume $b\ge0$.
Worked example $\max\ 3x_1+x_2$ s.t. $x_1-x_2\le2,\ x_1+x_2\ge1,\ x_1\ge0,\ x_2$ free.
  1. Max → min: $\min\ -3x_1-x_2$.
  2. Split $x_2=x_2^+-x_2^-$: objective $-3x_1-x_2^++x_2^-$.
  3. Row 1 ($\le$): add slack $s_1$: $x_1-x_2^++x_2^-+s_1=2$. Row 2 ($\ge$): subtract surplus $s_2$: $x_1+x_2^+-x_2^--s_2=1$.
  4. Variables $(x_1,x_2^+,x_2^-,s_1,s_2)\ge0$, $n=5,\ m=2$: $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&1&1&0\\1&1&-1&0&-1\end{bmatrix},\ b=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix},\ c=(-3,-1,1,0,0)^T.$$
Trap The column of $x_2^-$ is the negative of the column of $x_2^+$, and so is its cost. Slack/surplus costs are $0$. A surplus has coefficient $-1$, not $+1$ (with $+1$ you would have turned "$\ge$" into "$\le$").

B. Corners: the picture and the algebra

B1. The picture: the best point is at a corner

Draw the running example in the $(x_1,x_2)$ plane. The feasible set is a polygon with four corners. The cost $-2x_1-3x_2$ is constant along parallel lines $2x_1+3x_2=k$. Lowering the cost means sliding that line in the direction $(2,3)$. The last point of the polygon the line touches is the best point, and it is a corner: $(2,2)$, cost $-10$.

Blue region: feasible set. Dots: the four corners with their costs. Red circles: places where two boundary lines cross outside the region (they will be "basic but infeasible" in B2). Green: the simplex path of section E (Bland's rule): $(0,0)\to(4,0)\to(2,2)$. Purple dashed: the other possible path $(0,0)\to(0,3)\to(2,2)$. Dotted grey: the cost line through the best corner. Purple arrow: direction in which the cost decreases.

Why must a corner win? Along any straight segment the cost is linear, so it changes at a constant rate. Then one end of the segment is at least as good as every point in the middle. If the best point were in the middle of an edge, an end of that edge (a corner) would be at least as good. And if the cost is constant along the edge, the whole edge is optimal, corners included. This is the plain-words version of the fundamental theorem (B4).

B2. Corner = basic feasible solution (BFS)

Recipe for a basic solution (Def 8.2.1.1):

  1. Choose $m$ columns of $A$ that form a nonsingular $m\times m$ matrix $B$. Their variables are the basic variables $x_B$; all others are nonbasic, $x_N$, with the rest of the columns forming $N$. So $A=[B\ N]$ after reordering.
  2. Switch the nonbasic variables off: $x_N=0$.
  3. Then $Ax=Bx_B+Nx_N=b$ becomes $Bx_B=b$, so $x_B=B^{-1}b$ (one exact answer, because $B$ is invertible).

Why "basic"? $m$ independent columns in $\mathbb R^m$ form a basis of $\mathbb R^m$, so $b$ can be written in exactly one way as a combination of them. The numbers $x_B$ are exactly those coefficients.

A basic solution need not be feasible: $x_B=B^{-1}b$ might have a negative entry. If $x_B\ge0$, it is a basic feasible solution (BFS).

Four BFSs = the four corners. The two infeasible basic solutions are the red crossing points outside the polygon.

Since there are at most $\binom{n}{m}$ ways to choose $m$ columns, there are finitely many BFSs, hence finitely many corners. That's why a corner-to-corner walk can finish.

Support test (very useful in MCQs): a feasible $\bar x$ is a BFS iff the columns $a_j$ with $\bar x_j\gt0$ are linearly independent. Why: if $\bar x$ is basic, its positive entries are among the basic ones, whose columns are independent. Conversely, if the positive columns are independent, add more columns (rank $A=m$ makes this possible) to get $m$ independent ones; their extra variables are basic with value 0.

Check B2. (a) In the running example, is $x=(1,1,2,3)$ feasible? Is it a BFS? (b) A feasible point has 3 positive entries and $m=2$. Can it be a BFS?
Show answer

(a) $1+1+2=4$ ✓, $1+2+3=6$ ✓, all $\ge0$: feasible. All 4 entries are positive, and 4 columns in $\mathbb R^2$ are always dependent, so it is not a BFS (it's the interior point $(1,1)$ of the polygon). (b) No: 3 vectors in $\mathbb R^2$ are dependent. A BFS has at most $m$ positive entries.

B3. Degenerate corners

A BFS is degenerate if some basic variable happens to be 0, i.e. it has fewer than $m$ positive entries. $B$ is still nonsingular; the value 0 is just a coincidence of the numbers. Geometrically, more boundaries pass through that corner than are needed to pin it down, so several bases describe the same corner.

Worked example (slides p. 79) $\min\ -x_3$ s.t. $x_1+x_3=1,\ x_2+x_3=1,\ x\ge0$. So $a_1=(1,0)^T,\ a_2=(0,1)^T,\ a_3=(1,1)^T$, $m=2$.
  1. Basis $\{x_1,x_2\}$: $B=I$, $x_B=(1,1)$, point $(1,1,0)$: nondegenerate BFS (2 positive entries).
  2. Basis $\{x_3,x_2\}$: $B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$, $x_B=B^{-1}b=(1,0)$, point $(0,0,1)$: a degenerate BFS (basic $x_2=0$).
  3. Basis $\{x_1,x_3\}$ gives $x_B=(0,1)$: the same point $(0,0,1)$. One corner, two bases.

Why care? In D4 we'll see that at a degenerate corner the simplex method can change basis without moving, which is where "cycling" and Bland's rule come from.

Check B3. True or false: "a degenerate BFS has a singular basis matrix".
Show answer

False. $B$ is nonsingular by definition of a basis; degenerate only means some entry of $B^{-1}b$ is 0 (Quiz 5 Q13).

B4. The two theorems (statements)

Extreme point (precise word for "corner"). $x\in K$ is an extreme point if it is not a strict average of two different points of $K$: there are no $y\ne z$ in $K$ and $0\lt\alpha\lt1$ with $x=\alpha y+(1-\alpha)z$. A point in the middle of an edge is such an average (of the edge's two ends), so it is not extreme.

The two theorems (statements)
  • Theorem 8.2.2: $x\in K$ is an extreme point $\iff$ $x$ is a BFS.
  • Theorem 8.2.1 (fundamental theorem of LP): (i) if $K$ is nonempty, a BFS exists; (ii) if an optimal solution exists, an optimal BFS exists.

Not claimed: "every optimum is a BFS" (two optimal corners → their midpoint is optimal but not a corner), nor "$K$ is bounded".

The proofs (he wants them) use the ratio test of section D2 and the first-order condition, so they come right after the ratio test, in D2b. Why are they in a Chapter 6 problem set? The professor set the Ch 8 proofs as exercises P11–P12 of Chapter 6 Problem Set 1, because they use Chapter 6's first-order condition for constrained minima.

C. Is this corner optimal?

C1. The thought experiment: raise one switched-off variable

Stand at the corner $(4,0)$ of the running example: basic $x_1=4,\ x_4=2$; nonbasic $x_2=x_3=0$. The simplest moves away from this corner are to raise one nonbasic variable from 0; the equations then decide how the basic variables must change. (Every feasible move is a combination of these one-variable moves; the gap identity in C3 makes this precise.) Try both.

With numbers first

Raise $x_2$ to $t$ (keep $x_3=0$). Row 1: $x_1+t=4$, so $x_1=4-t$. Row 2: $x_1+2t+x_4=6$, so $x_4=6-2t-(4-t)=2-t$. Cost $=-2(4-t)-3t=-8-t$. Cost falls by 1 per unit of $x_2$. Worth doing.

Raise $x_3$ to $t$ (keep $x_2=0$). Row 1: $x_1=4-t$. Row 2: $x_4=6-x_1=2+t$. Cost $=-2(4-t)=-8+2t$. Cost rises by 2 per unit. Not worth it.

These two rates, $-1$ for $x_2$ and $+2$ for $x_3$, are the reduced costs. One is negative, so $(4,0)$ is not optimal.

C2. The general formula: reduced costs and the prices λ

Now do the same with letters. Raise nonbasic $x_q$ to $t$, keep the other nonbasics at 0. The equations become $Bx_B+t\,a_q=b$, so

$$x_B=B^{-1}b-t\,B^{-1}a_q=\bar x_B-t\,u,\qquad u:=B^{-1}a_q\ \ (\text{so }Bu=a_q).$$

($u$ = how much each basic variable must drop per unit of $x_q$; at $(4,0)$ with $q=2$ it was $u=(1,1)$.) The basic variables are always listed in one fixed order, an ordered basis such as $(x_1,x_4)$: $u_i$, $(\bar x_B)_i$ and “row $i$” all mean the $i$-th basic variable in that order. The cost becomes

$$c_B^Tx_B+c_q t=c_B^T\bar x_B+t\,\big(c_q-c_B^TB^{-1}a_q\big).$$

Give the vector $c_B^TB^{-1}$ a name: define $\lambda$ by $B^T\lambda=c_B$, i.e. $\lambda=B^{-T}c_B$. Then $c_B^TB^{-1}a_q=\lambda^Ta_q$ and the rate of change of the cost is

$$\boxed{\bar\mu_q=c_q-a_q^T\bar\lambda},\qquad\text{all at once:}\qquad \boxed{\bar\lambda=B^{-T}c_B,\qquad \bar\mu_N=c_N-N^T\bar\lambda.}$$

Meaning of $\lambda$: prices of the constraints. $\lambda_i$ is a price per unit of row $i$. The current cost equals $c_B^T\bar x_B=\lambda^Tb$ (the value of the right-hand side at these prices). The reduced cost $\bar\mu_q=c_q-a_q^T\bar\lambda$ = "own cost of $x_q$" minus "value, at these prices, of what its column $a_q$ uses up". Negative means $x_q$ is a bargain.

For basic variables the same formula gives $\bar\mu_B=c_B-B^T\bar\lambda=0$: that's how $\lambda$ was chosen.

The optimality test. At a BFS ($\bar x_B\ge0$): if all $\bar\mu_N\ge0$, no single variable can be raised profitably, and in fact the corner is optimal (proof in C3). If some $\bar\mu_q\lt0$ and the corner is nondegenerate, raising $x_q$ a little is feasible and lowers the cost, so the corner is not optimal. (At a degenerate corner a negative $\bar\mu_q$ does not settle it: D4.)

C3. Why the test is right: the gap identity, and the link to LP KKT

Collect what we have: for every index, $c=A^T\bar\lambda+\bar\mu$ (basic part: $\bar\mu_B=0$ and $B^T\bar\lambda=c_B$; nonbasic part: definition of $\bar\mu_N$). Take any feasible $x'$ and compare its cost to the corner's:

$$c^Tx'-c^T\bar x=(A^T\bar\lambda+\bar\mu)^T(x'-\bar x)=\bar\lambda^T\underbrace{A(x'-\bar x)}_{b-b=0}+\bar\mu^Tx'-\underbrace{\bar\mu^T\bar x}_{0}=\boxed{\bar\mu_N^Tx'_N}.$$

($\bar\mu^T\bar x=0$ because $\bar\mu_B=0$ and $\bar x_N=0$; and $\bar\mu^Tx'=\bar\mu_N^Tx'_N$ because $\bar\mu_B=0$.) This gap identity is exact, not an approximation. If $\bar\mu_N\ge0$, then since $x'_N\ge0$ the right side is $\ge0$: no feasible point is cheaper. That proves the optimality test.

Numbers

Corner $\bar x=(4,0,0,2)$, $\bar\mu_N=(\bar\mu_2,\bar\mu_3)=(-1,2)$. Take $x'=(0,3,1,0)$ (the corner $(0,3)$), $x'_N=(x'_2,x'_3)=(3,1)$. Gap $=-9-(-8)=-1$, and $\bar\mu_N^Tx'_N=-3+2=-1$ ✓.

The same thing as KKT (he said this is "worth memorizing"). Chapter 8 writes the Lagrangian of the standard-form LP as $c^Tx+\lambda^T(b-Ax)-\mu^Tx$. Its KKT conditions:

$$\underbrace{A^T\lambda+\mu=c}_{\text{stationarity}},\qquad Ax=b,\quad x\ge0,\qquad \mu\ge0,\qquad \mu^Tx=0\ (\text{complementarity}),\qquad \lambda\ \text{free}.$$

A basis builds a candidate that satisfies most of them automatically: it sets $x_N=0$ and $\mu_B=0$ (so $\mu^Tx=0$ holds), and solves $Bx_B=b$, $B^T\lambda=c_B$, $\mu_N=c_N-N^T\lambda$ (so stationarity and $Ax=b$ hold). Only two sign checks remain: $\bar x_B\ge0$ (primal feasibility) and $\bar\mu_N\ge0$ (dual feasibility). So the reduced costs are the KKT multipliers of $x\ge0$. When both hold, $c^T\bar x=b^T\bar\lambda$ (zero duality gap). At a degenerate basis, $\mu_B=0$ is a choice made by the construction (complementarity alone would not force it).

Sign conventions differ between the chapters Ch 8 uses $\lambda^T(b-Ax)$ so stationarity is $c=A^T\lambda+\mu$. Ch 9 uses $\lambda^T(Ax-b)$ so stationarity is $Qx-c+A^T\lambda=0$. Use the one the question states; if none is stated, say which you use.
Check C3. Why does the gap identity use $x'_N$ and not $\bar x_N$?
Show answer

$\bar x_N=0$, so $\bar\mu_N^T\bar x_N=0$ for every point: that would claim all feasible points cost the same (Quiz 5 Q7, option b). The gap is measured by how much of the other point's weight sits on the currently switched-off variables.

D. Moving to a better corner

Suppose $\bar\mu_q\lt0$. Then $x_q$ should enter (become basic). Three questions: in which direction do we move, how far, and which variable gets switched off?

D1. The direction

From C2, raising $x_q$ by $t$ moves the point along $x(t)=\bar x+t\,d$ with

$$d_q=1,\qquad d_j=0\ \text{for the other nonbasic }j,\qquad d_B=-u=-B^{-1}a_q.$$

Why $Ad=0$: $Ad=Bd_B+a_q d_q=-Bu+a_q=-a_q+a_q=0$. So $A x(t)=b$ for every $t$: the equations stay satisfied, and only the signs $x\ge0$ can stop us. Cost slope: $c^Td=c_q-c_B^Tu=c_q-a_q^T\bar\lambda=\bar\mu_q$, so $c^Tx(t)=c^T\bar x+t\,\bar\mu_q$.

Geometrically this is a walk along an edge of the polygon. At $(4,0)$ with $q=2$: $u=(1,1)$, so in the order $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$, $d=(-1,1,0,-1)$. In the $(x_1,x_2)$ picture that's the direction $(-1,1)$: up and left along the edge $x_1+x_2=4$ towards $(2,2)$.

Trap $d_B=-u$, not $+u$ (with $+u$, $Ad=2a_q\ne0$). $d_q=+1$ (with $-1$ you'd push $x_q$ below 0).

D2. How far: the ratio test

Along the edge the basic variables are $x_B(t)=\bar x_B-t\,u$. Look at one row $i$:

  • if $u_i\gt0$, that basic variable decreases and hits 0 at $t=(\bar x_B)_i/u_i$;
  • if $u_i\le0$, it stays the same or grows: it never blocks.

We must stop at the first one to hit zero:

$$\boxed{\theta=\min_{i:\,u_i\gt0}\ \frac{(\bar x_B)_i}{u_i}}\qquad(\text{min, not max; only rows with }u_i\gt0).$$

The row $r$ that attains the minimum leaves (its variable becomes 0 and nonbasic), $x_q$ enters at value $\theta$, and the cost changes by $\theta\,\bar\mu_q$.

D2b. Proofs of the two B4 theorems

Now that the ratio test is familiar, here are the proofs of the B4 theorems (Ch 6 Problem Set 1, P11–P12). One tool from Chapter 6 is needed: FONC (first-order necessary condition): at a minimiser $x^\star$, every feasible direction $d$ satisfies $\nabla f(x^\star)^Td\ge0$ (no feasible direction goes downhill). For an LP, $\nabla f=c$.

The one idea behind every proof. If the positive entries of $x$ have dependent columns, you can wiggle $x$ in both directions without leaving $K$.

  1. Dependent support ⇒ a null direction. If the columns $\{a_j:x_j\gt0\}$ are dependent, some combination of them is zero: there is $d\ne0$ with $Ad=0$ and $d_j=0$ wherever $x_j=0$.
  2. Both $\pm d$ keep you feasible. $A(x\pm\varepsilon d)=b+0$. The entries with $x_j=0$ have $d_j=0$, so they stay 0; the positive entries stay positive if $\varepsilon\gt0$ is small enough.
  3. Extreme ⇒ BFS (contrapositive): if $x$ is not a BFS, its support is dependent, and $x=\tfrac12(x+\varepsilon d)+\tfrac12(x-\varepsilon d)$ is the average of two different feasible points, so $x$ is not extreme.
  4. BFS ⇒ extreme: suppose $x=\alpha y+(1-\alpha)z$ with $0\lt\alpha\lt1$ and $y,z\in K$. For a nonbasic $j$: $0=x_j=\alpha y_j+(1-\alpha)z_j$ with $y_j,z_j\ge0$, which forces $y_j=z_j=0$. So $y_N=z_N=0$, hence $By_B=b=Bz_B=Bx_B$, and since $B$ is nonsingular, $y_B=z_B=x_B$: $y=z=x$. No genuine average exists.
  5. A BFS exists (Ch 6 PS1 P11): start from any feasible $x$. If its support is dependent, choose the sign of $d$ so that some $d_j\gt0$ and move to $x-\varepsilon^*d$ with $\varepsilon^*=\min_{d_j\gt0}x_j/d_j$ (the same kind of ratio test as D2). One positive entry hits 0, none goes negative, no zero entry becomes positive. The support shrank; repeat. It must stop (the support can't shrink forever), and it stops at an independent support = a BFS.
  6. Optimal version (Ch 6 PS1 P12): do the same at an optimal $x^\star$. Both $d$ and $-d$ are feasible directions, so FONC gives $c^Td\ge0$ and $-c^Td\ge0$, so $c^Td=0$. Because the cost is linear, $c^T(x^\star-td)=c^Tx^\star$ exactly for every $t$. So each reduction step keeps the point optimal, and it ends at an optimal BFS.
The proof on numbers (slide example of B3)
  1. $x=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ is feasible ($\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$ twice) with 3 positive entries but only $m=2$ rows, so its support is dependent: $a_1+a_2-a_3=0$, i.e. $d=(1,1,-1)$, $Ad=0$. Not a BFS, not a corner.
  2. Here $c^Td=+1\ne0$, so this $x$ is not optimal: moving along $-d$ lowers the cost.
  3. Reduction: $d_j\gt0$ for $j=1,2$, so $\varepsilon^*=\min(\tfrac{1/2}{1},\tfrac{1/2}{1})=\tfrac12$, and $x-\tfrac12d=(0,0,1)$: the optimal BFS, cost $-1$.

D3. When nothing blocks: unboundedness

If $u\le0$ (no row has $u_i\gt0$), the whole ray $\bar x+td$, $t\ge0$, is feasible. If also $\bar\mu_q\lt0$, the cost $c^T\bar x+t\bar\mu_q\to-\infty$: the LP is unbounded below, no minimum exists. Both conditions are needed: with $\bar\mu_q\ge0$ the ray goes uphill or flat and proves nothing.

Tiny example

$\min\ -x_1$ s.t. $x_1-x_2=1$, $x\ge0$ ($m=1$). Basis $\{x_1\}$: $\bar x_1=1$, $\bar\lambda=c_1/1=-1$, $\bar\mu_2=0-(-1)(-1)=-1\lt0$, $u=B^{-1}a_2=-1\le0$. Ray: $x_1=1+t$, $x_2=t$, cost $-1-t\to-\infty$.

D4. Degeneracy, zero steps, cycling and Bland's rule

Zero steps. If some basic variable is already 0 at a degenerate corner and its $u_i\gt0$, then its ratio is $0/u_i=0$, so $\theta=0$. The basis changes ($x_q$ enters at value 0, that variable leaves) but the point and the cost do not move.

Slide p. 81: a negative reduced cost at an optimal point

$\min\ x_1$ s.t. $x_1+x_2=0$, $x\ge0$. The only feasible point is $(0,0)$, so it is optimal. Basis $\{x_1\}$: $\bar x_1=0$ (degenerate), $\bar\lambda=1$, $\bar\mu_2=0-1\cdot1=-1\lt0$, $u=1\gt0$, so $\theta=0/1=0$. A negative reduced cost at a degenerate BFS does not prove non-optimality. (And a zero step does not prove optimality either.)

Ties create degeneracy. If two rows tie in the ratio test, both hit 0 at the same $t$; only one leaves, so the other stays basic with value 0. Example (B3's LP): from basis $\{x_1,x_2\}$, $\bar\mu_3=c_3-a_3^T\bar\lambda=-1$ with $\bar\lambda=0$, $u=(1,1)$, ratios $1,1$: a tie. If $x_1$ leaves, $x_2$ stays basic at 0: the new BFS $(0,0,1)$ is degenerate.

Cycling. A zero step keeps the cost equal, so in principle a sequence of zero steps can return to a basis already visited and repeat forever. Without degeneracy this can't happen: every step strictly lowers the cost, so no basis can repeat, and there are finitely many bases, so the method stops.

Bland's rule prevents cycling: entering = the smallest index $j$ with $\bar\mu_j\lt0$; leaving = among rows tied in the ratio test, the variable with the smallest original index (not the first row of the table). With it, no basis repeats, so the method terminates.

D5. The new basis really is a basis

The new basis $B'$ is $B$ with column $r$ replaced by $a_q$. Since $a_q=Bu$, we can write $B'=B\,E$, where $E$ is the identity with column $r$ replaced by $u$ (check: column $r$ of $BE$ is $Bu=a_q$, other columns unchanged). $E$ is the identity except one column, so $\det E=u_r$. Hence

$$\det B'=u_r\,\det B\ne0,\quad\text{because the ratio test only picks rows with }u_r\gt0.$$

This holds even for a zero step. (Running example: $B=[a_1\ a_4]$, $u=(1,1)$, $r=2$: $B'=[a_1\ a_2]$, $\det B'=1\cdot1=1$ ✓.)

F. The tableau: only the first four slides

In plain words: the tableau is the same algebra written as one array, so that after row operations you can read off $\bar x_B$, every $u$, every reduced cost and the current cost.

  1. Split $Ax=b$ as $Bx_B+Nx_N=b$ and solve for the basics: $x_B=\bar x_B-B^{-1}Nx_N$.
  2. Put that into the cost: $c^Tx=c_B^T\bar x_B+(c_N^T-c_B^TB^{-1}N)x_N=z_0+\bar\mu_N^Tx_N$, with $z_0=c_B^TB^{-1}b$ (the current cost) and $\bar\mu_N=c_N-N^TB^{-T}c_B$, the reduced costs again (the slides write $r_N$ for this vector).
  3. Start from the array $\begin{bmatrix}B&N&b\\c_B^T&c_N^T&0\end{bmatrix}$. Row operations (multiply the top block by $B^{-1}$, then subtract $c_B^T\times$ the top rows from the bottom row) give $$\begin{bmatrix}I&B^{-1}N&B^{-1}b\\0&\bar\mu_N^T&-z_0\end{bmatrix}.$$ The bottom-right entry is $0-c_B^TB^{-1}b=-z_0$: minus the current cost.
  4. So: right column = $\bar x_B$; the column of $x_q$ inside $B^{-1}N$ = $u$; bottom row = reduced costs; bottom-right = $-z_0$.

G. One idea, two chapters

Look back at what simplex did, in words that don't mention LP:

  1. Guess which constraints are tight. Simplex guesses "the nonbasic variables sit at their bound $x_N=0$".
  2. Solve an equality problem with only those: $Bx_B=b$, $B^T\lambda=c_B$.
  3. Sign test on the multipliers of the tight constraints: $\bar\mu_N\ge0$? A wrong sign ($\bar\mu_q\lt0$) means "this constraint is holding us back": release it ($x_q$ enters, leaves its bound).
  4. Move along a direction that keeps the other tight constraints tight, until the first new constraint blocks (ratio test); that one becomes tight.

The active-set method for QP (section I) does exactly these four things with general linear inequalities. The one real difference: an LP cost is linear, so moving further always keeps improving and you always walk until something blocks. A QP cost is a curved bowl: the best point along the direction may come before any constraint blocks. That's why the QP step is $\min\{1,\alpha_k\}$ instead of just "the ratio". Before that, we need the equality-constrained QP (section H), because it is the "solve an equality problem" step.

H. Equality-constrained QP

H1. The problem and its KKT system

$$\min_x\ f(x)=\tfrac12x^TQx-c^Tx\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Ax=b,\qquad Q=Q^T,\ \ \mathrm{rank}A=m\lt n.$$

The gradient is $\nabla f(x)=Qx-c$ and the Hessian (curvature) is $Q$. Without constraints and with $Q\succ0$, the minimiser solves $Qx=c$. With constraints, use the Chapter 9 Lagrangian $\ell(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda^T(Ax-b)$; setting its gradient in $x$ to zero and keeping feasibility gives the KKT system:

$$Qx-c+A^T\lambda=0,\quad Ax=b\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}Q&A^T\\A&0\end{bmatrix}}_{K}\begin{bmatrix}x\\\lambda\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c\\b\end{bmatrix}.$$

It's a linear system: solving a QP with equality constraints is just linear algebra. At a given $x$ the multiplier is unique ($A^T$ has independent columns because rank $A=m$). But a solution of this system is only a KKT point; whether it's a minimum depends on curvature (H2).

Running QP example

$\min\ x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2$ s.t. $x_1+x_2+x_3=3$. So $Q=2I$, $c=0$, $A=[1\ 1\ 1]$, $b=3$. KKT: $2x_i+\lambda=0$ for each $i$, so $x_i=-\lambda/2$; then $-3\lambda/2=3$ gives $\lambda=-2$, $x=(1,1,1)$.

H2. Why $Q$ only needs to be PD on null(A)

In plain words: on the plane $Ax=b$ you can only move in directions $d$ with $Ad=0$. Only the curvature of $f$ along those directions matters. If $f$ curves up along every allowed direction, there is exactly one minimiser, even if $f$ curves down in some forbidden direction.

The one-line derivation. Let $x^*$ be a KKT point and $x^*+d$ any other feasible point (so $Ad=0$). A quadratic's expansion is exact:

$$f(x^*+d)=f(x^*)+\nabla f(x^*)^Td+\tfrac12d^TQd.$$

At a KKT point $\nabla f(x^*)=Qx^*-c=-A^T\lambda$, so $\nabla f(x^*)^Td=-\lambda^TAd=0$. Therefore

$$\boxed{f(x^*+d)-f(x^*)=\tfrac12\,d^TQd\quad\text{for every feasible move }d\in\ker A.}$$

So $x^*$ is the unique global minimiser exactly when $d^TQd\gt0$ for every $d\ne0$ in $\ker A$: "$Q$ is PD on the null space of $A$". Nothing is said about directions outside $\ker A$.

Green: the constraint $x_2=0$ ($A=[0\ 1]$, $b=0$). The same test with a matrix. Let the columns of $Z$ ($n\times(n-m)$) be a basis of $\ker A$, so every allowed $d$ is $d=Zv$ for exactly one $v$. Then $d^TQd=v^T(Z^TQZ)v$, so

$$\text{"}Q\text{ PD on }\ker A\text{"}\iff H_r:=Z^TQZ\succ0\qquad(\text{the reduced Hessian}).$$
The facts (Theorem 9.1.1, Proposition 9.1.2)
  • Unique global minimiser $\iff H_r\succ0$ $\iff d^TQd\gt0$ for all $0\ne d\in\ker A$. $Q$ itself need not be PD.
  • $K$ nonsingular $\iff H_r$ nonsingular. So $H_r\succ0\Rightarrow K$ nonsingular, but not conversely: $K$ can be nonsingular with $H_r$ negative; then the unique KKT point is not a minimiser (practice P3(b), N7(f)).
  • Neither test depends on which $Z$ you pick ($\tilde Z=ZR$ gives $\tilde H_r=R^TH_rR$).

H3. The null-space method: deriving $\phi(z)$

Idea: describe all feasible points with free coordinates, substitute, and get an unconstrained problem.

  1. Find one feasible point $x_p$ ($Ax_p=b$).
  2. Every feasible $x$ differs from $x_p$ by something in $\ker A$, so $x=x_p+Zz$ for a unique $z\in\mathbb R^{n-m}$.
  3. Substitute and expand (use $Q=Q^T$, so the two cross terms $\tfrac12x_p^TQZz$ and $\tfrac12z^TZ^TQx_p$ are equal): $$f(x_p+Zz)=\tfrac12x_p^TQx_p+z^TZ^TQx_p+\tfrac12z^TZ^TQZz-c^Tx_p-c^TZz$$ $$=f(x_p)+z^T\underbrace{Z^T(Qx_p-c)}_{g_r}+\tfrac12z^T\underbrace{Z^TQZ}_{H_r}z=:\phi(z).$$
  4. $\phi$ is an ordinary quadratic in $z$ with no constraints. Stationarity: $\nabla\phi=H_rz+g_r=0$. If $H_r\succ0$: $z^*=-H_r^{-1}g_r$, $x^*=x_p+Zz^*$.
  5. Global uniqueness: with $g_r=-H_rz^*$, completing the square gives the exact identity $\phi(z)-\phi(z^*)=\tfrac12(z-z^*)^TH_r(z-z^*)\gt0$ for $z\ne z^*$ (practice P2).
  6. If you also need $\lambda$: solve $A^T\lambda=-(Qx^*-c)$.

He called deriving $\phi(z)$ "not difficult and also important": be able to write steps 2–4 from scratch.

H4. The range-space (Schur complement) method

Idea: if $Q$ is invertible, eliminate $x$ and solve a small system for $\lambda$.

  1. From stationarity $Qx-c+A^T\lambda=0$: $x=Q^{-1}(c-A^T\lambda)$.
  2. Put it into $Ax=b$: $AQ^{-1}c-AQ^{-1}A^T\lambda=b$, i.e. $$S\lambda=AQ^{-1}c-b,\qquad S:=AQ^{-1}A^T\ \ (m\times m).$$
  3. Solve for $\lambda$, then $x=Q^{-1}(c-A^T\lambda)$.

$K$ nonsingular $\iff S$ nonsingular (when $Q$ is). If $Q\succ0$ then $S\succ0$: for $\nu\ne0$, full row rank gives $A^T\nu\ne0$, so $\nu^TS\nu=(A^T\nu)^TQ^{-1}(A^T\nu)\gt0$. But $Q$ nonsingular alone is not enough: $Q=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$, $A=[1\ 1]$ gives $S=1-1=0$.

Running QP example by Schur

$Q^{-1}=\tfrac12I$, $S=A(\tfrac12I)A^T=\tfrac32$, $S\lambda=0-3$ ⇒ $\lambda=-2$, $x=\tfrac12(0-A^T\lambda)=(1,1,1)$ ✓. Three methods, one answer.

When to use which: null space when $n-m$ is small or $Q$ is indefinite (only $H_r$ needs to be PD); range space when $Q$ is easy to invert (e.g. diagonal) and $m$ is small.

Idea: to move without breaking $Ax=b$, strip from any vector $v$ the part that changes $Ax$. Look for $Pv=v-A^Tw$ (remove something in the row space of $A$) with $A(Pv)=0$: $Av-AA^Tw=0$, so $w=(AA^T)^{-1}Av$. Hence

$$P=I-A^T(AA^T)^{-1}A=Z(Z^TZ)^{-1}Z^T.$$

$P$ is symmetric, $P^2=P$, and its range is $\ker A$ (practice P5 of PS1). To apply it without $Z$: solve $(AA^T)w=Av$, then $Pv=v-A^Tw$.

Projected steepest descent. $d_P:=-P\nabla f(x)$ is feasible ($Ad_P=0$), and $\nabla f^Td_P=-\nabla f^TP\nabla f=-\nabla f^TP^TP\nabla f=-\|d_P\|^2$. With $H_r\succ0$ that certifies the unique minimiser.

Projected CG (relation only): with $Z^TZ=I$ it is ordinary conjugate gradients on $H_rz=Z^T(c-Qx_p)$, so it ends in at most $n-m$ steps.

I. Inequality QP: the active-set method

I1. The problem, the active set and the working set

$$\min_x\ f(x)=\tfrac12x^TQx-c^Tx\quad\text{s.t.}\quad a_i^Tx\le b_i,\ \ i=1,\dots,m.$$

(Now $a_i$ is the normal vector of constraint $i$.) The active set $\mathcal A(x)=\{i: a_i^Tx=b_i\}$ lists the constraints that are tight at $x$.

Idea: if we knew which constraints are tight at the answer, we would treat them as equalities and solve an equality QP (section H). We don't know, so we guess: the working set $W_k\subseteq\mathcal A(x_k)$ is the set of constraints we currently hold as equalities, with linearly independent normals. Then we improve the guess: add a constraint that blocks us, drop a constraint whose multiplier has the wrong sign. (A constraint can be active at $x_k$ without being in $W_k$.)

I2. The subproblem and why its direction goes downhill

From the current feasible $x_k$, look for a step $p$. For a quadratic the expansion is exact:

$$f(x_k+p)=f(x_k)+g_k^Tp+\tfrac12p^TQp,\qquad g_k=\nabla f(x_k)=Qx_k-c.$$

Keeping each working constraint tight means $a_i^T(x_k+p)=b_i$, i.e. $a_i^Tp=0$. So the direction is

$$p_k=\arg\min_p\ q_k(p):=g_k^Tp+\tfrac12p^TQp\quad\text{s.t.}\quad a_i^Tp=0,\ i\in W_k.$$

He stressed this form: "writing this in terms of the gradient is important to understand the algorithm". It is an equality QP in $p$ (section H). $x_k+p_k$ is the best point on the working-set equalities.

How to actually compute $p_k$. Stack the normals of the working constraints as the rows of a matrix $A_W$ (one row $a_i^T$ per $i\in W_k$). The subproblem is an equality QP in $p$ with linear term $g_k$ (in place of $-c$) and constraint $A_Wp=0$, so by H1 its KKT system is

$$\begin{bmatrix}Q&A_W^T\\A_W&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}p\\\lambda\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-g_k\\0\end{bmatrix}\qquad(\text{i.e. }Qp+A_W^T\lambda=-g_k,\ \ A_Wp=0).$$

Solve it (by hand for 2–3 unknowns, or by the null-space method of H3: write $p=Zv$ with $A_WZ=0$). Two remarks:

  • When the solution has $p=0$, the first block row reads $A_W^T\lambda=-g_k$, i.e. $g_k+\sum_{i\in W_k}\lambda_ia_i=0$: the same system gives the multipliers you need in I4.
  • When $p\ne0$, the $\lambda$ it produces is just a by-product; ignore it.

A fully worked step with $Q\ne I$ and a slanted working constraint is at the end of I3.

Why $p_k$ goes downhill (when $p_k\ne0$ and $Q$ is PD on the allowed directions). Every $\alpha p_k$ is also allowed, and $p_k$ is the best, so $h(\alpha)=q_k(\alpha p_k)=\alpha g_k^Tp_k+\tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}p_k^TQp_k$ is smallest at $\alpha=1$: $h'(1)=g_k^Tp_k+p_k^TQp_k=0$. Hence

$$\boxed{g_k^Tp_k=-p_k^TQp_k\lt0},\qquad f(x_k+\alpha p_k)-f(x_k)=\alpha g_k^Tp_k+\tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}p_k^TQp_k=\boxed{-\alpha\big(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\big)p_k^TQp_k}.$$

Read the second formula as a parabola in $\alpha$: $f$ decreases for every $0\lt\alpha\lt2$, is lowest at $\alpha=1$, and is back to $f(x_k)$ at $\alpha=2$. So $\alpha=1$ is the best step if nothing blocks.

I3. How far: the ratio test and the step $\min\{1,\alpha_k\}$

Moving to $x_k+\alpha p_k$ keeps the working constraints tight ($a_i^Tp_k=0$). A constraint $i\notin W_k$ stays satisfied iff

$$a_i^Tx_k+\alpha\,a_i^Tp_k\le b_i\iff \alpha\,a_i^Tp_k\le\underbrace{b_i-a_i^Tx_k}_{\text{slack}\ \ge0}.$$

If $a_i^Tp_k\le0$ we move away from (or parallel to) that boundary: no limit. If $a_i^Tp_k\gt0$: $\alpha\le\text{slack}/a_i^Tp_k$. So

$$\boxed{\alpha_k=\min_{i\notin W_k,\ a_i^Tp_k\gt0}\ \frac{b_i-a_i^Tx_k}{a_i^Tp_k}}\qquad(+\infty\text{ if no such }i).$$

(The minimum is over $i\notin W_k$; one line of the Ch 9 notes prints $i\in W_k$ under the min, a typo: working constraints have $a_i^Tp_k=0$ and can't block.)

The step rule ("a very important point you have to remember")

Step $=\min\{1,\alpha_k\}$.

  • $\alpha_k\gt1$ (including $+\infty$): nothing blocks before the best point. Take the full step $x_{k+1}=x_k+p_k$, $W$ unchanged. Not $\alpha_k$: going beyond 1 climbs back up the parabola (and past 2 it is worse than staying).
  • $\alpha_k\le1$: a constraint $i_k$ blocks first. Step $\alpha_k$: $x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_kp_k$, and add $i_k$ to $W$ (just one, even if several tie). $\alpha_k=0$ can happen when an active constraint is not in $W_k$: $x$ stays, $W$ grows.

Compare LP: there the cost is linear (no parabola), so you always go all the way to the blocker, $\theta$.

I4. When $p_k=0$: check the multipliers, maybe drop one

$p_k=0$ means $x_k$ is already the best point on the working-set equalities. Find the multipliers from

$$g_k+\sum_{i\in W_k}\lambda_i a_i=0.$$

(Ch 9 sign: $\ell=f+\sum\lambda_i(a_i^Tx-b_i)$; for "$\le$" constraints KKT needs $\lambda_i\ge0$.)

  • All $\lambda_i\ge0$: put $\lambda_i=0$ for $i\notin W_k$; then all KKT conditions hold. With $Q\succeq0$ the problem is convex, so $x_k$ is a global minimiser (Theorem 9.2.4). Stop.
  • Some $\lambda_j\lt0$: constraint $j$ is "holding us back the wrong way". Drop $j$ from $W$ (usually the most negative), keep $x_k$, and solve the subproblem again.

Why a negative multiplier means "release it". At $x_k$, $g_k=-\sum_{i\in W_k}\lambda_ia_i$. Take a direction $p$ that keeps the other working constraints tight ($a_i^Tp=0$, $i\ne j$) and moves strictly inside constraint $j$ ($a_j^Tp=-1$; possible because the normals are independent). Then $g_k^Tp=-\lambda_ja_j^Tp=\lambda_j\lt0$: downhill and feasible. So $x_k$ is not optimal, and the new subproblem's $p$ indeed has $a_j^Tp\lt0$ and $g_k^Tp\lt0$ (Ch 6 PS1 Problem 10). This is the QP twin of a negative reduced cost in simplex.

S4

Why is the new basis nonsingular after a pivot? What does Bland's rule say, and why is it needed?

Show answer

$B'=B[e_1\cdots u\cdots e_m]$, $\det B'=u_r\det B\ne0$ since $u_r\gt0$ ($r$ = the leaving row). Bland: enter the smallest index with negative reduced cost; among ratio ties, the smallest original index leaves. Needed because degenerate ($\theta=0$) pivots keep the cost equal, so bases could cycle.
