M6 · Unconstrained optimality and convexity

Chapter 1 · about 30–40 min (cheap MCQ marks: be quick, but be exact) · Goal: state FONC/SONC/SOSC exactly and know what they do not say, classify stationary points from the Hessian, handle quadratics and least squares, and test or prove convexity.

Time plan: Must remember 5 min · Concepts 12 min · Practice 15–20 min · Self-test 3 min.

1. Must remember

Memorise cold
  • FONC (necessary): $x^*$ local min, $f$ differentiable $\Rightarrow \nabla f(x^*) = 0$.
  • SONC (necessary): $x^*$ local min, $f \in C^2$ $\Rightarrow \nabla f(x^*) = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f(x^*) \succeq 0$ (PSD).
  • SOSC (sufficient): $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f(x^*) \succ 0$ (PD) $\Rightarrow x^*$ is a strict (strong) local min.
  • The gaps: $x^4$ (strict min, $f''=0$: PD is not necessary); $x^3$ and $-x^4$ (FONC+SONC hold, not a min: PSD is not sufficient).
  • Quadratics $f = \tfrac12 x^\top Q x - b^\top x + r$: $\nabla f = Qx - b$, $\nabla^2 f = Q$. Here "both directions" hold: $\{\nabla f = 0,\ Q\succeq 0\}$ ⇔ (global) min; $\{\nabla f = 0,\ Q \succ 0\}$ ⇔ strict (unique) min. $Q$ has a negative eigenvalue ⇒ unbounded below. $Q \succeq 0$ singular: $b\in\text{range}(Q)$ ⇒ infinitely many minimisers; $b\notin\text{range}(Q)$ ⇒ none.
  • Directional derivatives: $\frac{d}{dt}f(x+td)\big|_{0} = \nabla f(x)^\top d$, $\ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}f(x+td)\big|_{0} = d^\top \nabla^2 f(x)\, d$.
  • PD / PSD tests (symmetric $A$): PD ⇔ all eigenvalues $\gt 0$ ⇔ all leading principal minors $\gt 0$ (Sylvester). PSD ⇔ all eigenvalues $\ge 0$ ⇔ all principal minors $\ge 0$ (leading ones are not enough).
  • Least squares $\tfrac12\|Ax-\bar b\|^2$: normal equations $A^\top A x^* = A^\top \bar b$ (always solvable, since range$(A^\top A)$ = range$(A^\top)$). Unique ⇔ $A$ has full column rank; then $x^* = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top \bar b$, projector $P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ ($P^2=P$, $P^\top=P$), projector onto null$(A^\top)$ is $I-P$.
  • Convexity ($f\in C^2$ on $\mathbb R^n$): definition $f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)$ ⇔ $f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top(y-x)$ for all $x,y$ ⇔ $\nabla^2 f(x)\succeq 0$ for every $x$ ⇔ every restriction to a line is convex. Convex: local min = global min; stationary point = global min. Max of convex functions is convex.
  • Peano-type: $f(y,z) = (z-py^2)(z-qy^2)$, $0\lt p\lt q$: min at 0 along every line, but $f(y,my^2)\lt 0$ for $p\lt m\lt q$ ⇒ not a local min.
  • Weierstrass: $X$ nonempty closed, $f$ proper, lsc, coercive ($\|x\|\to\infty \Rightarrow f(x)\to+\infty$), $X\cap\text{dom} f\ne\emptyset$ ⇒ a minimiser exists. $r(h) = o(h^2)$ means $r(h)/h^2 \to 0$.

2. Concepts

2.1 FONC, SONC, SOSC and what they do NOT say

In plain words: At the bottom of a valley the ground is flat (gradient zero) and curves up or stays flat in every direction (Hessian PSD). Those are things a minimum must have (necessary). If the ground is flat and curves strictly up in every direction (Hessian PD), you are surely at a strict bottom (sufficient). The trouble lives in the gap: Hessian PSD but singular (some direction with zero curvature). Then the second derivative says nothing and the higher-order terms decide.

Precisely (course: Theorems 1.3.2–1.3.4). Let $f\in C^2$ near $x^*$. Taylor: $f(x^*+h) = f(x^*) + \nabla f(x^*)^\top h + \tfrac12 h^\top \nabla^2 f(x^*) h + o(\|h\|^2)$.

ConditionStatementType
FONClocal min ⇒ $\nabla f(x^*)=0$necessary
SONClocal min ⇒ $\nabla f(x^*)=0,\ \nabla^2 f(x^*)\succeq 0$necessary
SOSC$\nabla f(x^*)=0,\ \nabla^2 f(x^*)\succ 0$ ⇒ strict local minsufficient

What SONC means: if the Hessian at a stationary point has even one negative eigenvalue, that point is not a local min (it is a saddle or a max). That is the useful, "contrapositive" use of SONC.
What SONC does NOT mean: "FONC + PSD ⇒ local min" is false. And "strict local min ⇒ PD" is false.

"Group 1 / Group 2" (his revision-class language): G1 = {$\nabla f(x^*)=0$, $\nabla^2 f(x^*)\succeq 0$}, G2 = {$\nabla f(x^*)=0$, $\nabla^2 f(x^*)\succ 0$}.

  • General $f$: local min ⇒ G1 (G1 necessary). G2 ⇒ strict local min (G2 sufficient). The reverse arrows fail ($x^3$, $x^4$).
  • Quadratic $f$: both arrows hold: G1 ⇔ min (global); G2 ⇔ strict min (unique global). Reason: the Taylor expansion is exact (no $o(\|h\|^2)$ term), because the Hessian $Q$ is constant. It is not because PSD becomes PD.
Trap "SOSC fails ⇒ not a minimum" is wrong ($x^4$). "SONC holds ⇒ minimum" is wrong ($x^3$, $-x^4$). A local minimum (non-strict) by definition includes strict minima. Weak inequality $\succeq$ in SONC, strict $\succ$ in SOSC: Quiz 1 tested exactly this.

2.2 Classifying stationary points (Hessian eigenvalues / Sylvester)

In plain words: solve $\nabla f = 0$; at each solution look at the signs of the Hessian's eigenvalues. All $\gt 0$: strict local min. All $\lt 0$: strict local max. Both signs: saddle. Some zero and the rest one sign: the test is inconclusive (look at higher-order terms or along curves).

Precisely: for symmetric $A$, PD ⇔ $\det A_k \gt 0$ for the leading $k\times k$ blocks, $k=1..n$ (Sylvester). PSD ⇔ every principal minor (keep the same set of rows and columns) is $\ge 0$. Example why leading minors are not enough for PSD: $A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$ has leading minors $0, 0$ (both $\ge0$) but the principal minor $a_{22}=-1\lt 0$: not PSD. For a 2×2: $\det\lt0$ ⇒ indefinite (saddle).

Worked example (Tutorial 1, problem 7a) $f(x,y) = (x^2-4)^2 + y^2$.
  1. $\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix}4x(x^2-4)\\ 2y\end{bmatrix} = 0 \Rightarrow (0,0),\ (2,0),\ (-2,0)$.
  2. $\nabla^2 f = \begin{bmatrix}12x^2-16 & 0\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$.
  3. $(0,0)$: diag$(-16, 2)$, one negative and one positive eigenvalue ⇒ saddle (SONC fails, so not a min; also not a max).
  4. $(\pm2,0)$: diag$(32, 2)\succ 0$ ⇒ SOSC ⇒ strict local min. Since $f\ge 0$ and $f(\pm 2,0)=0$, both are global minima.
Trap A strict local min need not be global ($x^3+y^3-3xy$ has a strict local min at $(1,1)$ but $f\to-\infty$). To claim "global", give a reason: $f\ge$ value, convexity, or coercive + compare all stationary values.

2.3 Quadratics and least squares

In plain words: a quadratic is a bowl, a trough, or a saddle, decided entirely by $Q$. Bowl ($Q\succ0$): one bottom at $Q^{-1}b$. Trough ($Q\succeq 0$ singular): either a flat valley floor of minimisers (if the tilt $b$ does not point along the flat direction, i.e. $b\in$ range$(Q)$) or the floor tilts downhill forever (no minimiser). Any negative eigenvalue: walk along that eigenvector and $f\to-\infty$.

Precisely: $f(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Qx-b^\top x+r$, $Q=Q^\top$: $\nabla f=Qx-b$, $\nabla^2 f=Q$ (only the symmetric part of $Q$ matters). Least squares $f(x)=\tfrac12\|Ax-\bar b\|_2^2 = \tfrac12x^\top(A^\top A)x-(A^\top\bar b)^\top x+\tfrac12\|\bar b\|^2$, so $Q=A^\top A\succeq0$ always, and $x^\top A^\top A x=\|Ax\|^2\gt0$ for $x\neq0$ exactly when $A$ has full column rank. Then SOSC holds and the solution is unique. Projection: $\hat b = A x^* = P\bar b$ with $P=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$; residual $\tilde b=\bar b-\hat b=(I-P)\bar b \perp$ range$(A)$, i.e. $A^\top\tilde b=0$.

Worked example: fit a line through (0,0), (1,1), (2,1)
  1. Model $c_0 + c_1 t$: $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $\bar b=(0,1,1)^\top$. Columns independent ⇒ full column rank ⇒ unique solution.
  2. $A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}$, $A^\top\bar b=(2,3)^\top$. Solve: $x^*=\tfrac16\begin{bmatrix}5&-3\\-3&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}=(1/6,\ 1/2)^\top$.
  3. Residual $\bar b-Ax^*=(-1/6,\ 1/3,\ -1/6)^\top$; check $A^\top(\text{residual})=(0,0)^\top$ ✓ (that is the normal equation).
Trap "The least-squares solution is always unique": false (only with full column rank). But the normal equations always have a solution, because range$(A^\top A)$ = range$(A^\top)$. The projector onto range$(A)$ is $A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$, not $A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}A$ (that one is for range$(A^\top)$ when $A$ has full row rank).

2.4 Convexity

In plain words: a convex function's chord always lies above the graph; equivalently its tangent plane always lies below the graph; equivalently it curves up (or is flat) in every direction at every point. Big payoff: no false valleys. Any local min is global, and any stationary point is a global min.

Precisely (Theorems 1.8.1–1.8.4), $C$ convex:

  1. Definition: $f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)$, all $x,y\in C$, $\theta\in[0,1]$. Strict ($\lt$ for $x\ne y$, $\theta\in(0,1)$) = strictly convex.
  2. Lines: $f$ convex ⇔ $g(\alpha)=f(x+\alpha s)$ convex for every $x$, $s$.
  3. First order ($f$ differentiable): convex ⇔ $f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top(y-x)$ for all $x,y$. Consequence: $\nabla f(x^*)=0$ ⇒ global min.
  4. Second order ($f\in C^2$): convex ⇔ $\nabla^2 f(x)\succeq0$ for all $x$. PD everywhere ⇒ strictly convex (not ⇔: $x^4$ is strictly convex with $f''(0)=0$).
  5. Local min of convex $f$ ⇒ global min (Theorem 1.8.2).
  6. $f=\max_i f_i$ with each $f_i$ convex ⇒ $f$ convex (no Hessian needed, works when $f$ is not differentiable).

Contrast with SONC: SONC is PSD at one point, a property of a candidate. Convexity is PSD at every point, a property of the whole function.

How to check convexity: (a) Hessian PSD everywhere (eigenvalues or all principal minors); (b) to disprove, find one point where the Hessian has a negative eigenvalue, or a midpoint counterexample $f(\tfrac{u+v}2)\gt\tfrac{f(u)+f(v)}2$, or a line along which $g''\lt0$; (c) rules: sums with nonnegative weights, max, affine composition.

Worked example: log-sum-exp $f(x,y)=\log(e^x+e^y)$ (Tutorial 1, problem 14)
  1. Let $p_1=e^x/(e^x+e^y)$, $p_2=e^y/(e^x+e^y)$, so $p_1+p_2=1$, $p_i\gt0$.
  2. $\nabla^2 f=\text{diag}(p)-pp^\top=\begin{bmatrix}p_1-p_1^2&-p_1p_2\\-p_1p_2&p_2-p_2^2\end{bmatrix}=p_1p_2\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}$ (using $p_1-p_1^2 = p_1p_2$).
  3. $d^\top\nabla^2 f\,d=p_1p_2(d_1-d_2)^2\ge0$ for all $d$ ⇒ PSD at every point ⇒ convex. In $n$-D: $\sum p_id_i^2-(\sum p_id_i)^2\ge0$ by Cauchy–Schwarz.
  4. Not PD: $\nabla^2 f\,(1,1)^\top=0$ (along $(1,1)$, $f(x+t,y+t)=f(x,y)+t$ is a straight line). So not strictly convex.
Worked example: max of convex is convex (Tutorial 1, problem 20) $f=\max\{x^2,y^2\}$. For each $i$: $f_i(\theta u+(1-\theta)v)\le\theta f_i(u)+(1-\theta)f_i(v)\le\theta f(u)+(1-\theta)f(v)$ (since $f_i\le f$, $\theta\ge0$). The right side does not depend on $i$, so take the max over $i$ on the left: $f(\theta u+(1-\theta)v)\le\theta f(u)+(1-\theta)f(v)$. ∎
Trap $f\ge0$ does not imply convex: $(x_1^2-x_2^2)^2$ is not convex (Midterm 1 Q I). Convexity needs PSD everywhere, so one bad point kills it; one good point proves nothing. "PD Hessian" in a quiz means strict convexity is sufficient, not necessary. Never say "concave set".

2.5 Peano-type counterexample, Weierstrass, little-o

Peano (Tutorial 1, problem 8): If $x^*$ minimises $f$ along every line through it, then $g'(0)=\nabla f(x^*)^\top d=0$ for all $d$ ⇒ $\nabla f(x^*)=0$ (part a). But $x^*$ need not be a local min (part b):

Worked example $f(y,z)=(z-py^2)(z-qy^2)$, $0\lt p\lt q$.
  1. Line $(y,z)=\alpha(a,b)$: $g(\alpha)=\alpha^2(b-pa^2\alpha)(b-qa^2\alpha)$. $g'(0)=0$, $g''(0)=2b^2$. If $b\ne0$: $g''(0)\gt0$, strict min at $\alpha=0$. If $b=0$: $g=pqa^4\alpha^4$, again min at 0.
  2. Along the curve $z=my^2$ with $p\lt m\lt q$: $f=(m-p)(m-q)y^4\lt0=f(0,0)$ for $y\ne0$, arbitrarily close to 0. So 0 is not a local min.
  3. Lesson: "min along every line" ≠ "local min". The bad points sit on a curve between the two parabolas, which no straight line follows. (Hessian at 0 is diag$(0,2)$: PSD, consistent with SONC.)

Weierstrass (coercive version, Theorem 1.3.1): $X$ nonempty, closed; $f$ proper, lower semicontinuous, coercive; $X\cap\text{dom}f\ne\emptyset$ ⇒ $\min_{x\in X}f$ has a solution (solution set nonempty, compact). Use: a continuous coercive $f$ on $\mathbb R^n$ has a global min, so the global min is the stationary point with the smallest value.

Little-o: $r(h)=o(h^2)$ as $h\to0$ means $r(h)/h^2\to0$. It is why the sign of $\tfrac12 f''(x^*)h^2$ decides when $f''(x^*)\ne0$, and why it cannot decide when $f''(x^*)=0$.

4.1 Quiz 1 (past paper, Ch 1): 15 selected MCQs

P1 Quiz 1 Q3 (past paper)MCQ

For $f(x)=x^4$, which statement is true at $x^*=0$?

  1. (a) FONC fails.
  2. (b) SONC fails.
  3. (c) SOSC holds.
  4. (d) $x^*=0$ is a strict local minimum although SOSC does not hold.
Show answer

Answer: (d). $f'(0)=0$ (FONC ✓), $f''(0)=0\ge0$ (SONC ✓), $f''(0)\not\gt0$ (SOSC ✗), yet $x^4\gt0$ for $x\ne0$: strict min. (a), (b) wrong since both hold; (c) wrong since $f''(0)=0$. Lesson: SOSC is not necessary.

P2 Quiz 1 Q4 (past paper)MCQ

For $f(x)=x^3$, at $x=0$ we have $f'(0)=f''(0)=0$. What is the correct conclusion?

  1. (a) 0 is a strict local minimum.
  2. (b) 0 is a local maximum.
  3. (c) 0 is a stationary inflection point.
  4. (d) SOSC holds at 0.
Show answer

Answer: (c). $x^3\lt0$ for $x\lt0$ and $\gt0$ for $x\gt0$, so neither min (a) nor max (b). SOSC needs $f''(0)\gt0$ (d ✗). FONC and SONC both hold: they are not sufficient.

P3 Quiz 1 Q6 (past paper)MCQ

For positive weights $w_i$, the minimiser of $f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(x-y_i)^2$ is

  1. (a) $\frac1n\sum y_i$
  2. (b) $\sum w_iy_i$
  3. (c) $\sum w_iy_i/\sum w_i$
  4. (d) $\sum y_i/\sum w_i$
Show answer

Answer: (c). $f'(x)=2\sum w_i(x-y_i)=0\Rightarrow x^*=\sum w_iy_i/\sum w_i$; $f''=2\sum w_i\gt0$ so it is the unique min. (a) is the unweighted case only; (b), (d) are not even unchanged when all $w_i$ are scaled by 2, which the true answer must be.

P4 Quiz 1 Q7 (past paper)MCQ

The notation $r(h)=o(h^2)$ $(h\to0)$ means

  1. (a) $r(h)=h^2$ for all sufficiently small $h$.
  2. (b) $r(h)/h^2\to0$.
  3. (c) $h^2/r(h)\to0$.
  4. (d) $r(h)$ is always positive near 0.
Show answer

Answer: (b). $r$ goes to zero faster than $h^2$. (c) says the opposite ($r$ much bigger than $h^2$); (a) and (d) say nothing about the rate; $o(h^2)$ can have any sign.

P5 Quiz 1 Q8 (past paper)MCQ

At a stationary point $x^*$, $f(x^*+h)-f(x^*)=\tfrac12f''(x^*)h^2+o(h^2)$. If $f''(x^*)\lt0$, what follows?

  1. (a) $x^*$ can still be a local minimum because the remainder may dominate.
  2. (b) $x^*$ cannot be a local minimum.
  3. (c) $x^*$ must be a strict local minimum.
  4. (d) No conclusion is possible even locally.
Show answer

Answer: (b). Divide by $h^2$: $\frac{f(x^*+h)-f(x^*)}{h^2}\to\tfrac12 f''(x^*)\lt0$, so the difference is negative for small $h\ne0$. (This is SONC.) (a) is wrong because $o(h^2)/h^2\to0$, the remainder cannot dominate a nonzero quadratic term. (c) opposite; (d) wrong, it is in fact a strict local max.

P6 Quiz 1 Q10 (past paper)MCQ

For $\phi(t)=f(x^*+td)$, with $f$ differentiable at $x^*$, $\phi'(0)$ is

  1. (a) $d^\top\nabla^2f(x^*)d$
  2. (b) $\nabla f(x^*)^\top d$
  3. (c) $\|\nabla f(x^*)\|_2^2$
  4. (d) $\nabla f(x^*)+d$
Show answer

Answer: (b) (chain rule). (a) is $\phi''(0)$; (c) is the special case $d=\nabla f$; (d) is a vector, not a number.

P7 Quiz 1 Q13 (past paper)MCQ

For $f(x)=x_1^4+x_2^2+\dots+x_n^2$, at 0:

  1. (a) The Hessian is indefinite, so 0 is a saddle.
  2. (b) The Hessian is positive definite.
  3. (c) The Hessian is PSD but singular, and 0 is nevertheless a strict local minimum.
  4. (d) The Hessian is PSD and singular, and so 0 is a non-strict local minimum.
Show answer

Answer: (c). $\nabla^2f(0)=\text{diag}(0,2,\dots,2)$: PSD, singular, not indefinite (a ✗), not PD (b ✗). $f(x)\gt0$ for every $x\ne0$, so strict min. (d) wrongly infers "non-strict" from singularity: a singular Hessian decides nothing.

P8 Quiz 1 Q14 (past paper)MCQ

For $f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^3-3x_1x_2^2$, which pair of directions demonstrates saddle behaviour at the origin for $t\gt0$ (evaluate $f(td)$)?

  1. (a) $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, because both give positive values.
  2. (b) $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$, because they give values of opposite sign.
  3. (c) $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$, because both give zero values.
  4. (d) $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$, because both give negative values.
Show answer

Answer: (b). $f(t,0)=t^3\gt0$, $f(t,t)=t^3-3t^3=-2t^3\lt0$: values above and below $f(0)=0$ ⇒ saddle. (a) $f(0,t)=0$, not positive; (c) $f(-t,-t)=2t^3\ne0$; (d) $f(0,\pm t)=0$. Here $\nabla^2 f(0)=0$, so the Hessian test is useless; directions decide.

P9 Quiz 1 Q16 (past paper)MCQ

Under the coercive version of the Weierstrass theorem, which assumptions guarantee a minimiser of $f$ over $X$?

  1. (a) $X$ open, $f$ differentiable and bounded below.
  2. (b) $X$ nonempty and closed; $f$ proper, lower semicontinuous and coercive; $X\cap\text{dom}f\ne\emptyset$.
  3. (c) $X$ bounded; $f$ differentiable and convex.
  4. (d) $X=\mathbb R^n$ and $\nabla f(x)=0$ somewhere.
Show answer

Answer: (b), Theorem 1.3.1 word for word. (a) $e^x$ on $\mathbb R$ is bounded below with no min; (c) $X$ bounded but not closed, e.g. $f=x$ on $(0,1)$; (d) $x^3$ has $f'(0)=0$ and no min.

P10 Quiz 1 Q20 (past paper)MCQ

For positive semidefiniteness of a real symmetric matrix, which is correct?

  1. (a) It is enough to check only the leading principal minors.
  2. (b) All principal minors must be nonnegative.
  3. (c) The determinant alone must be positive.
  4. (d) All off-diagonal entries must vanish.
