Notation used on this page (one letter = one meaning):

  • $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$: the decision variable (a column vector). $x^T$ = transpose (row vector), so $a^Tx=a_1x_1+\dots+a_nx_n$.
  • Vector inequalities are entry by entry: $x\ge0$ means every $x_j\ge0$.
  • The primal problem (the original one): $\min f(x)$ s.t. ("subject to") $h(x)=0$, $g(x)\le0$. Here $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ is the cost, $h=(h_1,\dots,h_m)$ are the $m$ equality constraints, $g=(g_1,\dots,g_r)$ the $r$ inequality constraints.
  • $\Omega=\{x: h(x)=0,\ g(x)\le0\}$: the feasible set (points that satisfy every constraint). A point in it is "feasible".
  • $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m$: one price (multiplier) per equality, any sign ("free"). $\mu\in\mathbb{R}^r$: one price per inequality, $\mu\ge0$. Quiz 4 packs them as $\eta=(\lambda,\mu)$; the notes write $y=(u,v)$. Same thing.
  • $L(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\lambda^Th(x)+\mu^Tg(x)$: the Lagrangian. $q(\lambda,\mu)$: the dual function; $\mathbb{D}$: where $q\gt-\infty$ (sections D–E).
  • $\inf$ ("infimum") = the greatest lower bound: the lowest value a function gets down to, even if no point actually reaches it (e.g. $\inf_{x}e^x=0$, never reached). $\sup$ = the same for "highest". A value is attained if some point actually reaches it.
  • $p^*=\inf\{f(x):x\in\Omega\}$: the primal optimal value. $d^*=\sup\{q(\lambda,\mu):\mu\ge0\}$: the dual optimal value. A star on a point ($x^*$, $\lambda^*$, $\mu^*$) means "optimal".
  • $\nabla f(x)$: gradient, a column vector. $\nabla^2f$: Hessian. $J_h=[\nabla h_1\cdots\nabla h_m]$, $J_g=[\nabla g_1\cdots\nabla g_r]$: gradients stacked as columns (the course's Jacobian convention).
  • "PSD" = positive semidefinite ($v^THv\ge0$ for all $v$); "PD" = positive definite ($\gt0$ for $v\ne0$).
  • LP sections: $c\in\mathbb{R}^n$ cost vector, $A$ an $m\times n$ matrix, $b\in\mathbb{R}^m$; $a_i^T$ = row $i$ of $A$; $(A^T\lambda)_j$ = entry $j$ of the vector $A^T\lambda$.
  • $y$ (in sections A–C): "another point", usually another feasible point. $\alpha\in[0,1]$: a mixing weight.

0. Why this topic exists

Chapter 6 gave you the KKT conditions: equations that a constrained minimum must (usually) satisfy. Two questions were left open:

  1. When is a KKT point really the best point? In Ch 6, KKT was only necessary (and only under a constraint qualification). A point could satisfy KKT and still be a maximum or a saddle. Chapter 7's answer: if the problem is convex, a KKT point is the global minimum. Sections A–C.
  2. How do I know how good my answer is, without knowing the true minimum? Suppose you found a feasible point with cost 9.5. Is the best possible 9.4 or 2? Chapter 7's answer: build a second problem, the dual, whose every value is a guaranteed lower bound on the best cost. If you find a dual value of 9.5 too, you've proved 9.5 is optimal. Sections D–L.
The whole idea of duality in one sentence

Instead of forbidding constraint violations, charge a price for them; the cheapest you can do under any fair prices is a lower bound on the true optimum, and the dual problem looks for the prices that make this bound as high as possible.

For linear programs (LPs) this gives a famous pair: $\min c^Tx$ s.t. $Ax=b,\ x\ge0$ has dual $\max b^T\lambda$ s.t. $A^T\lambda\le c$. He will give an LP in a different form and ask you to derive its dual (section L).

A. Convex sets and convex functions (recap of Ch 1, only what we need)

A1. Convex set: "the straight path between any two points stays inside"

A set $C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ is convex if for any two points $x,y\in C$ the whole segment between them is in $C$:

$$x,y\in C,\ \alpha\in[0,1]\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha x+(1-\alpha)y\in C.$$

($\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y$ is the point a fraction $1-\alpha$ of the way from $x$ to $y$: $\alpha=1$ gives $x$, $\alpha=0$ gives $y$, $\alpha=\frac12$ the midpoint.)

Left (blue): an ellipse, convex. Right (red): a half-ring; the dashed segment between two of its points passes through the hole, so it is not convex.

Convex examples you'll meet: all of $\mathbb{R}^n$; a line or plane $\{x:a^Tx=b\}$; a half-space $\{x:a^Tx\le b\}$; a ball $\{x:\|x\|\le r\}$; and any intersection of convex sets (if $x,y$ are in every set, the segment is in every set). Not convex: a circle $\{x_1^2+x_2^2=1\}$ (just the curve), a ring, two separate intervals.

A2. Convex function: "every chord lies on or above the graph"

$f$ is convex (on a convex set) if for all $x,y$ and $\alpha\in[0,1]$:

$$f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y).$$

Left side: the height of the graph at a point between $x$ and $y$. Right side: the height of the straight chord joining $(x,f(x))$ and $(y,f(y))$ at that same point. So "graph below chord" = bowl shape.

Blue: a convex $f$. Red: the chord between $x$ and $y$; the graph (blue dot) is below it. Green dashed: the tangent line at the bottom; the graph is everywhere above its tangent lines.

Words you need:

  • Concave: $-f$ is convex (chord on or below the graph, a dome). Example: $\ln x$, $\sqrt x$ on $x\gt0$.
  • Affine: $f(x)=a^Tx+\beta$ (linear plus a constant). Affine functions are both convex and concave (chord = graph).
  • Strictly convex: the inequality is strict ($\lt$) whenever $x\ne y$ and $0\lt\alpha\lt1$. Example $x^2$; $|x|$ and affine functions are convex but not strictly.

Two tests (from Ch 1):

  • Second-order: twice-differentiable $f$ is convex iff $\nabla^2f(x)$ is PSD at every point (in 1D: $f''\ge0$). Example: $x_1^2+x_2^2$ has Hessian $2I$, PSD ✓. $e^x$: $f''=e^x\gt0$ ✓.
  • First-order (the one used in every proof below): differentiable $f$ is convex iff the graph lies above every tangent: $$f(y)\ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T(y-x)\quad\text{for all }x,y.$$
Trap: convex along a set, not everywhere (Quiz 4 Q1)

$f=x_1^2-x_2^2$ is not convex on $\mathbb{R}^2$ (Hessian $\mathrm{diag}(2,-2)$ is indefinite). But on the line $\{(t,at):t\in\mathbb{R}\}$ it becomes $\phi(t)=f(t,at)=(1-a^2)t^2$, which is convex iff $1-a^2\ge0$, i.e. $|a|\le1$. Only the curvature along the set matters. At $a=\pm1$ the restriction is the constant 0, which still counts as convex (just not strictly).

B. Which constraints give a convex feasible set

Plain words: each constraint cuts out a set; the feasible set $\Omega$ is the intersection of these sets. Intersections of convex sets are convex (A1). So we just need each single constraint to cut out a convex set.

Rule 1: $g(x)\le0$ with $g$ convex gives a convex set. Proof (two lines): take $x,y$ with $g(x)\le0$, $g(y)\le0$. By convexity of $g$, $$g(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\le\alpha\underbrace{g(x)}_{\le0}+(1-\alpha)\underbrace{g(y)}_{\le0}\le0,$$ so the in-between point also satisfies the constraint. ✓

Rule 2: $g(x)\ge0$ with $g$ concave is the same thing: it says $-g(x)\le0$ and $-g$ is convex.

Rule 3: $h(x)=0$ with $h$ affine gives a convex set (a line, plane, …). Why affine and not just convex: $h=0$ means $h\le0$ and $-h\le0$, so we need both $h$ and $-h$ convex, i.e. $h$ both convex and concave, i.e. affine.

Thm 7.2.1 (convex feasible set) and the definition of a convex problem

The feasible set is convex if every equality is affine $=0$, every "$\le0$" constraint has a convex function, every "$\ge0$" constraint has a concave function.

The problem $\min f(x)$ s.t. $h(x)=0$, $g(x)\le0$ is a convex optimization problem if $f$ is convex, every $g_j$ is convex and $h$ is affine.

Worked example: which constraints keep the feasible set convex?
  1. $x_1^2+x_2^2-4\le0$: convex function $\le 0$ ✓ (a disc).
  2. $-x_1\le0$ (i.e. $x_1\ge0$): affine, hence convex ✓.
  3. $\ln x_1\ge 0$ (on $x_1\gt0$): concave function $\ge0$ ✓ (it is just $x_1\ge1$).
  4. $x_1^2+x_2^2=1$: equality with a non-affine function ✗ (a circle, not convex).
  5. $x_1^2-x_2\ge0$: convex function $\ge0$ ✗ (the region outside a parabola, not convex: $(1,1)$ and $(-1,1)$ are in it, their midpoint $(0,1)$ is not).
Traps (he was explicit)
  • Be sure that you don't write concave optimization problem. Never, exam. Maximising a concave function is still called a convex problem (it equals minimising the convex $-f$). And there is nothing called concave set.
  • Write the constraint in the right direction before judging it: $x_1\ge2$ is $2-x_1\le0$ (affine ✓). "$g\ge0$ with $g$ convex" is the bad case.
  • The rules are sufficient, not necessary: $\{x^3\le0\}=\{x\le0\}$ happens to be convex although $x^3$ is not convex. For the exam, judge by the rules.

C. Why convex problems are nice

Plain words: a bowl over a dent-free region has no false bottoms. Every local minimum is the global one, the KKT equations are enough to prove you're at the global minimum, and if several points are optimal they form a convex set.

C1. Local minimum ⇒ global minimum (Thm 7.2.3)

Recall: $x^*\in\Omega$ is a local minimiser if $f(x^*)\le f(x)$ for all feasible $x$ near $x^*$; global if for all feasible $x$.

Proof (by contradiction, 3 steps)
  1. Suppose $x^*$ is a local minimiser but some feasible $y$ has $f(y)\lt f(x^*)$.
  2. Points on the segment $x_t=x^*+t(y-x^*)=(1-t)x^*+ty$, $t\in(0,1]$, are feasible ($\Omega$ is convex), and by convexity of $f$: $f(x_t)\le(1-t)f(x^*)+tf(y)\lt f(x^*)$.
  3. As $t\to0$, $x_t$ gets as close to $x^*$ as we like, with a smaller cost. That contradicts "local minimiser". So no such $y$ exists. ∎

C2. The set of minimisers is convex (Cor 7.2.6). If $x$ and $y$ both have the optimal value $p^*$, any in-between point is feasible and has $f\le\alpha p^*+(1-\alpha)p^*=p^*$, so it is optimal too. (So there is either 0, 1 or infinitely many minimisers, never exactly 2.)

C3. The geometric first-order condition (Thm 7.2.4)

For convex differentiable $f$ on a convex $\Omega$: $x^*$ is a global minimiser iff $$\nabla f(x^*)^T(y-x^*)\ge0\quad\text{for every feasible }y.$$ In words: from $x^*$, every direction that points into the feasible set goes uphill (or flat) at first order. Proof of "⇐": the first-order test gives $f(y)\ge f(x^*)+\nabla f(x^*)^T(y-x^*)\ge f(x^*)$. Note: $\nabla f(x^*)$ need not be 0 at a constrained minimum. Example: $\min x$ s.t. $x\ge1$: $x^*=1$, $f'(1)=1\ne0$, but $f'(1)(y-1)\ge0$ for all $y\ge1$ ✓.

C4. KKT ⇒ global minimum (Thm 7.2.8): the proof chain (Midterm 2 Q I)

Recall the KKT conditions at $x^*$ with multipliers $\lambda^*,\mu^*$ (all five parts, he insists):

$$\underbrace{\nabla f(x^*)+J_h(x^*)\lambda^*+J_g(x^*)\mu^*=0}_{\text{stationarity of }L}\quad h(x^*)=0\quad g(x^*)\le0\quad\mu^*\ge0\quad\underbrace{\mu_j^*g_j(x^*)=0\ \forall j}_{\text{complementary slackness (CS)}}$$

A constraint is active at $x^*$ if $g_j(x^*)=0$, inactive if $g_j(x^*)\lt0$. CS says: inactive ⇒ $\mu_j^*=0$.

Claim: for a convex problem with $h(x)=Ax-b$, any KKT point $x^*$ is a global minimiser. No constraint qualification needed. Idea: take any feasible $y$, look at the step $d=y-x^*$, and show $f$ cannot go down along it.

The five steps (learn the reason for each: these are the Midterm 2 MCQs)
  1. Equalities: $Ad=Ay-Ax^*=b-b=0$. Since $J_h=A^T$ (each $\nabla h_i$ is row $i$ of $A$, as a column), $d^TJ_h=(Ad)^T=0^T$.
  2. Each $g_j$ is convex, so by the first-order test $\nabla g_j(x^*)^Td\le g_j(y)-g_j(x^*)$. If $j$ is active this is $\le g_j(y)\le0$. If inactive, the right side can be positive: no fixed sign.
  3. CS kills the inactive $\mu_j^*$; the active ones are $\ge0$ times something $\le0$. So $d^TJ_g\mu^*=\sum_j\mu_j^*\nabla g_j(x^*)^Td\le0$.
  4. Convex $f$ + stationarity: $f(y)-f(x^*)\ge\nabla f(x^*)^Td=-d^T[J_h\lambda^*+J_g\mu^*]$.
  5. Combine: $f(y)-f(x^*)\ge-\underbrace{d^TJ_h\lambda^*}_{=0}-\underbrace{d^TJ_g\mu^*}_{\le0}\ge0$. So $f(y)\ge f(x^*)$ for every feasible $y$: global minimum. ∎ Contrapositive: a point that is not a (local) minimiser cannot satisfy KKT.

C5. But KKT is NOT necessary without a constraint qualification

Convexity makes KKT sufficient. It does not make it necessary: a convex problem can have a minimum where no multipliers exist. (In Ch 6, necessity needed a constraint qualification (CQ) such as LICQ; for convex problems the standard CQ is Slater's condition, section G.)

Worked example: optimum exists, KKT holds nowhere

$\min (x-1)^2$ s.t. $x^2\le0$. The only feasible point is $x^*=0$, so it is the global minimiser. Stationarity would need $2(0-1)+\mu\cdot2\cdot0=0$, i.e. $-2=0$: impossible for every $\mu$. No KKT multiplier. Strict convexity does not replace a CQ. (Slater fails: no $x$ has $x^2\lt0$.)

D. The Lagrangian as "prices on constraints"

Plain words. Imagine you are allowed to break the constraints, but you pay a fine. For each inequality $g_j(x)\le0$ you pay $\mu_j\,g_j(x)$:

  • if you violate it ($g_j\gt0$) you pay a positive amount;
  • if you satisfy it with room to spare ($g_j\lt0$) you get a refund.

For this to be a fine and not a reward, the price must be $\mu_j\ge0$. For an equality $h_i(x)=0$ you pay $\lambda_ih_i(x)$; violating in either direction might be tempting, so the price $\lambda_i$ may have either sign ("free"). Your total bill is the Lagrangian:

$$L(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\lambda^Th(x)+\mu^Tg(x)=f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_ih_i(x)+\sum_{j=1}^r\mu_jg_j(x),\qquad\lambda\text{ free},\ \mu\ge0.$$
The key fact (everything in F–I rests on it)

At a feasible $x$ and any prices with $\mu\ge0$: $\ h(x)=0$ and $\mu^Tg(x)\le0$ (each term is $\ge0$ times $\le0$), so

$$L(x,\lambda,\mu)\le f(x).$$

On feasible points the bill is never more than the true cost.

Before writing $L$, rewrite every constraint as $h=0$ or $g\le0$. Examples: $x_1\ge2\to 2-x_1\le0$; $x\ge0\to -x\le0$; $x_1+x_2=4\to x_1+x_2-4=0$. (He said he will give constraints in non-standard form on purpose.)

With numbers (the Ch 7 tutorial problem; we finish it in section K)

$\min\ \tfrac12(2x_1^2+x_2^2)$ s.t. $x_1+x_2=4$, $x_1\ge2$. Write $h=x_1+x_2-4$, $g=2-x_1\le0$:

$$L=x_1^2+\tfrac12x_2^2+\lambda(x_1+x_2-4)+\mu(2-x_1).$$

Take the feasible point $x=(3,1)$: $f=\frac12(18+1)=9.5$. With prices $\lambda=-1$, $\mu=1$: $h=0$, $g=2-3=-1$, so $L=9.5+(-1)(0)+(1)(-1)=8.5\le9.5$ ✓.

E. The dual function $q$: the best cost when constraints are priced instead of enforced

Plain words. Fix the prices $(\lambda,\mu)$. Now you may choose any $x$ at all (no constraints any more; you just pay the fines). The lowest bill you can get is $q(\lambda,\mu)$:

Definitions (course form) $$q(\lambda,\mu)=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^n}L(x,\lambda,\mu),\qquad \mathbb{D}=\{(\lambda,\mu):q(\lambda,\mu)\gt-\infty\}.$$

Dual problem: $\ \max\ q(\lambda,\mu)$ s.t. $\mu\ge0$, $(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{D}$. Its optimal value is $d^*$.

The infimum is over all $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$: the constraints are not put back. (Only a function domain stays: if a formula is only defined for some $x$, e.g. a constraint $x_1^2/x_2\le0$ only makes sense for $x_2\gt0$, then the infimum runs over $x_2\gt0$. Example J5 below is such a case.)

Why it can be $-\infty$: some prices let you get an unboundedly low bill. Those prices are useless as bounds, so the dual only looks inside $\mathbb{D}$.

E1. First example, every step

$\min x^2$ s.t. $x\ge1$ (answer obviously $x^*=1$, $p^*=1$). From Check D: $L=x^2+\mu(1-x)$.

  1. Fix $\mu\ge0$ and minimise over all $x\in\mathbb{R}$: $L$ is a parabola in $x$, derivative $2x-\mu=0$, so $x=\mu/2$.
  2. Plug in: $q(\mu)=\frac{\mu^2}{4}+\mu\big(1-\frac{\mu}{2}\big)=\mu-\frac{\mu^2}{4}$. Finite for every $\mu$, so $\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}$.
  3. Dual: $\max_{\mu\ge0}\ \mu-\frac{\mu^2}{4}$. Derivative $1-\frac{\mu}{2}=0$: $\mu^*=2$, $d^*=2-1=1$.
  4. Compare: $d^*=1=p^*$. And the $x$ that minimised $L$ at $\mu^*=2$ is $x=\mu^*/2=1=x^*$. The dual found the answer.

E2. The linear-in-$x$ rule (the whole LP dual rests on it)

If, after collecting terms, $L=r^Tx+\beta$ (with $r$ and $\beta$ not depending on $x$), then

$$\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^n}(r^Tx+\beta)=\begin{cases}\beta,&r=0\\-\infty,&r\ne0.\end{cases}$$

Why: if $r\ne0$, walk along $x=-tr$: the value is $-t\|r\|^2+\beta\to-\infty$ as $t\to\infty$. If $r=0$, $L$ is the constant $\beta$. In his words: The infimum is finite if and only if the coefficients of x vanishes.

Worked example (notes Example 7.4.2)

$\min x$ s.t. $-x\le0$ (i.e. $x\ge0$; answer $x^*=0$). $L=x-\mu x=(1-\mu)x$. Linear in $x$ with coefficient $1-\mu$: $q(\mu)=0$ if $\mu=1$, $-\infty$ otherwise. So $\mathbb{D}=\{1\}$ and $d^*=q(1)=0=p^*$.

E3. The picture: $q$ is the lower envelope of straight lines, so it is concave

Take Quiz 4's problem $\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}x^2+1$ s.t. $(x-2)(x-4)\le0$ (feasible set $[2,4]$, so $x^*=2$, $p^*=5$). $L=x^2+1+\mu(x-2)(x-4)$.

Freeze $x$: then $L$ is a straight line in $\mu$, intercept $x^2+1$, slope $(x-2)(x-4)$. E.g. $x=0$: $1+8\mu$; $x=1$: $2+3\mu$; $x=2$: the flat line 5; $x=3$: $10-\mu$. $q(\mu)$ is "the lowest of all these lines" at each $\mu$:

Grey lines: $L(x,\mu)$ as a function of $\mu$ for a few fixed $x$. Blue: $q(\mu)=10-\mu-\frac{9}{1+\mu}$, the lowest value over all $x$; it touches each line once. Green dashed: the line for the feasible point $x=2$, which is the true optimum $p^*=5$. The blue curve never goes above it (that's weak duality, section F) and touches it at $\mu^*=2$.

Computing $q$ here: $L=(1+\mu)x^2-6\mu x+1+8\mu$. For $\mu\ge0$ the $x^2$ coefficient is positive, so set $\partial L/\partial x=2(1+\mu)x-6\mu=0$: $x_\mu=\frac{3\mu}{1+\mu}$. Plugging in: $q(\mu)=1+8\mu-\frac{9\mu^2}{1+\mu}=10-\mu-\frac{9}{1+\mu}$. Then $q'(\mu)=-1+\frac{9}{(1+\mu)^2}=0$ gives $\mu^*=2$, $q(2)=10-2-3=5=p^*$.

$q$ is always concave and $\mathbb{D}$ is always convex (even for nonconvex problems)

Why: for each fixed $x$, $L(x,\cdot,\cdot)$ is affine in the prices. Write $\eta=(\lambda,\mu)$ and take $\eta_0,\eta_1$, $\alpha\in[0,1]$:

$$\begin{aligned}q(\alpha\eta_0+(1-\alpha)\eta_1)&=\inf_x\big[\alpha L(x,\eta_0)+(1-\alpha)L(x,\eta_1)\big]\\&\ge\alpha\inf_xL(x,\eta_0)+(1-\alpha)\inf_xL(x,\eta_1)=\alpha q(\eta_0)+(1-\alpha)q(\eta_1).\end{aligned}$$

(The "$\ge$": minimising the two parts separately can only do better than minimising their sum together.) That is the definition of concave. And if $\eta_0,\eta_1\in\mathbb{D}$, the right side is $\gt-\infty$, so the mix is in $\mathbb{D}$: $\mathbb{D}$ is convex. No convexity of $f$, $g$, $h$ was used. So the dual is always a convex problem (maximise a concave function = minimise the convex $-q$).

E4. How to compute the infimum in practice

  • $L$ linear in some variable: its coefficient must be $0$, else $q=-\infty$ (E2).
  • $L$ a positive definite quadratic in $x$: set $\nabla_xL=0$, solve for $x(\lambda,\mu)$, substitute back (or complete the square).
  • A coefficient that can be $\le0$, or exponential terms: check the edge cases separately. Example: $e^x+\mu x$ with $\mu\gt0$ goes to $-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$.

Restricting $x$ gives a different function (it would be the constant 5 here, Quiz 4 Q4's wrong option (d)).

F. Weak duality: every dual value is below every feasible primal value

He said: it's used frequently. You should always remember this.

Weak duality, with its 3-line proof

For any feasible $\bar x$ and any prices $(\bar\lambda,\bar\mu)$ with $\bar\mu\ge0$:

$$q(\bar\lambda,\bar\mu)\ \overset{(1)}{\le}\ L(\bar x,\bar\lambda,\bar\mu)\ \overset{(2)}{=}\ f(\bar x)+\bar\lambda^T\underbrace{h(\bar x)}_{=0}+\underbrace{\bar\mu^Tg(\bar x)}_{\le0}\ \overset{(3)}{\le}\ f(\bar x).$$

(1) $q$ is the infimum over all $x$, so it is $\le$ the value at the particular $\bar x$. (2) definition of $L$. (3) feasibility and $\bar\mu\ge0$. Taking the sup on the left and the inf on the right: $\boxed{d^*\le p^*}$.

With numbers (tutorial problem from D)

For that problem, minimising $L$ over $x$ (gradient zero gives $x_1=\frac{\mu-\lambda}{2}$, $x_2=-\lambda$; full algebra derived in K below) gives $q(\lambda,\mu)=-\frac14(\lambda-\mu)^2-\frac12\lambda^2-4\lambda+2\mu$. Then $q(0,0)=0$ and $q(-1,1)=-1-\frac12+4+2=4.5$. The feasible point $(3,1)$ has $f=9.5$. So without solving anything: $4.5\le d^*\le p^*\le9.5$. (The true value is $6$.)

Every dual value (blue) sits left of $d^*$; every feasible cost (red) sits right of $p^*$; and $d^*\le p^*$. The distance $p^*-d^*$ is the duality gap (section G).

Consequence: the optimality certificate. If a feasible $\bar x$ and dual-feasible prices have equal values, $f(\bar x)=q(\bar\lambda,\bar\mu)$, then both are optimal: nothing can lie strictly between them. This is how you prove an answer is optimal in LPs (section L).

Check F. A feasible $\hat x$ has $f(\hat x)=10$ and some prices with $\mu\ge0$ give $q=10$. What can you conclude? What if $q$ were $12$?

$10\le d^*\le p^*\le10$: both optimal, $p^*=d^*=10$. A value $q=12\gt f(\hat x)$ is impossible: it would break weak duality, so there's an algebra mistake (usually a sign error in $L$, or $\mu\lt0$).

G. Duality gap, strong duality, and Slater's condition

Definitions. The duality gap is $p^*-d^*\ge0$. Strong duality (or "zero gap") means $p^*=d^*$. Separately, each side may or may not be attained: "primal attained" = some feasible $x^*$ has $f(x^*)=p^*$; "dual attained" = some $(\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ with $\mu^*\ge0$ has $q(\lambda^*,\mu^*)=d^*$.

Plain words: four separate things can each be true or false: (1) zero gap; (2) primal attained; (3) dual attained; (4) the Lagrangian at the dual optimum has a unique minimiser. Each theorem below gives a specific subset. Exam MCQs test exactly which.

A gap can happen even for a convex problem. Example (worked in J5 below): $\min e^{-x}$ s.t. $x^2/y\le0$ on the domain $y\gt0$ has $p^*=1$ but $d^*=0$. It is convex: $e^{-x}$ is convex, and $x^2/y$ has Hessian $\begin{pmatrix}2/y&-2x/y^2\\-2x/y^2&2x^2/y^3\end{pmatrix}$, whose diagonal entries are $\ge0$ and whose determinant is $\frac{4x^2}{y^4}-\frac{4x^2}{y^4}=0$ on $y\gt0$, so it is PSD there. The most used guarantee against a gap:

Slater's theorem (Thm 7.4.3)

Assume: $f$ and all $g_j$ convex, $h$ affine, there is a Slater point $x^0$ with $h(x^0)=0$ and $g_j(x^0)\lt0$ for every $j$ (strictly inside every inequality), and $p^*$ is finite.

Then: $p^*=d^*$ and the dual optimum is attained (some $\mu^*\ge0$, $\lambda^*$ reach $d^*$).

Not given: that the primal optimum is attained; unique multipliers; a unique Lagrangian minimiser.

  • Finding a Slater point is usually guessing: tutorial problem $x_1+x_2=4$, $x_1\ge2$: $x^0=(3,1)$ gives $h=0$, $g=2-3=-1\lt0$ ✓. He said Slater points are "easy to guess".
  • Primal not attained under Slater: $\min e^x$ s.t. $x\le0$. Slater point $x=-1$. $p^*=\inf e^x=0$ but no $x$ reaches 0. (Dual: $q(0)=0$, attained.)
  • Slater as a constraint qualification: if Slater holds and a primal optimum $x^*$ exists (and everything is differentiable), then KKT multipliers exist at $x^*$ (combine Slater with section H). That's why Slater is "the CQ for convex problems".

H. KKT pair ⇔ zero gap and both optima attained (Thm 7.4.1)

Setting: differentiable convex problem ($f,g$ convex, $h$ affine), $x$ ranging over all of $\mathbb{R}^n$. A KKT pair is $(x^*,(\lambda^*,\mu^*))$ satisfying all five KKT conditions.

Theorem 7.4.1

A KKT pair exists $\iff$ $p^*=d^*\in\mathbb{R}$ and both are attained. In that case every primal optimum together with every dual optimum is a KKT pair.

Proof, both directions (it is only weak duality's chain read carefully)

(⇐) zero gap + both attained ⇒ KKT. Let $x^*$ be primal optimal and $(\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ dual optimal, with $q(\lambda^*,\mu^*)=f(x^*)$. Weak duality's chain at this pair reads

$$q(\lambda^*,\mu^*)\le L(x^*,\lambda^*,\mu^*)\le f(x^*),$$

and the two ends are equal, so both "$\le$" are "$=$".

  1. First equality: $L(x^*,\lambda^*,\mu^*)=\inf_xL(x,\lambda^*,\mu^*)$, so $x^*$ minimises $L(\cdot,\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ over all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, hence $\nabla_xL=0$ there: stationarity.
  2. Second equality: $(\mu^*)^Tg(x^*)=0$. Each term $\mu_j^*g_j(x^*)$ is $\le0$, and they add to 0, so each is 0: CS.
  3. Feasibility of $x^*$ and $\mu^*\ge0$ are given. All five conditions hold.

(⇒) KKT ⇒ zero gap + both attained. With $\mu^*\ge0$, $L(\cdot,\lambda^*,\mu^*)=f+\lambda^{*T}h+\mu^{*T}g$ is convex in $x$ (convex + affine + nonnegative multiples of convex). Stationarity says its gradient is 0 at $x^*$, so $x^*$ is a global minimiser of $L$ (first-order test). Hence

$$q(\lambda^*,\mu^*)=L(x^*,\lambda^*,\mu^*)=f(x^*)+\lambda^{*T}\underbrace{h(x^*)}_{0}+\underbrace{\mu^{*T}g(x^*)}_{0\text{ by CS}}=f(x^*).$$

By weak duality $q(\lambda^*,\mu^*)\le d^*\le p^*\le f(x^*)$, and the ends are equal: zero gap, $x^*$ attains $p^*$, $(\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ attains $d^*$. ∎

Why "full space" matters: step 1 of (⇐) used "minimiser over all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ ⇒ gradient 0". If $x$ is restricted to a domain like $y\gt0$, that step can fail (section J, last example).

I. Primal recovery: getting $x^*$ back from the dual

Plain words: once you know the optimal prices, minimise the bill $L(x,\lambda^*,\mu^*)$ over $x$. If there is exactly one minimiser, it is the answer. (That's what happened in E1: $x=\mu^*/2=1$.)

Corollary 7.4.2 (primal recovery)

Suppose a KKT pair exists, $\bar\eta=(\bar\lambda,\bar\mu)$ is dual optimal, and $L(\cdot,\bar\eta)$ has a unique minimiser $\bar x$. Then $\bar x$ is the unique primal optimum. Feasibility and CS come for free.

Proof (2 lines): by Thm 7.4.1, any primal optimum $x^*$ together with $\bar\eta$ is a KKT pair, so (as in H, ⇒) $x^*$ minimises $L(\cdot,\bar\eta)$. The minimiser is unique, so $x^*=\bar x$. ∎ (A primal optimum exists because a KKT pair exists.)

  • Hypothesis is uniqueness, not strict convexity. Strict convexity of $L(\cdot,\bar\eta)$ in $x$ (e.g. $f$ strictly convex) is just one convenient way to get uniqueness.
  • Without uniqueness it can fail: in $\min x$ s.t. $-x\le0$, $\mu^*=1$ and $L(x,1)=(1-1)x\equiv0$. Every $x$ minimises it, including $x=-1$ (infeasible) and $x=1$ (feasible but not optimal). Then use CS to pick out $x^*$ (here $\mu^*=1\gt0$ forces $-x=0$, so $x^*=0$).

L. LP duality from scratch

A linear program (LP) has a linear cost and linear constraints. Everything above applies, and it becomes very clean because the Lagrangian is linear in $x$: the rule from E2 ("coefficient of $x$ must vanish") does all the work.

L1. A tiny LP, every step

Sign convention first (used throughout L): an equality row $a_i^Tx=b_i$ can be written as $h=0$ in two ways, $a_i^Tx-b_i=0$ or $b_i-a_i^Tx=0$. Both are correct. In section L we always write $b_i-a_i^Tx$ (his derivation in Ch 7 §7.6), because then the dual comes out as "$\max b^T\lambda$". The other choice just flips the sign of that price. Pick one and don't switch mid-problem.

$\min x_1+2x_2$ s.t. $x_1+x_2=3$, $x_1\ge0$, $x_2\ge0$. (Obvious answer: put everything on the cheaper $x_1$: $x^*=(3,0)$, $p^*=3$.)

  1. Constraint forms: $h=3-x_1-x_2=0$ (price $\lambda$, free); $-x_1\le0$, $-x_2\le0$ (prices $\mu_1,\mu_2\ge0$).
  2. Lagrangian, collected by $x$: $L=x_1+2x_2+\lambda(3-x_1-x_2)-\mu_1x_1-\mu_2x_2=(1-\lambda-\mu_1)x_1+(2-\lambda-\mu_2)x_2+3\lambda$.
  3. Coefficients must vanish (else $q=-\infty$): $\mu_1=1-\lambda$, $\mu_2=2-\lambda$. Then $q=3\lambda$.
  4. Eliminate the sign prices: $\mu_1\ge0\iff\lambda\le1$; $\mu_2\ge0\iff\lambda\le2$.
  5. Dual: $\max 3\lambda$ s.t. $\lambda\le1$, $\lambda\le2$. So $\lambda^*=1$, $d^*=3=p^*$ ✓.
  6. Meaning of $\lambda^*$: if the right-hand side 3 became 4, the cost would rise by $1=\lambda^*$. The price of a constraint = how much the optimum would change per unit of its right-hand side.

L2. The standard pair, LP weak duality and LP KKT

As set in L1, the equality rows are written $h(x)=b-Ax$ (using $Ax-b$ would replace $\lambda$ by $-\lambda$ everywhere).

$\min c^Tx$ s.t. $Ax=b$, $x\ge0$. Prices: $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m$ (free) for $b-Ax=0$; $\mu\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $\mu\ge0$ for $-x\le0$.

$$L=c^Tx+\lambda^T(b-Ax)-\mu^Tx=(c-A^T\lambda-\mu)^Tx+b^T\lambda.$$

(We used $\lambda^TAx=(A^T\lambda)^Tx$.) Linear in $x$, so by E2:

$$q(\lambda,\mu)=\begin{cases}b^T\lambda,& A^T\lambda+\mu=c\\-\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Eliminate $\mu=c-A^T\lambda$; $\mu\ge0\iff A^T\lambda\le c$. Hence the pair he said is worth memorizing:

Standard LP pair $$\text{(P)}\ \ \min_x c^Tx\ \text{ s.t. } Ax=b,\ x\ge0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\text{(D)}\ \ \max_\lambda b^T\lambda\ \text{ s.t. } A^T\lambda\le c\quad(\lambda\text{ free}).$$

LP weak duality in one line: for feasible $x$ and $\lambda$, $c^Tx-b^T\lambda=c^Tx-\lambda^TAx=(c-A^T\lambda)^Tx=\mu^Tx\ge0$.

LP KKT (You have to remember it):

$$A^T\lambda^*+\mu^*=c,\quad \mu^*\ge0,\quad Ax^*=b,\quad x^*\ge0,\quad (\mu^*)^Tx^*=0.$$

Where the first equation comes from: it is the general stationarity $\nabla f+J_h\lambda+J_g\mu=0$ with $f=c^Tx$ ($\nabla f=c$), $h=b-Ax$ ($J_h=-A^T$) and $g=-x$ ($J_g=-I$): $c-A^T\lambda-\mu=0$. Why no LICQ is needed: in Ch 6, LICQ was there to make sure the linearised constraints describe the feasible directions correctly. When the constraints are already linear, their linearisation is the constraints themselves, so this is automatic: every LP minimum has KKT multipliers.

Value identity: $c^Tx^*=(A^T\lambda^*+\mu^*)^Tx^*=b^T\lambda^*+0$.

Complementary slackness in LP words. $(\mu^*)^Tx^*=\sum_j\mu_j^*x_j^*=0$ with every term $\ge0$, so for each $j$: $x_j^*\gt0\Rightarrow\mu_j^*=0$, i.e. dual constraint $j$ is tight ($(A^T\lambda^*)_j=c_j$); and dual constraint $j$ slack ($\mu_j^*\gt0$) $\Rightarrow x_j^*=0$. This is how you get one solution from the other.

Method: don't memorise many duals. Run the same recipe: rewrite each constraint, write $L$, collect the coefficient of each $x_j$, set it to zero, eliminate the sign prices. Three patterns:

  • Free variable $x_j$ (no sign constraint, so no sign price): its coefficient in $L$ must be exactly 0 ⇒ an equality in the dual.
  • $x_j\ge0$: adds $-\mu_jx_j$; eliminating $\mu_j\ge0$ gives "$\le$" ⇒ an inequality $(A^T\lambda)_j\le c_j$. ($x_j\le0$ adds $+\mu_jx_j$ and gives "$\ge$".)
  • Inequality row: rewrite as $(\cdot)\le0$; its price is $\ge0$. Equality row: price free.

Row-price convention for the table (stated explicitly): we write every row's term as $\lambda_i(b_i-a_i^Tx)$, so all row prices share one letter.

  • $=$ row: $b_i-a_i^Tx=0$ is an $h$, so $\lambda_i$ free.
  • $\ge$ row ($a_i^Tx\ge b_i$): $b_i-a_i^Tx\le0$ is a $g$, so $\lambda_i\ge0$ (it is the course's $\mu$ for that row; we only keep the letter $\lambda$).
  • $\le$ row ($a_i^Tx\le b_i$): the course writes $a_i^Tx-b_i\le0$ with a price $\ge0$; writing it as $\lambda_i(b_i-a_i^Tx)$ means $\lambda_i=-(\text{that price})\le0$.

Then for every form, $L=(c-A^T\lambda)^Tx+b^T\lambda+(\text{sign terms})$ and the dual is $\max b^T\lambda$ with:

Worked example: mixed form (a ≥ row, an = row, a free variable)

$\min 2x_1+3x_2$ s.t. $x_1+x_2\ge4$, $x_1-x_2=1$, $x_1\ge0$, $x_2$ free.

  1. Rows (prices multiply $b_i-a_i^Tx$): $\ge$ row → $\lambda_1(4-x_1-x_2)$, $\lambda_1\ge0$; $=$ row → $\lambda_2(1-x_1+x_2)$, $\lambda_2$ free. Signs: $-x_1\le0$ → $-\mu_1x_1$, $\mu_1\ge0$; $x_2$ free → no term.
  2. $L=2x_1+3x_2+\lambda_1(4-x_1-x_2)+\lambda_2(1-x_1+x_2)-\mu_1x_1=(2-\lambda_1-\lambda_2-\mu_1)x_1+(3-\lambda_1+\lambda_2)x_2+4\lambda_1+\lambda_2$.
  3. Coefficients vanish: $\mu_1=2-\lambda_1-\lambda_2\ge0$ (so $\lambda_1+\lambda_2\le2$) and $3-\lambda_1+\lambda_2=0$ (so $\lambda_1-\lambda_2=3$, an equality because $x_2$ is free).
  4. Dual: $\max 4\lambda_1+\lambda_2$ s.t. $\lambda_1+\lambda_2\le2$, $\lambda_1-\lambda_2=3$, $\lambda_1\ge0$, $\lambda_2$ free. (Tables 1–2 agree.)

Worked example: inequality form, $x$ free

$\min c^Tx$ s.t. $Ax\le b$, $x$ free. Rows are $\le$, so prices $\lambda\le0$: $L=c^Tx+\lambda^T(b-Ax)=(c-A^T\lambda)^Tx+b^T\lambda$. $x$ free ⇒ $A^T\lambda=c$. Dual: $\max b^T\lambda$ s.t. $A^T\lambda=c$, $\lambda\le0$.

(In plain course form with $\mu\ge0$ for $Ax-b\le0$: $L=(c+A^T\mu)^Tx-b^T\mu$, dual $\max -b^T\mu$ s.t. $A^T\mu=-c$, $\mu\ge0$. Same thing with $\mu=-\lambda$.)

Max problems

Convert first: $\max c^Tx=-\min(-c^Tx)$. Derive the dual of the min, then flip back. For $\max c^Tx$ s.t. $Ax\le b$, $x\ge0$: the min problem $\min(-c^Tx)$ has (with $w\ge0$ the course price of $Ax-b\le0$) the dual $\max(-b^Tw)$ s.t. $A^Tw\ge c$, $w\ge0$. Flip back using $-\max(-b^Tw)=\min b^Tw$: the max primal's dual is $\min b^Tw$ s.t. $A^Tw\ge c$, $w\ge0$, and both optimal values equal $\max c^Tx$ (Q15 does every step).

Worked example: the self-dual LP (Midterm 2 Q II, summary; letters as on the paper)

Here $q\in\mathbb{R}^N$ is a given vector (not the dual function) and $M$ is a given matrix with $M^T=-M$. $\min q^Tz$ s.t. $Mz\ge-q$, $z\ge0$. L4. The LP duality theorem

LP duality theorem

(i) If (P) or (D) has an optimal solution, so does the other, and the optimal values are equal (no gap, no Slater needed).

(ii) If one is unbounded (its value is $-\infty$ for a min, $+\infty$ for a max), the other is infeasible. (Reason: by weak duality any feasible point of the other side would be a finite bound.)

The converse of (ii) is FALSE. Being d infeasible doesn't imply p unbounded. Both can be infeasible.

Attainment fact (used below and in the self-dual argument): a feasible LP whose cost is bounded below (for a min) always attains its minimum. Unlike $\min e^x$ (J2), an LP can't creep towards a value without reaching it. So for an LP, "feasible and not unbounded" = "has an optimal solution".

Worked counter-example: both infeasible

$A=[0]$, $b=1$, $c=-1$ (one variable). (P): $\min -x$ s.t. $0\cdot x=1$, $x\ge0$: no $x$ satisfies $0=1$, infeasible. (D): $\max\lambda$ s.t. $0\cdot\lambda\le-1$: $0\le-1$ is false, infeasible.

So for an LP pair exactly one of these happens: both optimal with equal values; one unbounded and the other infeasible; both infeasible.

Check L4. (P) is a min LP and has a feasible point with cost 7; (D) is infeasible. What is (P)?
Show answer

(P) is feasible, so by (i) it cannot have an optimum (else (D) would too). A feasible LP without an optimum is unbounded: $p^*=-\infty$. (Infeasible (D) alone would not have told you this; you needed (P) feasible.)

M. The exam recipe

Recipe for "derive the dual" (write every step in the exam)
  1. Rewrite constraints as $h(x)=0$, $g(x)\le0$ (e.g. $x_1\ge2\to 2-x_1\le0$; $x\ge0\to -x\le0$). Name multipliers: $\lambda$ free for $h$, $\mu\ge0$ for $g$. Max problem? Convert to min first.
  2. Write $L=f+\lambda^Th+\mu^Tg$ and collect terms in $x$.
  3. Take $\inf$ over all $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ (no constraints put back). Linear parts: coefficient $=0$. Quadratic parts: $\nabla_xL=0$.
  4. Write $q$ with its domain $\mathbb{D}$ (where it is $\gt-\infty$).
  5. Eliminate "slack" multipliers (those that appear only in the coefficient conditions, e.g. $\mu$ for $x\ge0$): $\mu=\ldots\ge0$ becomes an inequality on the rest.
  6. Dual: maximise $q$ over the remaining multipliers with their sign restrictions. LP: check against the conversion table.
  7. (QP) Maximise; check $\mu^*\ge0$ (if the unconstrained maximiser has $\mu\lt0$, maximise on the boundary $\mu=0$). Recover $\bar x=\arg\min_xL(x,\lambda^*,\mu^*)$; if unique it is $x^*$. Check $p^*=d^*$. Name a Slater point.

Summary card (for revision; the formula sheet copies this)

Memorise cold

Symbols: $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ variable; $f$ cost; $h(x)=0$ equalities with prices $\lambda$ (free); $g(x)\le0$ inequalities with prices $\mu\ge0$; $p^*$ / $d^*$ = primal / dual optimal values; LP data $c\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^m$, $a_i^T$ = row $i$ of $A$.

  • Primal (always rewrite into this form first): $\min_x f(x)$ s.t. $h(x)=0$, $g(x)\le 0$. Lagrangian $L(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\lambda^Th(x)+\mu^Tg(x)$, $\lambda$ free, $\mu\ge 0$. (The notes write the multipliers as $y=(u,v)$, Quiz 4 as $\eta$; same thing.)
  • Dual function $q(\lambda,\mu)=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^n}L(x,\lambda,\mu)$: infimum over all $x$, constraints are not put back. Domain $\mathbb{D}=\{(\lambda,\mu):q\gt-\infty\}$. Dual problem: $\max q(\lambda,\mu)$ s.t. $(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{D}$, $\mu\ge0$.
  • Linear-in-$x$ rule: $\inf_x (r^Tx+\beta)=\beta$ if $r=0$, and $-\infty$ otherwise. ("The infimum is finite if and only if the coefficient of $x$ vanishes.")
  • $q$ is concave and $\mathbb{D}$ is convex, even when the primal is nonconvex (because $L$ is affine in the multipliers).
  • Weak duality: $x$ feasible, $\mu\ge0$ ⇒ $q(\lambda,\mu)\le L(x,\lambda,\mu)\le f(x)$. Hence $d^*\le p^*$. Equal values for a feasible pair ⇒ both optimal.
  • Standard LP pair: $\ \min c^Tx$ s.t. $Ax=b,\ x\ge0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \max b^T\lambda$ s.t. $A^T\lambda\le c$ ($\lambda$ free; derived with $h=b-Ax$).
  • LP KKT: $A^T\lambda^*+\mu^*=c,\ \ \mu^*\ge0,\ \ Ax^*=b,\ \ x^*\ge0,\ \ (\mu^*)^Tx^*=0$. Value identity $c^Tx^*=b^T\lambda^*$.
  • LP duality theorem: (i) one has an optimal solution ⇒ both do, equal values. (ii) one unbounded ⇒ the other infeasible. The converse of (ii) is FALSE: both can be infeasible ($A=[0]$, $b=1$, $c=-1$).
  • KKT-equivalence (Thm 7.4.1), differentiable convex, full space: a KKT pair exists ⇔ $p^*=d^*\in\mathbb{R}$ and both are attained. Then every primal optimum with every dual optimum is a KKT pair.
  • Slater (Thm 7.4.3): $f,g$ convex, $h$ affine, some $x^0$ with $h(x^0)=0$, $g(x^0)\lt 0$, and $p^*$ finite ⇒ $p^*=d^*$ and the dual optimum is attained. Primal attainment is NOT guaranteed ($e^x$, $x\le0$).
  • Primal recovery (Cor 7.4.2): KKT pair exists, $\bar\eta=(\bar\lambda,\bar\mu)$ dual optimal, $L(\cdot,\bar\eta)$ has a unique minimiser $\bar x$ ⇒ $\bar x$ is the unique primal optimum (feasibility and complementary slackness come free). Uniqueness, not strict convexity, is the hypothesis.
  • Convex problems: local min = global min; minimiser set convex; KKT ⇒ global min (no CQ); KKT not necessary without a CQ ($(x-1)^2$, $x^2\le0$).
  • Convex feasible set from: $h(x)=0$ with $h$ affine; $g(x)\le0$ with $g$ convex; $g(x)\ge0$ with $g$ concave.
  • Never write "concave optimization problem" or "concave set".
