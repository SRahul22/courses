  • $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_m)$: multipliers of the equalities (any sign). $\mu=(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_p)$: multipliers of the inequalities (must be $\ge0$).
  • $\ell(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\sum_i\lambda_ih_i(x)+\sum_j\mu_jg_j(x)$: the Lagrangian. (Midterm 2 wrote $L$ for the same thing.) $\mathbf L_\star=\nabla^2_{xx}\ell(x^\star,\lambda^\star,\mu^\star)$: its Hessian in $x$ at the KKT point, multipliers held fixed.

0. Why this topic exists (the problem it solves)

In Chapter 1 you learnt: at a minimum of a smooth function with no constraints, the gradient is zero, $\nabla f(x^\star)=0$. Real problems have constraints: a budget, a capacity, "this quantity can't be negative".

A concrete problem. Find the point closest to $(2,2)$ among the points with $x_1+x_2\le2$. As a minimisation: $\min\ f(x)=(x_1-2)^2+(x_2-2)^2$ subject to $x_1+x_2\le2$.

  • Without the constraint the answer is $(2,2)$, where $\nabla f=0$. But $2+2=4\gt2$: that point is not allowed.
  • The best allowed point is $(1,1)$, on the boundary line (the figure below shows it). There $\nabla f(1,1)=\big(2(1-2),\,2(1-2)\big)=(-2,-2)\ne0$.

The pain: the test "$\nabla f=0$" fails at the real answer. We need a new test for constrained minima. That test is KKT, and this whole chapter builds it.

Dashed circles are level sets of $f$ (points of equal distance to $(2,2)$). The best feasible point $x^\star=(1,1)$ is where a level circle just touches the line. There, the downhill direction $-\nabla f$ (red) points straight out through the wall, the same way as the wall's outward normal $\nabla g$ (green) for $g=x_1+x_2-2$: $-\nabla f=2\,\nabla g$.
The whole chapter in one sentence

At a constrained minimum, every direction you are allowed to move in goes uphill (or flat); equivalently, the downhill direction $-\nabla f$ is "held back" by the walls: $-\nabla f$ equals a combination of the active constraints' gradients, with non-negative weights on the inequality walls. Those weights are the multipliers $\mu$ (here $\mu=2$).

A. The set-up: the course form, active constraints, and why ∇f = 0 fails

A1. The course form (he accepts no other)

Every problem is first rewritten in exactly this form:

$$\min_{x\in\mathbb R^n} f(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad h_i(x)=0\ \ (i=1,\dots,m),\qquad g_j(x)\le0\ \ (j=1,\dots,p).$$

The feasible set is $\Omega=\{x:\ h_i(x)=0\text{ for all }i,\ g_j(x)\le0\text{ for all }j\}$. A point $x^\star\in\Omega$ is a local minimiser if $f(x^\star)\le f(x)$ for every feasible $x$ near $x^\star$ (strict: $\lt$ for every such $x\ne x^\star$).

Rewriting rules:

  • A maximisation $\max F$ becomes $\min f$ with $f=-F$.
  • "$\ge$" constraints are flipped by moving everything to one side: $x_1\ge0$ becomes $-x_1\le0$; $x^\top Qx\ge1$ becomes $1-x^\top Qx\le0$; $x_1+x_2\ge1$ becomes $1-x_1-x_2\le0$.
  • Equalities go to "$\dots=0$": $x_1^2+x_2^2=4$ becomes $x_1^2+x_2^2-4=0$.
Worked rewrite Problem: $\max\ x_1x_2$ s.t. $x_1+x_2\ge1$, $x_1^2+x_2^2=4$, $x_2\le3$.
  1. Max → min: $f(x)=-x_1x_2$.
  2. Equality: $h(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2-4=0$.
  3. Inequalities: $g_1(x)=1-x_1-x_2\le0$, $g_2(x)=x_2-3\le0$.
Trap If you keep $x_1+x_2-1\ge0$ and later use the KKT signs for "$\le0$", every inequality multiplier comes out with the wrong sign and you will reject the true answer. He said other books' forms are not accepted Ch 7 lec 2 [41:00]. Always rewrite first.

A2. Active and inactive constraints

At a feasible point $x^\star$, an inequality $g_j\le0$ is either

  • active: $g_j(x^\star)=0$. You are standing on that wall; a small move can break it.
  • inactive: $g_j(x^\star)\lt0$. You are strictly inside; any small enough move keeps it satisfied, so near $x^\star$ it does not matter at all.

The active set is $\mathcal A(x^\star)=\{j:\ g_j(x^\star)=0\}$. Equalities $h_i=0$ always count as "on the wall".

Numbers: with $g_1=x_1+x_2-2$, $g_2=-x_1$: at $(1,1)$: $g_1=0$ (active), $g_2=-1$ (inactive), so $\mathcal A=\{1\}$. At $(0,2)$: $g_1=0$, $g_2=0$, so $\mathcal A=\{1,2\}$ (a corner).

A3. Why "∇f = 0" is the wrong test with constraints

Recall what $\nabla f$ tells you: moving a small step $t$ in direction $d$ changes $f$ by about $t\,\nabla f(x)^\top d$ (first-order Taylor). So $f$ goes down in direction $d$ exactly when $\nabla f^\top d\lt0$.

  • No constraints: you may move in every direction. If $\nabla f\ne0$, the direction $d=-\nabla f$ gives $\nabla f^\top d=-\|\nabla f\|^2\lt0$: downhill. So a minimum needs $\nabla f=0$.
  • With constraints: you may only move in allowed directions. At $(1,1)$ in section 0, the downhill direction $d=-\nabla f=(2,2)$ leaves the feasible set (it increases $x_1+x_2$). Every direction that stays feasible (with $d_1+d_2\le0$) has $\nabla f^\top d=-2(d_1+d_2)\ge0$: not downhill. So $(1,1)$ is a minimum although $\nabla f\ne0$.

So the correct condition must be: "$\nabla f(x^\star)^\top d\ge0$ for every allowed direction $d$". The next section makes "allowed direction" precise. It is subtler than it looks, because on a curved constraint no straight line stays feasible.

B. Which directions are you allowed to move in? Two cones

B1. Straight rays fail on curved constraints

First idea: call $d$ allowed if the straight ray $x^\star+td$ stays feasible for all small $t\gt0$ (a "feasible direction"). This works for straight-line constraints, but not for curved ones.

Example: $\Omega=\{(u,v):\ v=u^2\}$, a parabola, at the origin. Which straight rays stay on it? The point $(t\,d_1,t\,d_2)$ is on the parabola only if $t\,d_2=t^2d_1^2$ for all small $t$, which forces $d=0$. So no direction is a feasible direction, yet you can obviously move along the curve. The feasible-direction idea throws away everything.

Better idea: allow curves. Walk along feasible points $x_k=(t_k,t_k^2)$ towards the origin. The chord from the origin to $x_k$, scaled by $1/t_k$, is $(1,t_k)$, which tends to $(1,0)$ as $t_k\to0$. So $(1,0)$ is the direction from which feasible points approach the origin. That's a tangent direction.

Blue: the feasible set $v=u^2$. Dashed chords from the origin to feasible points $x_k$ flatten towards the horizontal as $x_k$ gets closer, so the limiting direction is $d=(1,0)$ (green). The straight ray $(t,0)$ itself (red ×) leaves the set immediately. A tangent direction is a limit of directions to feasible points, not a direction you can travel in a straight line.

B2. The tangent cone $T_\Omega(x^\star)$: the honest set of directions

In plain words: $d$ is a tangent direction if there are feasible points approaching $x^\star$ "from direction $d$".

Precisely (Def. 6.1.2.1): $d\in T_\Omega(x^\star)$ if there are numbers $t_k\gt0$, $t_k\downarrow0$, and vectors $d_k\to d$ with

$$x^\star+t_kd_k\in\Omega\quad\text{for every }k.$$

Read it as: $x_k=x^\star+t_kd_k$ are feasible points; $t_k$ is how far away they are (roughly), $d_k=(x_k-x^\star)/t_k$ is the scaled direction to them, and these directions settle down to $d$. The $d_k$ need not have length 1. In the parabola example, $t_k$ is the $u$-value and $d_k=(1,t_k)\to(1,0)$.

  • $d=0$ is always in $T_\Omega$ (take $x_k=x^\star$). If $d\in T_\Omega$ then so is $s\,d$ for $s\gt0$ (rescale $t_k$). So $T_\Omega$ is a cone.
  • For the parabola at the origin, $T_\Omega=\{(d_1,0)\}$: the whole horizontal line (approach from the left with $u\lt0$ too: take feasible points $x_k=(-s_k,s_k^2)$ with $s_k\downarrow0$ and $t_k=|u_k|=s_k\gt0$; then $d_k=(x_k-0)/t_k=(-1,s_k)\to(-1,0)$, so $(-1,0)\in T_\Omega$).
  • If $x^\star$ is an interior point (no active constraints, no equalities), $T_\Omega=\mathbb R^n$.

The problem with $T_\Omega$: it's defined by limits of sequences of points. You can't compute it by a formula. So we look for a formula-based replacement.

B3. The linearised cone $\mathcal L_\Omega(x^\star)$: what the gradients claim

In plain words: replace each constraint by its first-order (linear) approximation at $x^\star$, and ask which directions keep the linear versions satisfied.

  • Equality $h_i$: to first order $h_i(x^\star+td)\approx h_i(x^\star)+t\,\nabla h_i(x^\star)^\top d=t\,\nabla h_i^\top d$. To stay at 0 we need $\nabla h_i(x^\star)^\top d=0$ (move along the surface, not across it).
  • Active inequality ($g_j(x^\star)=0$): $g_j(x^\star+td)\approx t\,\nabla g_j^\top d$. To stay $\le0$ we need $\nabla g_j(x^\star)^\top d\le0$ (don't move outward through the wall). $\nabla g_j$ is the wall's outward normal: it points to where $g_j$ increases, i.e. out of the feasible side.
  • Inactive inequalities: no condition (small moves keep them strictly negative).

Precisely (Def. 6.1.3.1):

$$\mathcal L_\Omega(x^\star)=\{d:\ \nabla h_i(x^\star)^\top d=0\ \ \forall i,\qquad \nabla g_j(x^\star)^\top d\le0\ \ \forall j\in\mathcal A(x^\star)\}.$$

This is a finite list of linear conditions, so you can always write it down. It is a closed convex cone. (A set is convex if the straight segment between any two of its points stays inside it; closed if it contains the limits of its convergent sequences, e.g. it is cut out by "$=$" and "$\le$", never "$\lt$".)

Numbers: parabola $h(u,v)=v-u^2$: $\nabla h=(-2u,1)=(0,1)$ at the origin, so $\mathcal L=\{d:d_2=0\}$, the horizontal line. Same as $T_\Omega$. Good, here the gradients told the truth.

Show answer

B4. Why $T_\Omega\subseteq\mathcal L_\Omega$ always (the gradients can only over-promise)

Take a tangent direction $d$: feasible points $x_k=x^\star+t_kd_k$ with $t_k\downarrow0$, $d_k\to d$.

  1. Equality $h_i$: $0=h_i(x_k)=h_i(x^\star)+t_k\nabla h_i(x^\star)^\top d_k+o(t_k)$ (Taylor; $o(t_k)$ means "a remainder that, divided by $t_k$, goes to 0").
  2. $h_i(x^\star)=0$, so $0=t_k\nabla h_i^\top d_k+o(t_k)$. Divide by $t_k\gt0$: $0=\nabla h_i^\top d_k+o(1)$.
  3. Let $k\to\infty$: $\nabla h_i(x^\star)^\top d=0$.
  4. Active $g_j$: same steps with "$\le$": $0\ge g_j(x_k)=0+t_k\nabla g_j^\top d_k+o(t_k)$ ⇒ $\nabla g_j(x^\star)^\top d\le0$.

So every tangent direction passes the linearised tests: $\boxed{T_\Omega(x^\star)\subseteq\mathcal L_\Omega(x^\star)}$ (Theorem 6.1.1, needs only $C^1$ constraints, i.e. continuously differentiable). The reverse can fail: the linear test can accept directions that no feasible points come from, when a gradient is zero or the gradients are "degenerate".

Blue: the true feasible set, which here is also the tangent cone. Orange shading: the linearised cone. In both cases the constraint gradient is zero at the origin, so the linear test accepts every direction and $T_\Omega\subsetneq\mathcal L_\Omega$. Note $v^3=0$ is the same set as $v=0$, but $v=0$ has $\nabla h=(0,1)$ and gives $\mathcal L=$ the $u$-axis $=T$. Whether $T=\mathcal L$ depends on how the constraint is written.

Also note: for $uv=0$, $T_\Omega$ is the union of two lines. It contains $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ but not their sum $(1,1)$, so it is not convex and not a subspace. $\mathcal L_\Omega$ is always convex.

B5. LICQ: the condition that makes the gradients honest

In plain words: the gradients that matter at $x^\star$ (all equalities and the active inequalities) point in genuinely different directions: none of them is zero and none is a combination of the others. Then the linear picture is faithful and $T_\Omega=\mathcal L_\Omega$.

Precisely (Def. 6.1.4.1): $x^\star$ is a regular point (LICQ holds) if

$$\{\nabla h_i(x^\star)\}_{i=1}^m\ \cup\ \{\nabla g_j(x^\star)\}_{j\in\mathcal A(x^\star)}\ \text{ is linearly independent.}$$

Theorem 6.1.1: if $x^\star$ is regular, then $T_\Omega(x^\star)=\mathcal L_\Omega(x^\star)$. (Why, in one line: with independent gradients you can bend any straight ray $x^\star+td$, $d\in\mathcal L$, by a tiny correction so it stays on the constraints; this uses the implicit function theorem, and the proof isn't examined.)

How to check it: put the relevant gradients as columns of a matrix; LICQ ⇔ that matrix has full column rank. With two gradients in 2-D: determinant $\ne0$. With one gradient: it is nonzero. With no active constraints and no equalities: LICQ holds automatically ("vacuously").

The Midterm 2 III problem (used again in D3, E5 and Practice D) $$\min f(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g_1=4-x_1-x_2^2\le0,\quad g_2=3x_2-x_1\le0,\quad g_3=-3x_2-x_1\le0.$$

In words: stay to the right of the parabola $x_1=4-x_2^2$ and inside the wedge $x_1\ge3|x_2|$; find the feasible point nearest the origin. Its KKT points (found in Practice D) are $(4,0)$ with $\mu=(8,0,0)$, and the corners $(3,\pm1)$ with $\mu=(4,2,0)$ resp. $(4,0,2)$.

Worked example (Midterm 2 III at the corner (3,1)) Active: $g_1=4-x_1-x_2^2$ and $g_2=3x_2-x_1$. $\nabla g_1=(-1,-2x_2)=(-1,-2)$, $\nabla g_2=(-1,3)$. $\det\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\-2&3\end{pmatrix}=(-1)(3)-(-1)(-2)=-3-2=-5\ne0$ ⇒ LICQ holds.
  • Another way to get $T=\mathcal L$: if all constraints are linear (affine), then $T=\mathcal L$ even if LICQ fails. E.g. $v=0$ and $2v=0$ together: dependent gradients $(0,1),(0,2)$, but both cones are the $u$-axis. So LICQ is sufficient, not necessary.
  • LICQ is a property of the representation: $v=0$ is regular at the origin, $v^3=0$ and $v^2=0$ are not.

C. The first-order condition, and how Farkas turns it into multipliers

C1. At a minimum, no tangent direction goes downhill

Plain words: if some tangent direction $d$ had $\nabla f^\top d\lt0$, the feasible points approaching from $d$ would have smaller $f$ than $x^\star$ (for points close enough). So at a local minimum that can't happen.

Theorem 6.2.1 (fundamental FONC: no constraint qualification needed)

If $x^\star$ is a local minimiser of $f$ over $\Omega$ and $f$ is differentiable at $x^\star$, then

$$\nabla f(x^\star)^\top d\ \ge\ 0\qquad\text{for every }d\in T_\Omega(x^\star).$$

This is the only theorem he proves in the chapter; the proof is in section H. It's correct but not usable yet, because $T_\Omega$ is hard to compute and there are infinitely many $d$ to check.

Worked example (Quiz 3 Q12: linear constraints, so T = 𝓛) $f=u+2v$ on $u+v+w=0$, $u\ge0$, $v\le1$. Is the origin a local min?
  1. Course form: $h=u+v+w$, $g_1=-u\le0$ (active at 0), $g_2=v-1\le0$ (value $-1$: inactive).
  2. Linear constraints ⇒ $T=\mathcal L=\{d=(d_1,d_2,d_3):\ d_1+d_2+d_3=0,\ -d_1\le0\}$.
  3. $\nabla f=(1,2,0)$. Try $d=(0,-1,1)$: $0-1+1=0$ ✓ and $d_1=0\ge0$ ✓, so $d\in T$. $\nabla f^\top d=0-2+0=-2\lt0$.
  4. A downhill tangent direction exists ⇒ FONC fails ⇒ the origin is not a local min.
  5. Careful: $(-1,0,1)$ also gives $\nabla f^\top d=-1$, but it is not tangent ($d_1=-1\lt0$ breaks $u\ge0$).

The plan from here: (1) at a regular point, replace $T$ by $\mathcal L$, which is a list of linear inequalities; (2) use Farkas' lemma to replace "for every $d$" by "there exist a few numbers". Those numbers are the multipliers.

C2. Farkas' lemma: from "every direction" to "a few numbers"

The picture first. Take a corner where two walls are active: $g_1=x_1-1\le0$ and $g_2=x_2-1\le0$ at $x^\star=(1,1)$, and $f=(x_1-2)^2+(x_2-2)^2$. The outward normals are $\nabla g_1=(1,0)$ and $\nabla g_2=(0,1)$. Allowed directions ($\mathcal L$) satisfy $d_1\le0$, $d_2\le0$: they point back into the box.

Blue: the feasible box near the corner $x^\star=(1,1)$. Green: the active walls' outward normals and the cone they span (all non-negative combinations). Red solid: $-\nabla f=(2,2)=2\nabla g_1+2\nabla g_2$ lies inside the cone, so $\mu=(2,2)\ge0$ exists and $x^\star$ is a KKT point. Red dashed: if $-\nabla f$ were $(1,-2)$, it would need $\mu_2=-2\lt0$; then the allowed direction $d=(0,-1)$ (slide down along the wall $x_1=1$) has $\nabla f^\top d=-2\lt0$: downhill.

What the picture says: "no allowed direction goes downhill" is the same as "the downhill arrow $-\nabla f$ lies in the cone of the active outward normals", i.e. $-\nabla f=\sum\mu_j\nabla g_j$ with all $\mu_j\ge0$. If $-\nabla f$ pokes out of that cone, you can find an allowed direction making an acute angle with it, and that direction is downhill.

Why the weights on equalities have no sign: an equality $h=0$ is like two walls, $h\le0$ and $-h\le0$, with normals $\nabla h$ and $-\nabla h$. Non-negative weights on both give any real weight on $\nabla h$. So $\lambda$ is free.

Lemma 6.3.1 (Farkas, course form)

Let $A$ be an $n\times m$ matrix, $B$ an $n\times r$ matrix, $c\in\mathbb R^n$. These two statements are equivalent:

  • Directional form: $d^\top c\ge0$ for every $d$ with $A^\top d=0$ and $B^\top d\le0$.
  • Multiplier form: there exist $\lambda\in\mathbb R^m$ (no sign restriction) and $\mu\in\mathbb R^r$, $\mu\ge0$, with $c+A\lambda+B\mu=0$.

No rank assumption on $A$ or $B$.

The easy direction, by hand (multiplier form ⇒ directional form): suppose $c=-A\lambda-B\mu$ with $\mu\ge0$. Take any $d$ with $A^\top d=0$, $B^\top d\le0$. Then

$$d^\top c=-(A^\top d)^\top\lambda-(B^\top d)^\top\mu=-0-\underbrace{(B^\top d)^\top\mu}_{\le0\ \text{(each term: }\le0\text{ times }\ge0)}\ \ge0.$$

The other direction is the hard part of the lemma (it uses a separating-hyperplane argument); he doesn't ask for it. Know the statement and the signs.

Check C2. In Farkas, which sign does $\mu$ get if the directional form says $B^\top d\ge0$ instead of $\le0$?
Show answer

$\mu\le0$ (flip $B\to-B$). That is exactly what happens if you write $g\ge0$ constraints and keep "$+\mu g$": the multiplier signs flip. Hence the rule: always $g\le0$.

C3. Putting it together: the equation worth 70% of the marks

Chain of reasoning (Theorem 6.3.2), at a local minimiser $x^\star$ that is regular:

  1. Local min ⇒ $\nabla f(x^\star)^\top d\ge0$ for all $d\in T_\Omega$ (C1, no assumption).
  2. Regular ⇒ $T_\Omega=\mathcal L_\Omega$, so $\nabla f^\top d\ge0$ for all $d$ with $\nabla h_i^\top d=0$, $\nabla g_j^\top d\le0$ ($j$ active) (B5).

Active-set first-order condition ("this equation") $$\nabla f(x^\star)+\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i\nabla h_i(x^\star)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal A(x^\star)}\mu_j\nabla g_j(x^\star)=0,\qquad \mu_j\ge0\ \ (j\in\mathcal A).$$

D. The KKT conditions, and how to solve them by hand

D1. The five parts

The trick that hides the active set: give every inequality a multiplier $\mu_j\ge0$, and demand $\mu_j g_j(x^\star)=0$ for every $j$. That product condition (complementary slackness) says, in plain words:

  • If constraint $j$ is inactive ($g_j\lt0$), its multiplier must be $\mu_j=0$: a wall you aren't touching can't push you.
  • If $\mu_j\gt0$ (the wall is pushing), then $g_j=0$: you must be on that wall.
  • Active with $\mu_j=0$ is allowed: you're on the wall but it isn't pushing (the minimum would be there even without that wall).

So $\sum_{j=1}^p\mu_j\nabla g_j$ over all $j$ equals the active-only sum from C3. With the Lagrangian $\ell(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\sum_i\lambda_ih_i(x)+\sum_j\mu_jg_j(x)$ (plus signs everywhere), the first condition is just "$\nabla_x\ell=0$".

Theorem 6.3.3 (KKT necessary conditions)

If $f,h_i,g_j$ are $C^1$ near a local minimiser $x^\star$ and $x^\star$ is regular, then there exist $\lambda^\star\in\mathbb R^m$, $\mu^\star\in\mathbb R^p$ with:

  1. Stationarity: $\nabla f(x^\star)+\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i^\star\nabla h_i(x^\star)+\sum_{j=1}^p\mu_j^\star\nabla g_j(x^\star)=0$, i.e. $\nabla_x\ell=0$.
  2. Primal feasibility (equalities): $h_i(x^\star)=0$ for all $i$.
  3. Primal feasibility (inequalities): $g_j(x^\star)\le0$ for all $j$.
  4. Dual feasibility: $\mu_j^\star\ge0$ for all $j$. ($\lambda^\star$: no sign restriction.)
  5. Complementarity: $\mu_j^\star g_j(x^\star)=0$ for all $j$.

A point $x^\star$ with such multipliers is a KKT point. Under LICQ the multipliers are unique (the gradients are independent, so the combination is unique). That says nothing about the minimiser being unique.

Useful fact: since every product $\mu_jg_j$ is $\le0$ (a $\ge0$ number times a $\le0$ number), the single equation $\sum_j\mu_jg_j=0$ already forces each product to be 0; nothing can cancel.

Check D1. At a KKT point, which of these must be true? (i) $g_j\lt0\Rightarrow\mu_j=0$; (ii) $g_j=0\Rightarrow\mu_j\gt0$; (iii) $\mu_j\gt0\Rightarrow g_j=0$.
Show answer

(i) and (iii). (ii) is false: an active wall can have $\mu_j=0$ (e.g. $\min x^2$ s.t. $-x\le0$ at $x=0$: $2\cdot0-\mu=0$ ⇒ $\mu=0$, yet the constraint is active). He called this an important distinction you have to remember Ch 6 lec 2 [29:00].

  • $\lambda$ free: an equality can push either way.

D3. Solving KKT by hand: try every active set

Why this works: complementarity says each inequality is either "on the wall" ($g_j=0$) or "not pushing" ($\mu_j=0$). Guess which ones are on the wall (an active set guess $\mathcal A$), and every product $\mu_jg_j=0$ turns into a plain equation. With $p$ inequalities there are $2^p$ guesses. For each guess:

  1. Set $\mu_j=0$ for $j\notin\mathcal A$ and $g_j(x)=0$ for $j\in\mathcal A$.
  2. Solve stationarity + equalities + those $g_j=0$ for $x$, $\lambda$, $\mu_{\mathcal A}$. (Now as many equations as unknowns.) If the system is nonlinear (curved constraints, products like $x_2(1-\mu_1)=0$), split it into branches ("$x_2=0$ or $\mu_1=1$"), solve every branch, and carry each solution forward as a separate candidate; a guess can give several points or none.
  3. Keep the solution only if $\mu_j\ge0$ for $j\in\mathcal A$ and $g_j(x)\le0$ for $j\notin\mathcal A$. Write down which condition kills each rejected case; marks are given for that.
Fully by hand: min (x₁−2)² + (x₂−1)² s.t. x₁ + x₂ ≤ 2, x₁ ≥ 0
  1. Course form. $g_1(x)=x_1+x_2-2\le0$, $g_2(x)=-x_1\le0$. No equalities.
  2. Lagrangian. $\ell=(x_1-2)^2+(x_2-1)^2+\mu_1(x_1+x_2-2)+\mu_2(-x_1)$.
  3. All five KKT parts. $$\begin{aligned} &\partial\ell/\partial x_1:\ 2(x_1-2)+\mu_1-\mu_2=0 &&\text{(S1)}\\ &\partial\ell/\partial x_2:\ 2(x_2-1)+\mu_1=0 &&\text{(S2)}\\ &x_1+x_2\le2,\quad -x_1\le0;\qquad \mu_1,\mu_2\ge0;\qquad \mu_1(x_1+x_2-2)=0,\ \ \mu_2x_1=0. \end{aligned}$$

  5. Guess $\mathcal A=\{1\}$ ($\mu_2=0$, $x_1+x_2=2$). (S2): $x_2=1-\mu_1/2$. (S1): $x_1=2-\mu_1/2$. Add them: $x_1+x_2=3-\mu_1$. Set equal to 2: $3-\mu_1=2$ ⇒ $\mu_1=1$. So $x=(3/2,\,1/2)$. Checks: $\mu_1=1\ge0$ ✓; inactive $g_2=-3/2\lt0$ ✓. KKT point $x^\star=(3/2,1/2)$, $\mu^\star=(1,0)$.
  6. Guess $\mathcal A=\{2\}$ ($\mu_1=0$, $x_1=0$). (S2): $x_2=1$. (S1): $2(0-2)-\mu_2=0$ ⇒ $\mu_2=-4\lt0$: dual feasibility fails, reject. (Pressing against $x_1\ge0$ would need a pull, not a push.)

  8. LICQ at the survivor. Only $g_1$ active, $\nabla g_1=(1,1)\ne0$ ✓. LICQ holds at every feasible point here (linear constraints, never both gradients dependent), so every local min must be among the KKT points: the only candidate is $(3/2,1/2)$.
  9. Sanity check with geometry. $f$ is squared distance to $(2,1)$; the nearest point of the line $x_1+x_2=2$ is $(2,1)-\frac{(2+1-2)}{2}(1,1)=(3/2,1/2)$ ✓. Whether it is a minimum is decided in E5 (second order).

Shortcuts to kill cases fast (from Midterm 2):

  • Geometrically impossible sets: if forcing some $g_j=0$ together makes another constraint fail, that exact active set never occurs (Midterm 2 III.1, problem in B5: $g_2=g_3=0$ forces $x=0$, where $g_1=4\gt0$).
  • Too many actives: more active constraints than unknowns usually has no solution; if it has one, check LICQ (more than $n$ gradients in $\mathbb R^n$ can't be independent).
  • Sign contradictions: Midterm 2 IV (Practice E1): $\min\tfrac12[(x_1-a)^2+(x_2+1)^2]$ s.t. $g_1=x_1^2+x_2^2-1\le0$, $g_2=-x_2\le0$; the second stationarity equation is $(1+2\mu_1)x_2+1-\mu_2=0$. If $x_2\gt0$ then $\mu_2=0$ and $(1+2\mu_1)x_2+1=0$ is impossible, so $x_2=0$ at every KKT point.
  • Homogeneity trick: when the $g_j$ are linear with no constant term, $\nabla g_j^\top x=g_j(x)$; dotting stationarity with $x$ then kills the multiplier terms by complementarity (Midterm 2 III.2, problem in B5: with $\mu_1=0$ it gives $2\|x\|^2=0$).

E. Second order: telling minima from saddles

E1. Why first order isn't enough

KKT is only necessary. Compare two problems at $x=0$, both with the wall $g=-x\le0$:

  • $\min x^2$: stationarity $2x-\mu=0$ at 0 ⇒ $\mu=0$. KKT holds. And 0 is the minimum.
  • $\min -x^2$: stationarity $-2x-\mu=0$ at 0 ⇒ $\mu=0$. KKT holds. But $f(t)=-t^2\lt0$ for $t\gt0$: 0 is not a minimum.

First-order information is identical ($\nabla f(0)=0$ in both). The difference is curvature. At a KKT point, every direction $d\in\mathcal L$ has $\nabla f^\top d\ge0$. Directions with $\nabla f^\top d\gt0$ are settled (uphill at first order). Directions with $\nabla f^\top d=0$ are ties: first order says nothing, just like $f'(x)=0$ in 1-D could be a min, a max or an inflection. On the ties we must look at second derivatives.

E2. Which Hessian? The Lagrangian's, because constraints bend

The surprise first. $\min f=v-u^2$ s.t. $h=v-2u^2=0$, at the origin.

  1. $\nabla f=(-2u,1)=(0,1)$, $\nabla h=(-4u,1)=(0,1)\ne0$ ⇒ regular. Stationarity $(0,1)+\lambda(0,1)=0$ ⇒ $\lambda^\star=-1$.
  2. Tie directions: $\nabla f^\top d=d_2=0$, i.e. $d=(d_1,0)$, along the $u$-axis.
  3. $\nabla^2 f=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$: along $d=(1,0)$ the curvature of $f$ is $-2$. "Looks like a maximum"?
  4. But walk along the feasible curve $v=2u^2$: $f(u,2u^2)=2u^2-u^2=u^2$. It's a strict minimum.

Why: the feasible curve bends upward, and $f$ increases in $v$ ($\partial f/\partial v=1$), so the bending of the constraint adds curvature. The Lagrangian accounts for it automatically:

$$\mathbf L_\star=\nabla^2f+\lambda^\star\nabla^2h=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}+(-1)\begin{pmatrix}-4&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad d^\top\mathbf L_\star d=2d_1^2\gt0.$$

Why the Lagrangian is the right function (the reason in three lines). Take feasible points $x(t)$ near $x^\star$ that stay on the equalities and on the walls with $\mu_j\gt0$. On them $h_i=0$ and those $g_j=0$, so $f(x(t))=\ell(x(t))$. Taylor-expand $\ell$ around $x^\star$:

$$f(x(t))-f(x^\star)=\ell(x(t))-\ell(x^\star)=\underbrace{\nabla_x\ell^\top(x(t)-x^\star)}_{=0\text{ by stationarity}}+\tfrac12(x(t)-x^\star)^\top\mathbf L_\star(x(t)-x^\star)+\dots$$

The first-order term vanishes no matter how the path curves, so what's left is the Lagrangian's curvature. With $x(t)-x^\star\approx td$: the change is about $\tfrac12t^2\,d^\top\mathbf L_\star d$. Check on the example: $d=(1,0)$, $\tfrac12t^2\cdot2=t^2$ ✓ (matches $f(t,2t^2)=t^2$).

Definition $$\mathbf L_\star=\nabla^2_{xx}\ell(x^\star,\lambda^\star,\mu^\star)=\nabla^2f(x^\star)+\sum_i\lambda_i^\star\nabla^2h_i(x^\star)+\sum_j\mu_j^\star\nabla^2g_j(x^\star)\quad\text{(multipliers fixed at their KKT values).}$$

Linear constraints have zero Hessian, so then $\mathbf L_\star=\nabla^2 f$.

Trap Using $\nabla^2 f$ alone with curved constraints. He: objective function or constraint function alone can be misleading Ch 6 lec 4 [45:30]. Quiz 3 Q16 (Practice B8) is this exact trap with $h=v-3u^2$.

E3. The critical cone C: exactly the tie directions

Which directions $d\in\mathcal L_\Omega$ are ties ($\nabla f^\top d=0$)? Use stationarity to rewrite $\nabla f$, then dot with $d$:

$$\nabla f^\top d=-\sum_i\lambda_i^\star\underbrace{\nabla h_i^\top d}_{=0\ (d\in\mathcal L)}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}\mu_j^\star\nabla g_j^\top d=\sum_{j\in\mathcal A}\underbrace{\mu_j^\star}_{\ge0}\cdot\underbrace{(-\nabla g_j^\top d)}_{\ge0\ (d\in\mathcal L)}.$$

(Inactive $j$ dropped out because $\mu_j^\star=0$.) Each term is $\ge0$, so the sum is 0 exactly when every term is 0:

  • $j\in\mathcal A_+$ ($\mu_j^\star\gt0$): need $\nabla g_j^\top d=0$. Moving inward off a pushing wall costs first-order increase, so that direction is settled, not a tie. Ties must stay on pushing walls: "$=$" rows.
  • $j\in\mathcal A_0$ ($\mu_j^\star=0$): the term is $0$ anyway. Moving inward off a non-pushing wall costs nothing at first order, so it's still a tie and must still be tested: keep "$\le$" rows.
Definition 6.4.1.1 (critical cone) $$C=\left\{d:\ \begin{array}{ll}\nabla h_i(x^\star)^\top d=0, & i=1,\dots,m\\ \nabla g_j(x^\star)^\top d=0, & j\in\mathcal A_+=\{j\in\mathcal A:\mu_j^\star\gt0\}\\ \nabla g_j(x^\star)^\top d\le0, & j\in\mathcal A_0=\{j\in\mathcal A:\mu_j^\star=0\}\end{array}\right\}\ =\ \{d\in\mathcal L_\Omega:\ \nabla f(x^\star)^\top d=0\}.$$

$C$ is a closed convex cone; it is a subspace when $\mathcal A_0=\varnothing$.

Worked example (notes Ex. 6.4.3.2): f = u + v² on u ≥ 0, v ≥ 0, at the origin
  1. $g_1=-u$, $g_2=-v$, both active. $\nabla f=(1,2v)=(1,0)$.
  2. Stationarity: $(1,0)+\mu_1(-1,0)+\mu_2(0,-1)=0$ ⇒ $\mu_1=1$, $\mu_2=0$. So $\mathcal A_+=\{1\}$, $\mathcal A_0=\{2\}$.
  3. $\mathcal L=\{d_1\ge0,\ d_2\ge0\}$. $C$: row for $g_1$ becomes $-d_1=0$; row for $g_2$ stays $-d_2\le0$. So $C=\{(0,d_2):\ d_2\ge0\}$, a ray (not the whole line, not $\{0\}$).
  4. $\mathbf L_\star=\nabla^2f=\operatorname{diag}(0,2)$ (linear constraints). On $C$: $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d=2d_2^2\gt0$ for $d_2\gt0$ ⇒ SOSC (next subsection) ⇒ strict local min.
  5. Change to $f=u-v^2$: same $\mu$, same $C$, but $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d=-2d_2^2\lt0$ ⇒ not a min (indeed $f(0,t)=-t^2$). If you had wrongly written "$=$" for the $\mathcal A_0$ row, $C$ would be $\{0\}$ and you would wrongly declare a strict minimum.

E4. The second-order conditions (SONC and SOSC)

On $C$, test the sign of $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d$ (curvature of the Lagrangian). The course has two theorems:

Theorem 6.4.1 (SONC: necessary)

$f,h,g$ are $C^2$ (twice continuously differentiable) near $x^\star$, $x^\star$ is a local minimiser, and $x^\star$ is regular ⇒ KKT multipliers exist and $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d\ge0$ for every $d\in C$.

Use it backwards: at a regular KKT point, one $d\in C$ with $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d\lt0$ ⇒ not a local minimiser.

Theorem 6.4.2 (SOSC: sufficient)

$f,h,g$ are $C^2$ near $x^\star$, $(x^\star,\lambda^\star,\mu^\star)$ satisfies KKT, and $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d\gt0$ for every $d\in C$ with $d\ne0$ ⇒ $x^\star$ is a strict local minimiser. No LICQ needed.

Why eigenvectors appear: stationarity always has the form "$x$ = number × $Qx$", i.e. $Qx=(\text{number})\,x$, so every boundary KKT point is an eigenvector rescaled to lie on the constraint surface.

Worked template: min xᵀx s.t. xᵀQx ≥ 1
  1. Course form. $g=1-x^\top Qx\le0$. $\ell=x^\top x+\mu(1-x^\top Qx)$. (Gradients: $\nabla(x^\top x)=2x$, $\nabla(x^\top Qx)=2Qx$ for symmetric $Q$.)
  2. KKT. $2x-2\mu Qx=0$; $x^\top Qx\ge1$; $\mu\ge0$; $\mu(1-x^\top Qx)=0$.
  3. Inactive ⇒ $\mu=0$ ⇒ $2x=0$ ⇒ $x=0$, but $0^\top Q0=0\lt1$: infeasible. So the constraint is active and $\mu\ne0$.
  4. Active: $x=\mu Qx$ ⇒ $Qx=\tfrac1\mu x$ ⇒ $x$ is an eigenvector: $x=s\,q_i$ with $\tfrac1\mu=\theta_i$, i.e. $\mu=1/\theta_i\gt0$ ✓. On the surface: $x^\top Qx=s^2\theta_i=1$ ⇒ $s=\pm1/\sqrt{\theta_i}$. KKT points $x_i^\pm=\pm q_i/\sqrt{\theta_i}$, value $f=s^2=1/\theta_i$.
  5. LICQ. $\nabla g=-2Qx=-2\theta_ix\ne0$ ✓.
  6. Critical cone. $\mu\gt0$ ⇒ "$=$" row: $\nabla g^\top d=0$ ⇔ $x_i^\top d=0$ ⇔ $q_i^\top d=0$. So $C=\operatorname{span}\{q_j:j\ne i\}$: write $d=\sum_{j\ne i}\alpha_jq_j$.
  7. Hessian. $\mathbf L_\star=2I-2\mu Q=2(I-Q/\theta_i)$. With orthonormal $q_j$: $d^\top d=\sum_{j\ne i}\alpha_j^2$ and $d^\top Qd=\sum_{j\ne i}\theta_j\alpha_j^2$, so $$d^\top\mathbf L_\star d=2\sum_{j\ne i}\Big(1-\frac{\theta_j}{\theta_i}\Big)\alpha_j^2.$$
  8. Classify. $i=n$: every $\theta_j\lt\theta_n$ ⇒ every coefficient $\gt0$ ⇒ SOSC ⇒ strict local min. $i\lt n$: $d=q_n$ gives $2(1-\theta_n/\theta_i)\lt0$, regular ⇒ SONC fails.
  9. Global. Feasible set closed and nonempty, $f$ coercive ($\to\infty$ as $\|x\|\to\infty$) ⇒ a global min exists; it's a KKT point (LICQ everywhere) ⇒ $\min=1/\theta_n$ at $\pm q_n/\sqrt{\theta_n}$.

H. The FONC proof (the only proof in the Ch 6 syllabus)

He: this is the only theorem we'll prove in this chapter Ch 6 lec 1 [1:04:00]. It's short; learn it as a chain of five links.

Statement (Theorem 6.2.1): $x^\star\in\Omega$ a local minimiser, $f$ differentiable at $x^\star$ ⇒ $d^\top\nabla f(x^\star)\ge0$ for all $d\in T_\Omega(x^\star)$.

  1. Pick a tangent direction. $d\in T_\Omega(x^\star)$: there are $t_k\downarrow0$, $d_k\to d$ with $x_k=x^\star+t_kd_k\in\Omega$. Since $d_k$ converges it is bounded, so $x_k\to x^\star$.
  2. Use local minimality. For large $k$, $x_k$ is feasible and close to $x^\star$, so $f(x_k)-f(x^\star)\ge0$.
  3. Use differentiability. $f(x_k)-f(x^\star)=t_k\,d_k^\top\nabla f(x^\star)+o(t_k)$. Here $R(s)=f(x^\star+s)-f(x^\star)-\nabla f(x^\star)^\top s$ is the Taylor remainder; differentiability means $R(s)/\|s\|\to0$ as $s\to0$. The remainder is $o(t_k)$ because $d_k$ is bounded: $\dfrac{R(t_kd_k)}{t_k}=\dfrac{R(t_kd_k)}{\|t_kd_k\|}\,\|d_k\|\to0\cdot(\text{bounded})=0$.
  4. Divide by $t_k\gt0$ (keeps "$\ge$"): $0\le d_k^\top\nabla f(x^\star)+o(1)$.
  5. Let $k\to\infty$: $d^\top\nabla f(x^\star)\ge0$. No regularity used anywhere.

I. The exam recipe: the KKT ladder

Every descriptive Ch 6 question so far (Midterm 2 IV, V, VI) is this ladder, sometimes with a parameter. Write every rung; he marks them.

The ladder (write every rung)
  1. Rewrite constraints as $h=0$, $g\le0$; max → min of $-F$. Name each constraint.
  2. Lagrangian $\ell=f+\lambda^\top h+\mu^\top g$.
  3. Write all five KKT parts explicitly.
  4. Enumerate active-set guesses $\mathcal A\subseteq\{1,\dots,p\}$: $\mu_j=0$ for $j\notin\mathcal A$, $g_j=0$ for $j\in\mathcal A$.
  5. Solve stationarity + $h=0$ + active $g_j=0$ for $x,\lambda,\mu_{\mathcal A}$.
  6. Check $\mu_{\mathcal A}\ge0$, inactive $g_j(x)\le0$, $h=0$. Discard failures, saying which condition fails.
  7. LICQ at each survivor (and ask: could a non-regular feasible point be a minimum the KKT search missed?).
  8. Split the active set into $\mathcal A_+$ ($\mu\gt0$) and $\mathcal A_0$ ($\mu=0$).
  9. Critical cone $C$: "$=$" rows for $h$ and $\mathcal A_+$, "$\le$" rows for $\mathcal A_0$.
  10. Hessian of $\ell$ with multipliers fixed: $\mathbf L_\star$.
  11. Test $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d$ on $C$: $\gt0$ for all $d\ne0$ ⇒ SOSC; some $d\in C$ with $\lt0$ at a regular point ⇒ SONC fails; $\ge0$ with some zero ⇒ undecided. $C=\{0\}$ ⇒ SOSC holds.
  12. Classify each point in one clear sentence (strict local min / not a local min / undecided), then the global answer (compare values; existence: a continuous $f$ on a compact (closed and bounded) feasible set attains its minimum (Weierstrass); or $f$ coercive, i.e. $f\to\infty$ as $\|x\|\to\infty$, on a closed nonempty set).
Worked example: full ladder with an equality (Quiz 3 Q13) $\min (u-2)^2+v^2$ s.t. $u+v=1$, $u\ge0$, $v\ge0$.
  1. Form. $h=u+v-1=0$, $g_1=-u\le0$, $g_2=-v\le0$. $\ell=(u-2)^2+v^2+\lambda(u+v-1)-\mu_1u-\mu_2v$.
  2. KKT. $\partial\ell/\partial u$: $2(u-2)+\lambda-\mu_1=0$; $\partial\ell/\partial v$: $2v+\lambda-\mu_2=0$; $u+v=1$; $u,v\ge0$; $\mu_1,\mu_2\ge0$; $\mu_1u=0$, $\mu_2v=0$.
  3. $\mathcal A=\varnothing$: $\mu=0$. Subtract the two stationarity equations: $2(u-2)-2v=0$ ⇒ $u-v=2$. With $u+v=1$: $u=3/2$, $v=-1/2$ (then $\lambda=1$). Violates $v\ge0$: reject.
  4. $\mathcal A=\{1\}$: $u=0$, so $v=1$; $\mu_2=0$ ⇒ $\lambda=-2v=-2$; then $\mu_1=2(0-2)+\lambda=-6\lt0$: reject (dual feasibility).
  5. $\mathcal A=\{2\}$: $v=0$, so $u=1$; $\mu_1=0$ ⇒ $\lambda=-2(1-2)=2$; then $\mu_2=2\cdot0+\lambda=2\ge0$ ✓. KKT point $(1,0)$, $\lambda^\star=2$, $\mu^\star=(0,2)$.
  6. $\mathcal A=\{1,2\}$: $u=v=0$ violates $u+v=1$: impossible.
  7. LICQ at $(1,0)$: $\nabla h=(1,1)$, $\nabla g_2=(0,-1)$, $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}=-1\ne0$ ✓.
  8. $\mathcal A_+=\{2\}$, $\mathcal A_0=\varnothing$. $C=\{d: d_1+d_2=0,\ -d_2=0\}=\{0\}$.
  9. $\mathbf L_\star=2I$ (constraints linear). $C=\{0\}$ ⇒ SOSC holds ⇒ $(1,0)$ is a strict local minimiser; global too (convex problem, only KKT point).
Worked example 2: full ladder where an active constraint has μ = 0 (exam style) $\min f(x)=x_2-x_1^2$ s.t. $x_1\ge0$, $x_2\ge0$, $x_1+x_2\le1$ (a triangle).
  1. Form. $g_1=-x_1\le0$, $g_2=-x_2\le0$, $g_3=x_1+x_2-1\le0$. $\ell=x_2-x_1^2-\mu_1x_1-\mu_2x_2+\mu_3(x_1+x_2-1)$.
  2. KKT. $\partial\ell/\partial x_1$: $-2x_1-\mu_1+\mu_3=0$ (S1); $\partial\ell/\partial x_2$: $1-\mu_2+\mu_3=0$ (S2); $x_1,x_2\ge0$, $x_1+x_2\le1$; $\mu\ge0$; $\mu_1x_1=0$, $\mu_2x_2=0$, $\mu_3(x_1+x_2-1)=0$.
  3. Guesses with $\mu_2=0$ ($\varnothing$, $\{1\}$, $\{3\}$, $\{1,3\}$): (S2) becomes $1+\mu_3=0$. If $\mu_3=0$ too ($\varnothing$, $\{1\}$) this reads $1=0$: impossible. If $3$ is active ($\{3\}$, $\{1,3\}$), $\mu_3=-1\lt0$: reject. (So $g_2$ must be active at every KKT point.)
  4. $\{2\}$ ($x_2=0$, $\mu_1=\mu_3=0$): (S1) $x_1=0$; (S2) $\mu_2=1$. Point $(0,0)$. There $g_1=0$ as well, so the exact active set is $\{1,2\}$ with $\mu_1=0$. Guess $\{1,2\}$ gives the same: $x=(0,0)$, (S1) $\mu_1=0$, (S2) $\mu_2=1$. KKT point $(0,0)$, $\mu^\star=(0,1,0)$.
  5. $\{2,3\}$ ($x_2=0$, $x_1=1$, $\mu_1=0$): (S1) $-2+\mu_3=0$ ⇒ $\mu_3=2$; (S2) $\mu_2=1+2=3$. $g_1=-1\lt0$ ✓. KKT point $(1,0)$, $\mu^\star=(0,3,2)$.
  6. $\{1,2,3\}$: $x_1=x_2=0$ gives $g_3=-1\ne0$: impossible.
  7. LICQ. At $(0,0)$: $\nabla g_1=(-1,0)$, $\nabla g_2=(0,-1)$, $\det=1\ne0$ ✓. At $(1,0)$: $\nabla g_2=(0,-1)$, $\nabla g_3=(1,1)$, $\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=1\ne0$ ✓. (At the third corner $(0,1)$: $(-1,0),(1,1)$, $\det=-1\ne0$; elsewhere at most one constraint is active.) LICQ holds everywhere, so every local min is one of these two KKT points.
  8. $(0,0)$: split. $\mathcal A_+=\{2\}$, $\mathcal A_0=\{1\}$. $C$: $-d_2=0$ ("$=$" row) and $-d_1\le0$ ("$\le$" row) ⇒ $C=\{(d_1,0):d_1\ge0\}$, a ray. $\mathbf L_\star=\nabla^2f=\operatorname{diag}(-2,0)$ (constraints linear). $d=(1,0)\in C$: $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d=-2\lt0$, regular ⇒ SONC fails ⇒ not a local min. Check: $f(t,0)=-t^2\lt0=f(0,0)$. (Writing "$=$" for the $\mathcal A_0$ row would give $C=\{0\}$ and the false verdict "strict local min".)
  9. $(1,0)$: split. $\mathcal A_+=\{2,3\}$, $\mathcal A_0=\varnothing$. $C$: $-d_2=0$, $d_1+d_2=0$ ⇒ $C=\{0\}$ ⇒ SOSC holds ⇒ strict local min, although $\mathbf L_\star=\operatorname{diag}(-2,0)$ is not positive semidefinite.
  10. Global. The triangle is compact and $f$ continuous ⇒ a global min exists and is a KKT point ⇒ global min $f=-1$ at $(1,0)$.

Parametric version (Midterm 2 IV, Practice F1). When $f$ contains a parameter $a$, the active set changes with $a$. Do the enumeration with $a$ as a symbol; each case gives a condition on $a$ ("$\mu\ge0$ iff $a\ge1$", "inactive iff $a\lt1$"). Then treat each switching value on its own: there the constraint is usually active with $\mu=0$ (it's in $\mathcal A_0$), so its row in $C$ is "$\le$", not "$=$".

Last-line traps
  • Forgetting to rewrite "$\ge$" constraints (wrong-sign $\mu$).
  • Putting a sign restriction on $\lambda$.
  • Using $\nabla^2 f$ instead of $\mathbf L_\star$ when constraints are curved.
  • Testing curvature on $\mathbb R^n$ instead of on $C$; making $\mathcal A_0$ rows "$=$".
  • Saying "SOSC fails" when $C=\{0\}$.
  • Saying "KKT fails ⇒ not a minimum" at a non-regular point.

Symbols: $f$ objective; $h_i=0$ ($i=1..m$) equalities; $g_j\le0$ ($j=1..p$) inequalities; $\Omega$ feasible set; $x^\star$ the point tested; $\mathcal A=\{j:g_j(x^\star)=0\}$ active set; $\lambda$ equality multipliers (free), $\mu$ inequality multipliers ($\ge0$); $d$ a direction; $\theta_i,q_i$ eigenvalues/unit eigenvectors of $Q$.

  • Problem form: $\min f(x)$ s.t. $h_i(x)=0$, $g_j(x)\le0$. Rewrite first: $x_1\ge0\to-x_1\le0$; $x^\top Qx\ge1\to1-x^\top Qx\le0$; $\max F\to\min -F$.
  • Lagrangian: $\ell(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\sum_i\lambda_ih_i(x)+\sum_j\mu_jg_j(x)$ (plus signs; Midterm 2 calls it $L$).
  • KKT (five parts): (1) $\nabla f+\sum\lambda_i\nabla h_i+\sum\mu_j\nabla g_j=0$; (2) $h=0$; (3) $g\le0$; (4) $\mu\ge0$; (5) $\mu_jg_j=0$ for every $j$. $\lambda$ has no sign. Inactive ⇒ $\mu_j=0$; $\mu_j\gt0$ ⇒ active; active does not force $\mu_j\gt0$.
  • LICQ / regular point: gradients of all $h_i$ and all active $g_j$ linearly independent. KKT is necessary only at regular points; under LICQ the multipliers are unique.
  • Cones: tangent $T_\Omega$: $d$ with $x^\star+t_kd_k\in\Omega$, $t_k\downarrow0$, $d_k\to d$. Linearised $\mathcal L_\Omega=\{d:\nabla h_i^\top d=0,\ \nabla g_j^\top d\le0\ (j\in\mathcal A)\}$. $T_\Omega\subseteq\mathcal L_\Omega$ always; equal if regular (sufficient, not necessary) or all constraints linear. Counterexamples: $uv=0$, $v^3=0$.
  • Geometric FONC (no CQ): local min ⇒ $\nabla f(x^\star)^\top d\ge0$ for all $d\in T_\Omega(x^\star)$. A KKT point satisfies it without LICQ.
  • Farkas: [$c^\top d\ge0$ whenever $A^\top d=0,\ B^\top d\le0$] ⇔ [$c+A\lambda+B\mu=0$ for some $\lambda$ free, $\mu\ge0$]. No rank assumption.
  • Active split: $\mathcal A_+=\{j\in\mathcal A:\mu_j^\star\gt0\}$, $\mathcal A_0=\{j\in\mathcal A:\mu_j^\star=0\}$.
  • Critical cone: $C=\{d:\ \nabla h_i^\top d=0\ \forall i,\ \nabla g_j^\top d=0\ (j\in\mathcal A_+),\ \nabla g_j^\top d\le0\ (j\in\mathcal A_0)\}=\{d\in\mathcal L_\Omega:\nabla f(x^\star)^\top d=0\}$.
  • Hessian of the Lagrangian: $\mathbf L_\star=\nabla^2f+\sum\lambda_i^\star\nabla^2h_i+\sum\mu_j^\star\nabla^2g_j$ at $x^\star$, multipliers fixed. Never $\nabla^2 f$ alone with curved constraints.
  • SONC ($C^2$, local min, regular): $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d\ge0$ for all $d\in C$. Fails at a regular KKT point ⇒ not a local min.
  • SOSC ($C^2$, KKT holds, no LICQ needed): $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d\gt0$ for all $0\ne d\in C$ ⇒ strict local min. $C=\{0\}$ ⇒ SOSC holds. Indefinite $\mathbf L_\star$ on $\mathbb R^n$ is irrelevant.
  • Ladder: rewrite → $\ell$ → five KKT parts → active-set guesses → solve → check signs/feasibility → LICQ → $\mathcal A_+/\mathcal A_0$ → $C$ → $\mathbf L_\star$ → test on $C$ → classify (local, then global).
  • Eigenvector answers ($Q\succ0$, $0\lt\theta_1\lt\dots\lt\theta_n$): $\max x^\top x$ s.t. $x^\top Qx\le1$ → $1/\theta_1$ at $\pm q_1/\sqrt{\theta_1}$; $\min x^\top x$ s.t. $x^\top Qx\ge1$ (or $=1$) → $1/\theta_n$ at $\pm q_n/\sqrt{\theta_n}$; on $C=q_i^\perp$, writing $d=\sum_{j\ne i}\alpha_jq_j$: $d^\top\mathbf L_\star d=2\sum_{j\ne i}(1-\theta_j/\theta_i)\alpha_j^2$ for the min problem.
