Why this pair of questions is interesting

Here are all four boxes, with the tickets drawn so the matching parts line up.

In each choice, 89 tickets are the same in both boxes: 89 × 1 million in Choice 1, 89 × nothing in Choice 2. Those tickets pay the same whichever box you pick, so they should not change your mind.

Hide them and the two choices become identical: 11 tickets of 1 million against 10 tickets of 5 million plus 1 ticket of nothing. So a consistent decision-maker picks A with C, or B with D.

When Maurice Allais put these choices to economists at a 1953 conference, many (including trained decision theorists) chose A and D. This breaks the independence axiom of expected-utility theory: adding the same outcome to both options should not reverse a preference.

The usual reading: certainty feels special (A's sure million is hard to give up), but once the chances are small, 10% and 11% feel the same, so people go for the bigger prize.
