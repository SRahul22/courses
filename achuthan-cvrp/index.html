      One depot, a few trucks, and an astronomical number of plans

      The capacitated vehicle routing problem asks for the shortest set of truck routes that serves every customer once without overloading any truck.

      It is the core model behind parcel delivery, milk runs and waste collection, and this paper is about solving it with a proof of optimality.

      It is like planning carpools for a wedding: every guest must ride, cars have seats, and you want the least total driving.

      A 7-customer problem has 877 ways to split customers into groups. A 30-customer one has 8.5 × 10²³. Nobody checks them one by one.

      The setting p. 153

      A depot (node 1) supplies customers 2, …, n. Customer $j$ has demand $q_j$. Every vehicle has the same capacity $Q$. Each customer is on exactly one route, no route carries more than $Q$, and the total distance is minimised.

      The paper's assumptions p. 154

      $q_j$ and $Q$ are whole numbers with $Q \ge \max_j q_j$. Distances $c_{ij}$ are symmetric and satisfy the triangle inequality. The number of vehicles $m$ may be fixed or variable. That last choice matters later: two of the new cuts work only when $m$ is variable.

      if heuristics are fast and usually good, why bother with exact methods?

      A heuristic hands you a solution but no way to know how far it is from the best. An exact method also produces a lower bound, a number the optimum cannot go below. When the bound meets the best solution found, you have a certificate. Exact methods are also how heuristics get benchmarked: the 14 hard problems in the paper's Table 4.6 are compared with best-known heuristic values.

      Why it is hard

      The CVRP contains the travelling salesman problem (one truck with room for everything) and bin packing (which customers share a truck), so it is NP-hard p. 153. Enumerating plans fails almost immediately: the number of ways to split $k$ customers into groups is the Bell number, already 1,382,958,545 for 15 customers, before choosing the order inside each route.

      The paper's approach is the one that conquered large TSPs (Padberg and Rinaldi 1991): solve a linear programme, find inequalities the LP solution breaks but every real solution obeys, add them, repeat, and branch only when stuck. That is branch-and-cut, the method of Lectures 8–9 applied to routing.

      Every exact method keeps an upper bound $Z^*$ (cost of the best plan found) and a lower bound $\bar Z$ (from an LP). The optimum lies between them. The paper's contribution is better ways to push $\bar Z$ up.

      One variable per pair of locations

      The paper uses Laporte, Nobert and Desrochers' (1985) model: one variable $x_{ij}$ per pair of locations, counting how often a truck drives between them.

      Every cut in the paper is an inequality in these variables, so this notation is the language of the rest of the paper.

      It is like describing a road trip by which roads were driven, not by who drove them.

      Two variables are enough to say "truck goes 1 → 7 → 1": $x_{17} = 2$. Why 2 and not 1?

      For $i \lt j$, $$x_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if a vehicle travels on a single trip between } i \text{ and } j,\\ 2 & \text{if } i = 1 \text{ and } (1, j, 1) \text{ is a route},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ $x_{ij}$ is only defined for $i \lt j$ and $q_i + q_j \le Q$: two customers who cannot share a truck never get an edge.

      is it called "two-index"?

      Each variable has two indices, $i$ and $j$. A three-index model (Lecture 12 slide 18) adds a vehicle index $k$: $x_{ijk}$. Two-index models are smaller and have no symmetry between identical trucks, but they cannot tell trucks apart, so capacity cannot be written per vehicle. It has to be enforced through sets of customers instead. That is why capacity lives in exponentially many set constraints (1.4).

      $$\min\; f = \sum_{i \lt j} c_{ij} x_{ij} \tag{1.1}$$ $$\sum_{j \in \mathscr C} x_{1j} = 2m \tag{1.2}$$ $$\sum_{i \lt k} x_{ik} + \sum_{j \gt k} x_{kj} = 2 \quad \text{for each } k \in \mathscr C \tag{1.3}$$ $$\sum_{i,j \in S} x_{ij} \le |S| - \ell(S) \quad \text{for all } S \subseteq \mathscr C,\; 3 \le |S| \le n-1 \tag{1.4}$$ $$x_{ij} \in \{0,1,2\} \text{ for } i = 1, \qquad x_{ij} \in \{0,1\} \text{ otherwise} \tag{1.5}$$ $$m \text{ is a positive integer.} \tag{1.6}$$

      (1.2) and (1.3): degrees

      Each customer touches total edge value 2 (arrive once, leave once). The depot touches $2m$: each of the $m$ routes leaves and returns. These are degree constraints.

      (1.4): subtours and capacity in one line

      $\ell(S)$ is the fewest vehicles the customers in $S$ need. If $S$ is served by $\ell(S)$ or more routes, at most $|S| - \ell(S)$ edges can lie inside $S$. A closed loop on $S$ has $|S|$ edges inside, so it is cut off, whether it misses the depot or carries too much. This is the subtour elimination constraint, the CVRP version of the TSP's $x(E(S)) \le |S| - 1$.

      What is $\ell(S)$, exactly? pp. 154, 159

      §1 defines $\ell(S)$ as the greatest lower bound on the number of vehicles needed to visit $S$ in an optimal solution. The implementation (§3) uses $\ell(S) = \lceil \tfrac{1}{Q}\sum_{j \in S} q_j \rceil$, the bin-packing lower bound. With that choice (1.4) is the rounded capacity inequality.

      Shave off the fractional corner, keep every whole point

      A valid inequality is one that every feasible integer solution satisfies; a cut is a valid inequality that the current LP solution violates.

      Adding cuts raises the LP bound without ever losing a real solution, which is the whole engine of this paper.

      It is like trimming a hedge: you may cut leaves that stick out, never the trunk.

      A cut is allowed to remove infinitely many LP points. It may not remove a single integer one.

      Let $X$ be the set of feasible integer solutions. $\pi x \le \pi_0$ is valid for $X$ if $\pi x \le \pi_0$ for every $x \in X$. Given an LP solution $x^*$, a valid inequality with $\pi x^* \gt \pi_0$ is a cut for $x^*$. Finding one is the separation problem.

      does the paper call (1.4) a "subtour elimination constraint" and also a cut?

      • Feasibility-based: valid for every feasible solution. (1.4), (1.7), (2.1), (2.3), (2.8). This is what the literature used until then.
      • Optimality-based: valid for some optimal solution, not every feasible one. (2.9) and (2.10). They may cut off feasible, even optimal, solutions, but never all optimal ones. Part 4 treats them.

      When any two neighbours overload the truck

      If a set $S$ together with any two of the sets $T_1,\dots,T_k$ is too heavy for one truck, the route through $S$ can touch at most one of the $T$'s, and that limits how many edges can join them.

      These cuts catch fractional solutions that every subtour inequality misses, especially in "tight" problems where trucks are nearly full.

      It is like a lift that holds you plus one suitcase: whoever shares it with you, there is room for only one of the suitcases at a time.

      At bound 317.35 the toy's LP satisfies every (1.4) and (1.7). One three-set cut still finds it illegal.

      should these hold? (the idea of the proof, p. 155)

      Take an integer solution and look at $H = G[S]$, the routes restricted to $S$. It is a set of $r$ paths, so $x(S) \le |S| - r$ (2.4). Each $T_p$ can carry at most $|T_p| - 1$ internal edges, so $\sum_p x(T_p) \le |T| - k$ (2.6).

      Cuts that may remove optimal solutions, and why that is fine

      (2.9) says: if a group of customers fits in one truck, at most one route stays entirely inside that group. Some feasible, even optimal, solutions break it, but at least one optimal solution never does.

      This is the paper's most original idea: cuts based on the structure of an optimal solution, not on feasibility.

      It is like a rule "never send two half-empty vans to the same street": not every legal plan follows it, but some best plan always does.

      Two trucks each serve one customer near the depot. Combined they fit in one truck. Is merging them ever worse?

      There exists an optimal solution of the CVRP (1.1)–(1.6) with variable $m$ satisfying $$\sum_{i,j \in S} x_{ij} + \sum_{j \in S} x_{1j} \;\le\; |S| + 1 \quad \text{for all } S \subseteq \mathscr C,\ 2 \le |S| \le |\mathscr C|,\ \sum_{i \in S} q_i \le Q. \tag{2.9}$$

      does the left side count routes?

      Look at $H = G[S \cup \{1\}]$ in an integer solution. A route that lies entirely inside $S \cup \{1\}$ with $u$ customers contributes $u + 1$ edges; a route that only passes through part of $S$ contributes a path, at most one edge per customer. So $x(S) + x(1{:}S) \le |S| + (\text{number of whole routes inside } S)$. (2.9) says at most one route lives entirely inside $S$.

      Proof of Theorem 2.3 (p. 156), on the toy's numbers.

      1. Take an optimal solution and a set $S$ with $q(S) \le Q$ that violates (2.9). Then at least two whole routes $\tau_1 = (1, i_1, \dots, i_u, 1)$ and $\tau_2 = (1, j_1, \dots, j_v, 1)$ lie inside $S \cup \{1\}$.

        From the counting above: more than $|S| + 1$ edges means at least two whole routes.

      2. Join them: $\tau = (1, i_1, \dots, i_u, j_1, \dots, j_v, 1)$. Its load is at most $q(S) \le Q$, so it is feasible.

        Toy example: routes 1→7→1 and 1→8→1, with $q_7 + q_8 = 8 \le 10$.

      3. Cost: $c(\tau) = c(\tau_1) + c(\tau_2) - c_{1 i_u} - c_{1 j_1} + c_{i_u j_1} \le c(\tau_1) + c(\tau_2)$ by the triangle inequality.

        Toy: separate routes $2(5.1) + 2(33.4) = 77.0$; merged $5.1 + 30.4 + 33.4 = 68.9$.

      4. So replacing $\tau_1, \tau_2$ by $\tau$ gives another optimal solution with one fewer violated (2.9) and none newly violated. Repeat until none is violated.

        Each step removes a route, so it terminates. The result is an optimal solution satisfying all of (2.9).

      Cut until stuck, then branch, then cut again

      Branch-and-cut solves an LP at each node of a search tree, adds violated inequalities until none is found, then either prunes the node or splits it on a fractional variable.

      The new cuts matter only through this loop: better bounds mean nodes are pruned earlier and the tree stays small.

      It is like a detective who interviews everyone before splitting the case into "the butler did it" and "the butler did not".

      With the new cuts the toy is solved in 5 nodes. With only (1.4) and (1.7), the same code needs 7.

      1. Upper bound $Z^*$: Paessens' modified savings heuristic (or the best-known value, for literature problems).
      2. Root LP: (1.1)–(1.3), $m \ge \ell(\mathscr C)$; variables at zero after the root are eliminated.
      3. At each node: solve the LP (CPLEX 2.1) to get $\bar Z$. If infeasible or $\bar Z \ge Z^*$, prune.
      4. Separate with Searches 1–6 in the stated order; add violated (1.4), (1.7), (2.1), (2.3), (2.8), (2.9); add Gomory cuts; purge ineffective constraints; repeat step 3, unless tailing off.
      5. Integral solution: update $Z^*$. Fractional and no cut: choose a branching variable, create two children.
      6. Forced variables, branching-variable choice and backtracking follow Achuthan et al. (1996).

      Why a two-index model and not a three-index or set-partitioning model?

      It is compact (one variable per edge) and has no symmetry between identical trucks. The price: capacity must be written as exponentially many set constraints, so they are generated as cuts. This paper is about making those cuts stronger and easier to find. Set-partitioning models give stronger bounds but need column generation (Lectures 13–14).

      Why may $x_{1j}$ be 2?

      A single-customer route 1 → j → 1 uses the depot edge twice. Allowing $x_{1j} = 2$ keeps the model undirected and lets degree constraints count it correctly (p. 154).

      Is $\ell(S) = \lceil q(S)/Q \rceil$ the true number of vehicles $S$ needs?

      No, it is a bin-packing lower bound; the true minimum can be higher (e.g. three demands of 6 with Q = 10 need 3 trucks, not 2). Using a lower bound keeps (1.4) valid but possibly weaker than it could be. §1 defines ℓ as the greatest lower bound; §3 implements the ceiling (pp. 154, 159).

      (1.7) is equivalent to (1.4). What is new about it?

      Nothing about the polytope. Given the degree equations, (1.7) for $S$ equals (1.4) for $\mathscr C \setminus S$. The gain is in separation: heuristic searches build small sets, and (1.7) lets a small $S$ reveal a violated constraint on its large complement. Achuthan et al.       What does (2.1) add?

      It is (1.7) applied inside one tour component $S \cup \{1\}$, using the tour's own vehicle count $m_S = |S| - x(S)$ instead of the global $m$. It catches subsets $P$ whose complement in the tour needs more vehicles than the tour provides, when $S$ itself gives no violation. On the toy, round 3, (2.1) with $P = \{4,5,6\}$ is violated by 0.5 while (1.4) and (1.7) for the same sets are not.

      Give the intuition for Theorem 2.1.

      If $S$ with any two of the $T$'s overloads a truck, the route through $S$ can reach at most one $T$. A path through $S$ has only two ends to leave by; in an integer solution, "both ends going into $T$-sets, and those sets being paths themselves" would put two $T$'s on one route. So one unit of edge value is lost, and the factor 3 on $x(S)$ handles the case where $S$ is split into several paths.

      When are (2.3)/(2.8) better than subtour cuts?

      Is (2.9) a valid inequality?

      Not in the usual sense: some feasible, and even some optimal, solutions violate it (Remark 2.2). Theorem 2.3 proves that at least one optimal solution satisfies all of (2.9), when $m$ is variable. So adding it keeps the optimal value; that is all an exact method needs.

      Prove Theorem 2.3 on the board.

      Suppose an optimal solution violates (2.9) for $S$ with $q(S) \le Q$. Then two whole routes lie inside $S \cup \{1\}$. Concatenate them: load ≤ $q(S) \le Q$; cost changes by $c_{i_u j_1} - c_{1 i_u} - c_{1 j_1} \le 0$ by the triangle inequality. The new solution is optimal with one fewer violation and none added. Repeat until none remains.

      What if distances violate the triangle inequality?

      Then merging two routes can increase cost, and Theorem 2.3's proof breaks: (2.9) could cut off every optimal solution. The paper assumes the triangle inequality throughout (p. 154); Euclidean distances satisfy it, apart from rounding to two decimals, which can break it by at most a few hundredths.

      Can (2.9) be used with a fixed fleet?

      Only if $m = \ell(\mathscr C)$, where it holds anyway. For $m \gt \ell(\mathscr C)$ it may make the problem infeasible: Remark 2.3's example with unit demands and $m = Q = |\mathscr C|$. The literature problems in Tables 4.5–4.6 have fixed $m$.

      Can you add other dominance rules together with (2.9)?

      Not without a new proof. Each optimality-based rule guarantees that some optimal solution survives; two such guarantees may refer to different solutions, and together they could remove all optimal solutions (Remark 2.6).

      Are the separation routines exact?

      No. Searches 1–6 are greedy heuristics of polynomial time, at most $O(n^3)$ (Remark 3.1). If they fail, a violated inequality may still exist, and the algorithm branches. Exact separation of rounded capacity inequalities is NP-hard in general, which is why heuristics are standard.

      How is the branching done?

      On a fractional variable, as in Achuthan et al. (1996); the paper refers there for forced variables, branching-variable choice and backtracking (p. 159). Branching happens when no cut is found or after tailing off (six rounds with improvement below 2.5 × 10⁻⁵).
