1 · The problem

One depot, seven customers, trucks that carry 10

  • Depot = node 1 (black square). Every truck starts and ends there.
  • Customer $j$ wants $q_j$ (next to each circle). A truck carries $Q = 10$.
  • Visit each customer once, never overload a truck, drive the least distance.

Total demand 28, so at least $\lceil 28/10 \rceil = 3$ trucks. This is the capacitated vehicle routing problem.

Key point: the rules of the game. Click customer 2: demand 9, nearly a full truck by itself. Click 4: demand 1. Real uses: deliveries, garbage collection, school buses.

1 · The problem

Let's plan the routes by hand

Let's join customers whose shared trip saves the most distance, until a truck is full.

  • Customer 2 nearly fills a truck alone: 1 → 2 → 1, load 9.
  • Next truck: 1 → 7 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 1, load 10.
  • Last truck: 1 → 6 → 8 → 1, load 9. Total 322.4.

A good plan. Is it the best one?

Key point: a quick method gives a good plan, but no guarantee. Each → adds the next route on the map. This is the savings heuristic, the paper's own starting plan (Paessens' version). Afterwards you can click customers to build a plan live if someone asks.

1 · The problem

A good plan is not a proof

  • The best plan costs 318.2, 1.3% shorter.
  • Only 354 plans here: we checked them all. With 50 customers: hopeless.

An exact method proves optimality: push the lower bound up until it meets the plan.

Key point: upper bound = a plan we have; lower bound = nothing is cheaper; when they meet, we are done. That sentence is the whole talk. Point at the three boxes under the maps.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Let's describe a plan by the roads it uses

For each pair $i \lt j$, one number $x_{ij}$: how many times a truck drives between $i$ and $j$.

  • 1 if driven once, 0 if not; 2 for an out-and-back trip: 1 → 2 → 1 is $x_{12} = 2$.
  • No truck, no direction, only roads: the two-index model.
  • Pairs that cannot share a truck ($q_i + q_j \gt Q$) get no variable.
Key point: one number per road. Click a road on the right to show its value and length. The smaller index comes first: x56, never x65. The toy has 23 variables plus m, the number of trucks. Customer 2 (q = 9) can only pair with customer 4 (q = 1).

2 · Writing it for a computer

What we minimise: total distance

For every road: its length times how many times it is driven. Add up.

(1.1) $$\min \; \sum_{i \lt j} \htmlClass{tA}{\underbrace{c_{ij}}_{\text{length}}} \; \htmlClass{tB}{\underbrace{x_{ij}}_{\text{times driven}}}$$

Best plan: $97.8 + 93.4 + 127.0 = 318.2$.

Key point: the objective is just total distance. The trip to customer 2 counts its road twice (2 × 48.9 = 97.8).

2 · Writing it for a computer

Rule 1: one truck in, one truck out

At a customer, one truck arrives and one leaves: its roads add up to 2. At the depot, every truck leaves and returns: $2m$.

(1.2) and (1.3) $$\htmlClass{tA}{\underbrace{\sum_{j \in \mathscr C} x_{1j}}_{\text{roads at the depot}}} = 2\,\htmlClass{tB}{\underbrace{m}_{\text{trucks}}} \qquad \htmlClass{tC}{\underbrace{\sum_{i \lt k} x_{ik} + \sum_{j \gt k} x_{kj}}_{\text{roads at customer } k}} = 2$$

These are the degree constraints. Let's check them on the best plan.

Key point: degrees count trucks in and out. Click customer 4: x34 + x48 = 2. Click the depot: 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 6 = 2 × 3. Customer 2 gets its 2 from x12 alone. 𝒞 = {2,…,8} is the set of customers.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Is rule 1 enough? No.

With only (1.1)–(1.3), the cheapest answer costs 246.0: below the best plan.

  • Rule 1 holds at every node.
  • But 3–4–5–6 is a loop that never visits the depot, and it carries 11 > 10.

We need a rule that forbids such loops.

Key point: degrees alone allow subtours. A truck with no garage, and overloaded. The rest of the model exists to forbid pictures like this.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Rule 2: a group of customers needs enough trucks

A group $S$ needs at least $\ell(S) = \lceil q(S)/Q \rceil$ trucks. Each truck covers a run of $S$'s customers, and a run of $r$ has $r - 1$ roads inside.

(1.4) $$\htmlClass{tA}{\underbrace{\sum_{i,j \in S} x_{ij}}_{\text{roads inside } S}} \;\le\; \htmlClass{tB}{\underbrace{|S|}_{\text{customers}}} - \htmlClass{tC}{\underbrace{\ell(S)}_{\text{trucks } S \text{ needs}}}$$

Loop $\{3,4,5,6\}$: 4 roads inside, only $4 - \lceil 11/10 \rceil = 2$ allowed. Broken: that answer is gone.

Key point: one rule handles both subtours and capacity. Switch to the best plan: satisfied. Click 3 and 6 away and add 7 to try other groups. Even a light loop is caught: ℓ ≥ 1 allows |S| − 1 roads, and a closed loop has |S|.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Too many rules to write down

  • One rule (1.4) per group: 99 groups for 7 customers, about $10^{30}$ for 100.
  • So: start without them, solve, and add only the rules the answer breaks.

A rule added because the answer breaks it is a cut: the "cut" in branch-and-cut.

Key point: the model is exact but exponentially large, so it is used lazily. Drag the slider to 100 customers.

3 · How the computer solves it

A lower bound: allow fractions

Let roads be used "half-way" ($x = \tfrac12$): a linear program (LP), which computers solve fast.

  • More options than real plans, so its value is a lower bound: $246.0 \le$ best $\le 322.4$.

The game: push the lower bound up. Cuts do that.

Key point: the LP value is a lower bound. On the right: the blue point is what we can prove, the orange dashed line is the best plan we hold. The gap is what is left to prove.

3 · How the computer solves it

Let's watch the cutting-plane loop

  1. Solve the LP.
  2. Find rules the answer breaks.
  3. Add them as cuts. Repeat.

Each round removes the current wrong answer, and the bound climbs.

Each cut's group lights up on the map; click a cut in the list to see another.

Key point: every round removes a "cheat" and raises the bound. Each → is one round: 246.0 → 293.1 → 310.8 → 315.7 → 317.3 → 317.35 → 317.75 → 318.0. Round 0: the 3-4-5-6 loop, cut by (1.4). Some cuts are the paper's new ones, which we meet in Part 4. After 7 rounds the bound is 318.0, only 0.2 below the best plan.

3 · How the computer solves it

When cuts run out: branch

At 318.0 no rule is broken, but road 3–4 is used half-way.

  • Split: road 3–4 unused ($x_{34} = 0$) or used ($x_{34} = 1$).
  • Unused: more cuts, bound 319.15, still fractional: split again.
  • Used: a real plan, 318.2. New best plan.
  • Both halves of the first branch have bound 319.15 > 318.2: pruned.

Lower bound = upper bound = 318.2, proven optimal.

Key point: branching finishes the proof; good cuts keep the tree small. Every real plan is in one of the two halves, so nothing is lost by splitting. Cuts + branching = branch-and-cut.

4 · What the paper adds · the starting point

A known rule: leave enough trucks for everyone else

Trucks that serve only customers of $S$ are useless to the others, who still need $\ell(\bar S)$ trucks.

(1.7) $$\htmlClass{tA}{\underbrace{x(S)}_{\text{roads inside}}} + \htmlClass{tB}{\underbrace{x(1{:}S)}_{\text{depot roads into } S}} - \htmlClass{tC}{\underbrace{m}_{\text{trucks}}} \;\le\; |S| - \htmlClass{tD}{\underbrace{\ell(\bar S)}_{\text{trucks the others need}}}$$

Starting LP, $S = \{2,7,8\}$: all three trucks are busy there, none left for the other four. 3 > 1: broken.

Key point: the old rules, before this paper. (1.4) is Laporte et al. 1985 (Algorithm B in the results); (1.7) is the same authors' 1996 paper (Algorithm A). Why (1.7) holds: a truck serving only r customers of S uses (r − 1) + 2 = r + 1 counted roads, so the left side is |S| + (trucks only in S) − m; mixed trucks count less. Numbers: 0 + 6 − 3 = 3 against 3 − ℓ({3,4,5,6}) = 1.

4 · What the paper adds · new cut 1

Groups that cannot share a truck

If $S$ with any two of the groups $T_1, \dots, T_k$ overloads a truck, the truck through $S$ can reach at most one of them.

(2.3) $$3\,\htmlClass{tA}{\underbrace{x(S)}_{\text{inside } S}} + \htmlClass{tB}{\underbrace{x(E[S,T])}_{S \text{ to the } T\text{'s}}} + \htmlClass{tC}{\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_p x(T_p)}_{\text{inside each } T_p}} \;\le\; 3|S| - 2 + |T| - k$$

$S = \{3\}$, $T = \{4\}, \{5,6\}$: $3 + 1 + 7 = 11 \gt 10$. The starting LP links 3 to both: 3 > 2, broken, though no single group is overloaded.

Key point: a rule about several groups at once, which (1.4) cannot see. |T| = customers in all T's, k = number of groups. Roads 3–4, 3–6 and 5–6: left side 0 + 2 + 1 = 3, right side 3 − 2 + 3 − 2 = 2. Best plan: 3 links only to 4, left side 2, satisfied. (2.8) is the sharper version for exactly three groups.

4 · What the paper adds · why it matters

Where the old rules run dry

Round 5 of our loop, bound 317.35: no (1.4), (1.7), (2.1) or (2.9) is broken. The old method would have to branch here.

  • The new (2.8) still finds a cut: $S = \{4,5\}$ with $\{3\}, \{6\}, \{8\}$, loads 11, 12, 13, all over 10.
  • $2x(S) + x(E[S,T]) + \sum_p x(T_p) \le 2|S| + |T| - 4$: here $3.5 \gt 3$.

That is how the new cuts lift the root bound to 318.0 instead of 317.35.

Key point: this is the paper's contribution in one picture. The old cuts stall; the new cut keeps the bound climbing. Left side: 2 × 1 + 1.5 + 0 = 3.5; right side 2·2 + 3 − 4 = 3.

4 · What the paper adds · new cut 2

Don't split a group that fits in one truck

If $S$ fits in one truck, joining two trips that serve only $S$ is never longer.

(2.9), for $q(S) \le Q$ $$\htmlClass{tA}{\underbrace{x(S)}_{\text{roads inside } S}} + \htmlClass{tB}{\underbrace{x(1{:}S)}_{\text{depot roads into } S}} \;\le\; |S| + 1$$

Two trips to 7 and 8: 77.0. One trip 1 → 7 → 8 → 1: 68.9, load $8 \le 10$.

A new kind of rule: it removes some real (wasteful) plans but always keeps an optimal one. An optimality cut.

Key point: rules justified by what an optimal plan looks like. Costs: 2(5.1) + 2(33.4) = 77.0 vs 5.1 + 30.4 + 33.4 = 68.9 (triangle inequality). One trip through S uses |S| + 1 roads; two trips use one more. Switch "two trips" (4 > 3, broken) and "one trip" (3 ≤ 3). Caveat: needs a free fleet or the minimum fleet.

4 · What the paper adds · new cut 3

A cap on the number of trucks

In an optimal plan, any two routes together are heavier than $Q$, or we would merge them. So each route carries more than $Q/2$ on average.

Corollary 2.5, from (2.10) $$m \;\le\; \Bigg\lfloor \frac{2\,(\htmlClass{tA}{\underbrace{q(\mathscr C)}_{\text{total demand}}} + \delta)}{\htmlClass{tB}{\underbrace{Q}_{\text{capacity}}} + 1 + \delta} \Bigg\rfloor$$

Toy: $\lfloor 2 \cdot 29 / 12 \rfloor = 4$, and at least 3 are needed: the fleet is 3 or 4.

Key point: the same merging idea caps the fleet. δ = 1 if Q is even, 0 if odd (it makes the rounding exact). Loads 9, 10, 9: every pair is above 10. (2.10) applies the same count to any group too heavy for one truck: x(S) + x(1:S) ≤ |S| + ⌊2(q(S) + δ)/(Q + 1 + δ)⌋.

5 · The paper's results

What the paper reports

  • 1,650 random problems with 15–100 customers, plus 24 from the literature; 1 hour of CPU each on a 1990s workstation.
  • Three algorithms: NEW (all cuts, Searches 1–6), A = (1.4) + (1.7), B = (1.4) only.
  • NEW solves 1,369 of 1,590 problems, against 1,239 for A and 1,107 for B, in about half of B's total time.
  • The gain is largest when trucks are tight, and vanishes when one truck carries everything.
Key point: the new cuts let more problems finish within the hour. A is Achuthan et al. 1996, B is Laporte et al. 1985. Most of the time saved comes from solving more problems (an unsolved one costs the full hour). When one truck carries everything it is a travelling salesman problem, and the new cuts never fire.

6 · Our implementation

How we reproduced the results

  1. Rebuild the test problems. The authors' problems were never published, so we regenerated all 1,650 with the recipe on p. 161 and fixed seeds.
  2. Run NEW, A and B on the same problems with identical settings (one Gurobi thread, same time limit).
  3. Check every answer: routes rebuilt and re-costed; the same optimum as CVRPLIB on all 18 benchmarks we solved; brute-force tests that no cut removes a real plan.
  4. Compare with the paper table by table.
Key point: same recipe, same three algorithms, every answer checked. Recipe (tab on the right): integer points in a 1,000 × 1,000 square, depot at the origin, demands uniform on 1–100, capacity Q = (1 − α)·max q + α·Σq for α = 0, 0.33, 0.67, 1; 55 cells of 30 problems. 2,286 returned solutions all pass the route check. 7 · Wrap-up

Strengths and limits

Strengths

  • Optimality cuts (2.9)/(2.10): rules justified by what an optimal plan looks like, not only by feasibility. A new idea.
  • A large, systematic test, with a clear comparison against the two earlier algorithms.

Limits

  • The test problems were not published, so others cannot rerun them exactly.
  • (2.9)/(2.10) need triangle-inequality distances and a free (or minimal) fleet.
  • Later methods (Lysgaard et al. 2004; Fukasawa et al. 2006; Pecin et al. 2017) add more cuts and column generation and solve much larger problems.
Key point: a real contribution with clear conditions. Small slips in the text if asked: Q_j for q_j in (3.4), Search 3's formula labelled Z''_2, "Theorem 1" in Search 5. Baldacci et al. 2008 is another later method.

7 · Wrap-up

Three things to remember

  1. Model: one number per road. Rule 1: one truck in, one out. Rule 2: a group needs enough trucks, too many rules to write, so they are added as cuts.
  2. Method: the LP gives a lower bound, cuts push it up, branching finishes; stop when it meets the best plan.
  3. Paper: new cuts raise the bound, so trees shrink and more problems are solved. We reproduced this.

Backup · likely question

"(2.9) cuts off optimal solutions. Can the algorithm miss the optimum?"

No. (2.9) can remove some optimal plans (Remark 2.2), but Theorem 2.3 guarantees that at least one optimal plan satisfies all of them, so the optimal value is still found.

Proof idea (Theorem 2.3):

  1. Suppose an optimal plan breaks (2.9) for a group $S$ with $q(S) \le Q$. Then two whole routes stay inside $S$.
  2. Join them end to end: the load is at most $q(S) \le Q$, and by the triangle inequality the cost does not go up.
  3. The new plan is still optimal and has one fewer violation. Repeat until none is left.
Say it calmly: this is an existence theorem. "Some optimal solution survives; that is all an exact method needs." Our brute-force tests confirmed it on 60 random problems.

Backup · likely question

"What if distances break the triangle inequality, or the fleet is fixed?"

  • No triangle inequality: joining two routes can cost more, the proof breaks, and (2.9) could cut off every optimal plan. The paper assumes it throughout (p. 154); Euclidean distances satisfy it.
  • Fixed fleet $m$: safe only if $m$ is the minimum fleet. With more trucks than needed it can make the problem infeasible (Remark 2.3). Our code switches (2.9) on only in the safe cases.
  • Other dominance rules together with (2.9)? Not without a new proof: each rule keeps some optimal plan, possibly a different one (Remark 2.6).
The literature problems in Tables 4.5–4.6 have fixed m; that is where this matters.

Backup · likely questions

Questions about the model

  • Why two-index, not three-index or set partitioning? One variable per road and no symmetry between identical trucks. The price: capacity must be written as exponentially many group rules, added as cuts. Set partitioning gives stronger bounds but needs column generation.
  • Is $\ell(S) = \lceil q(S)/Q \rceil$ the true number of trucks? No, a lower bound. Three customers of demand 6 with $Q = 10$ need 3 trucks, not 2. The rule stays valid, just weaker.
  • (1.7) equals (1.4) for the other customers. What is new? Nothing in the model; the gain is in finding cuts: a small group $S$ can reveal a broken rule on its large complement.
Why x_1j may be 2: a one-customer route uses the depot road twice; this keeps the model undirected.

Backup · likely question

"Are the searches exact? How is branching done?"

  • Not exact. Searches 1–6 are greedy heuristics, each at most $O(n^3)$ (Remark 3.1). Exact separation of these capacity rules is NP-hard in general, so heuristics are standard.
  • If no cut is found, a broken rule may still exist; the algorithm then branches, so the answer is still exact.
  • Branching on a fractional road, as in Achuthan et al. (1996). It also branches after tailing off: six rounds in a row with almost no bound improvement.
Tailing-off threshold: improvement below 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ for six rounds (p. 159).

Backup · if asked how cuts are found

Finding broken rules fast: Searches 1–6

There are far too many groups to test them all, so the paper uses six quick greedy searches on the LP picture.

  • Start from the connected pieces of the LP answer (roads with $x \gt 0$). In our example: $\{2\}$, $\{3,4,5,6\}$, $\{7\}$, $\{8\}$. Each piece is a candidate $S$.
  • Searches 1–2: shrink a piece while the rule stays broken: (1.4), (2.1), (2.9).
  • Search 3: try unions of pieces for (1.7) and (2.9).
  • Searches 4–6 (new): grow groups by merging neighbours, and look for the "$S$ plus groups $T$" pattern of (2.3)/(2.8).

Fixed order of checks, with Searches 4–6 only when 1–3 find nothing. Each search costs at most $O(n^3)$. The searches are heuristics: they can miss a broken rule, which branching then covers.

Keep this short unless asked. The key message: separation is heuristic; the paper's contribution is both new inequalities and searches that find them.

Backup · likely question

"Why is (1.7) the same as (1.4) for the other customers?"

Using only the degree rules, with $\bar S$ = the other customers:

  1. Every road has two ends: customers give $2|\mathscr C|$, the depot $2m$, so the total road value is $|\mathscr C| + m$.
  2. Summing (1.3) over $\bar S$ and subtracting gives $x(S) + x(1{:}S) - m = |S| - |\bar S| + x(\bar S)$.
  3. So (1.7) for $S$ reads $x(\bar S) \le |\bar S| - \ell(\bar S)$: exactly (1.4) for $\bar S$.

Same LP, different view: a small set near the depot exposes a broken rule on its large complement. Toy: $\{2,7,8\}$ and $\{3,4,5,6\}$ are both broken by 2.

The gain is in finding cuts, not in the model: heuristic searches build small sets, and (1.7) lets a small set reveal the violation of its large complement.

Backup · likely question

"Why does the three-set cut (2.3) hold?"

Take a real plan. Inside $S$ the routes form $r$ separate paths, so $x(S) \le |S| - r$, and $\sum_p x(T_p) \le |T| - k$.

  • $r = 1$: only the two ends leave $S$, so $x(E[S,T]) \le 2$. If every bound were tight, one truck would carry $S$ and two $T$'s, which the condition forbids, so one bound has slack 1: $3(|S| - 1) + 2 + |T| - k - 1$.
  • $r \ge 2$: each path meets $T$ in at most 2 roads: $3(|S| - r) + 2r + |T| - k \le 3|S| - 2 + |T| - k$.

(2.8) plus $x(S) \le |S| - 1$ gives (2.3) with $k = 3$, so (2.8) is the stronger one. Useful when $\ell(S) = 1$ and $k \ge 3$ (Remark 2.1).

The factor 3 on x(S) handles S being split into several paths.

Backup · likely question

"Where do your results differ from the paper's, and why?"

  • Times 10–1,000× smaller: about 30 years of hardware and LP-solver progress, not the method.
  • A and B close: with our fixed root loop, (1.7) adds little that (1.4) and Search 1 don't already find.
  • More nodes on the tightest problems: the paper re-separates at every node until no cut is left and adds Gomory cuts; Gurobi gives our callback one or two rounds per node, so we branch more.
  • Slightly lower root bounds when one truck carries all: we add no Gomory cuts.
Different random problems too: same recipe, different draws, so cell averages compare statistically. With 30 per cell our root averages match within a few tenths of a point.
