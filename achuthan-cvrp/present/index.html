1 · The problem

One depot, seven customers, identical trucks

  • The depot (the black square, node 1) holds the goods. Every truck starts and ends there.
  • Customers 2–8 each want an amount $q_j$, written next to each circle.
  • Every truck carries at most $Q = 10$.

Find routes so that every customer is visited exactly once, no truck carries more than $Q$, and every route starts and ends at the depot.

Goal: the least total distance driven.

This is the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP): deliveries, garbage collection, school buses.

Tap customer 2 on the right: demand 9, so it almost fills a truck by itself. Tap 4: demand 1. Total demand is 28 and Q is 10, so at least 3 trucks are needed.

1 · The problem

Let's plan the routes by hand

We pick customers in the order a truck visits them. When a truck is full, the next one starts.

  • For each truck we keep an eye on its load against $Q = 10$ and the distance it drives.
  • A standard quick method (a savings heuristic) gets 322.4.
  • The best possible plan costs 318.2. Let's see how close we get.

Finding a good plan is easy. Knowing that nothing better exists is the hard part.

Build one route live: 1 → 3 → 4 → 8. Load 9. Then customer 2 alone (9). Then 6, 5, 7 (load 10). Total 318.2. Ask: how would you convince someone that no plan beats 318.2?

1 · The problem

A good plan is not a proof

Let's take a look at two plans for the same customers.

  • Heuristic plan: 322.4. Quick, sensible, no guarantee.
  • Best plan: 318.2, about 1.3% shorter.
  • This toy has only 354 possible plans, so we could check them all. With 50 customers the number of plans is astronomically large: listing them is impossible.

The paper's question: how do we prove a plan is the best without listing every plan? Answer: compute a lower bound, a number no plan can beat, and push it up until it meets the best plan we know.

Upper bound = cost of a plan we have (322.4 or 318.2). Lower bound = something we can prove about every plan. When the two meet, we are done. That sentence is the whole talk.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Describe a plan by the roads it uses

For every pair of locations $i \lt j$, one number $x_{ij}$: how many times a truck drives between $i$ and $j$.

  • $x_{ij} = 1$ if the road is driven once, $0$ if not.
  • $x_{1j} = 2$ only for a depot road, when a truck serves customer $j$ alone: out and back. Route 1 → 2 → 1 is $x_{12} = 2$.
  • We do not record which truck or which direction, only which roads. Two indices, $i$ and $j$: the two-index model.
  • Two customers that cannot share a truck ($q_i + q_j \gt Q$) get no variable at all. Customer 2 ($q = 9$) can only pair with customer 4 ($q = 1$).
Tap a road on the right: it shows its x value and length. Point out x12 = 2. The smaller index always comes first, so it is x56, never x65. The toy has 23 such variables plus m, the number of trucks.

2 · Writing it for a computer

What we minimise: total distance

In words: for every road, its length times how many times it is driven; add these up.

(1.1) objective $$\min \; \sum_{i \lt j} \htmlClass{tA}{c_{ij}} \; \htmlClass{tB}{x_{ij}}$$
  • $c_{ij}$: length of the road between $i$ and $j$
  • $x_{ij}$: times it is driven (0, 1, or 2 for a depot road)

Best plan: $2(48.9)$ for the out-and-back trip to customer 2, plus the two loops: $97.8 + 127.0 + 93.4 = 318.2$.

Point at the per-route costs on the right. The x12 = 2 term counts the road to customer 2 twice: once out, once back.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Rule 1: one truck in, one truck out

Stand at a customer: one truck arrives and one leaves, so its roads add up to 2. Stand at the depot: each of the $m$ trucks leaves once and returns once, so its roads add up to $2m$.

(1.2) depot · (1.3) each customer $k$ $$\sum_{j \in \mathscr C} \htmlClass{tA}{x_{1j}} = 2\,\htmlClass{tB}{m} \qquad\qquad \htmlClass{tC}{\sum_{i \lt k} x_{ik} + \sum_{j \gt k} x_{kj}} = 2$$
  • roads at the depot; $\mathscr C = \{2,\dots,8\}$ is the set of customers
  • $m$: number of trucks, chosen by the model
  • all roads touching customer $k$ (two sums only because the smaller index is written first)

These are the degree constraints. Let's check them node by node on the best plan.

Tap customer 4: x34 + x48 = 2. Tap the depot: 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 6 = 2 × 3. Tap customer 2: x12 = 2 alone.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Is rule 1 enough? No.

Give the computer only (1.1)–(1.3) and ask for the cheapest $x$.

  • It answers 246.0, cheaper than the best real plan (318.2). Something is wrong.
  • Every customer has its 2 and the depot has $6 = 2 \cdot 3$: rule 1 holds everywhere.
  • But customers 3, 4, 5, 6 form a loop that never visits the depot: a truck with no garage.
  • And that loop carries $3 + 1 + 3 + 4 = 11 \gt 10$: too heavy for one truck anyway.

We need a rule that forbids loops away from the depot and overloaded trucks.

This is the classic subtour. The rest of the model exists to forbid pictures like this one.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Rule 2: a group of customers needs enough trucks

Pick any group $S$ of customers. Together they need at least $\ell(S) = \lceil q(S)/Q \rceil$ trucks (total demand divided by capacity, rounded up).

Each truck visits a run of $S$'s customers; a run of $r$ uses $r - 1$ roads inside $S$. So at most $|S| - \ell(S)$ roads lie inside.

(1.4) capacity / subtour rule, every group $S$ $$\htmlClass{tA}{\sum_{i,j \in S} x_{ij}} \;\le\; \htmlClass{tB}{|S|} - \htmlClass{tC}{\ell(S)}$$
  • $x(S)$: roads with both ends in $S$
  • number of customers in $S$
  • $\ell(S)$: fewest trucks $S$ needs

The loop $S = \{3,4,5,6\}$ has 4 roads inside, but only $4 - \lceil 11/10 \rceil = 2$ are allowed. Broken: that answer is forbidden.

Show on the right: S = {3,4,5,6} is broken on the starting answer. Switch to the best plan: satisfied. Tap 3 and 6 away and add 7 to try other groups. Also say: even a light loop is caught, since ℓ ≥ 1 allows |S| − 1 roads and a closed loop has |S|.

2 · Writing it for a computer

Too many rules to write down

Rule (1.4) is one inequality per group of customers.

  • 7 customers: 99 groups of size 3 or more.
  • 100 customers: about $1.3 \times 10^{30}$ groups. Nobody can write them all.
  • Instead: start without them. Solve. If the answer breaks one, add that one and solve again.

An inequality added because the current answer breaks it is called a cut: it cuts the wrong answer off. That is the "cut" in branch-and-cut.

Drag the slider. The point: the model is exact but exponentially large, so it can only be used lazily.

3 · How the computer solves it

A lower bound: allow fractions

Solving with whole numbers is hard. If we let roads be used "half-way" ($x = 0.5$), the problem becomes a linear program (LP), which computers solve very fast.

  • The LP chooses from more options than real plans, so its cheapest answer can only be lower or equal to the best real plan.
  • So: LP value ≤ best plan ≤ any plan we have. Here $246.0 \le \text{best} \le 322.4$.
  • If the two ends meet, the plan we have is proven best.

The whole game is to push the lower bound up as fast as possible. Cuts do that.

The bar on the right: blue is what we can prove (lower bound), orange is the best plan we hold (upper bound). The gap is what is left to prove.

3 · How the computer solves it

The cutting-plane loop

  1. Solve the LP.
  2. Look for rules the answer breaks.
  3. Add them as cuts.
  4. Solve again. Repeat until no broken rule is found.

Let's watch it run. Each round removes the current wrong answer and the bound climbs: 246.0 → 293.1 → 310.8 → 315.7 → 317.3 → 317.35 → 317.75 → 318.0.

Every cut is about a group of customers, which lights up on the map. Some are rule (1.4); others are the paper's new rules, which we meet in Part 4.

Step through live. Round 0: the 3-4-5-6 loop, cut by (1.4). Watch how each new answer is a different "cheat" and gets cut. After 7 rounds the bound is 318.0: only 0.2 below the best plan.

3 · How the computer solves it

When cuts run out: branch

At 318.0 no rule is broken, but road 3–4 is used half-way ($x_{34} = \tfrac12$).

  • Split into two smaller problems: road 3–4 is not used ($x_{34} = 0$), or it is ($x_{34} = 1$). Every real plan is in one of them.
  • $x_{34} = 1$: the LP answer is a real plan, cost 318.2. New best plan.
  • $x_{34} = 0$: after more cuts its bound is 319.15. It splits again, but both halves have bound $319.15 \gt 318.2$: they cannot beat what we have, so they are pruned.

Nothing left to explore: lower bound = upper bound = 318.2, proven optimal. Cuts + branching = branch-and-cut.

Step through the tree. The tree is small here because the cuts already brought the bound within 0.2 of the answer. Better cuts mean smaller trees: that is the paper's whole point.

4 · What the paper adds · the starting point

A known rule: leave enough trucks for everyone else

Suppose $r$ trucks serve only customers of $S$. Those trucks are useless to the other customers $\bar S$, who still need $\ell(\bar S)$ trucks. So $m - r \ge \ell(\bar S)$.

(1.7) any group $S$, with $\bar S$ = the other customers $$\htmlClass{tA}{x(S)} + \htmlClass{tB}{x(1{:}S)} - \htmlClass{tC}{m} \;\le\; |S| - \htmlClass{tD}{\ell(\bar S)}$$
  • roads inside $S$
  • depot roads into $S$
  • trucks in total
  • trucks the other customers need

Starting LP, $S = \{2,7,8\}$: all three trucks are busy with 2, 7 and 8, none left for the other four. Left side 3, right side 1. Broken.

Before this paper: (1.4) from Laporte et al. (1985) and (1.7) from the same authors' 1996 paper; (1.7) is Algorithm A in the results. Why it works: a truck serving only r customers of S uses (r−1) + 2 = r + 1 of the counted roads, so the left side is |S| + (trucks only in S) − m; mixed trucks count less. Numbers: 0 + 6 − 3 = 3 against 3 − ℓ({3,4,5,6}) = 1.

4 · What the paper adds · new cut 1

Groups that cannot share a truck

Take a group $S$ and other groups $T_1, \dots, T_k$ such that $S$ together with any two of the $T$'s is too heavy for one truck. Then the truck through $S$ can pick up at most one of the $T$'s.

Example: $S = \{3\}$, $T_1 = \{4\}$, $T_2 = \{5,6\}$. Together $3 + 1 + 7 = 11 \gt 10$, so customer 3's route cannot reach both.

(2.3), Theorem 2.1 $$3\,\htmlClass{tA}{x(S)} + \htmlClass{tB}{x(E[S,T])} + \htmlClass{tC}{\sum_{p} x(T_p)} \;\le\; 3|S| - 2 + |T| - k$$
  • roads inside $S$ (counted 3 times)
  • roads between $S$ and the $T$'s
  • roads inside each $T_p$; $|T|$ = customers in all $T$'s, $k$ = number of groups

Starting LP: one chain links 3 to both groups. Left side 3, right side 2. Broken, though no single group is overloaded, so (1.4) misses it.

Roads 3–4, 3–6 and 5–6 are all used: left side 0 + 2 + 1 = 3, right side 3·1 − 2 + 3 − 2 = 2. Switch to the best plan: 3 links only to 4, left side 2, satisfied. The idea is new: it reasons about several sets at once. (2.8) is a sharper version for exactly three T's.

4 · What the paper adds · new cut 2

Don't split a group that fits in one truck

If the customers of $S$ fit in one truck, an optimal plan never needs two separate trips that serve only $S$: joining them is never longer.

Two trips to 7 and 8 cost 77.0; one trip 1 → 7 → 8 → 1 costs 68.9 and carries $8 \le 10$.

(2.9), Theorem 2.3, groups with $q(S) \le Q$ $$\htmlClass{tA}{x(S)} + \htmlClass{tB}{x(1{:}S)} \;\le\; |S| + 1$$
  • roads inside $S$
  • depot roads into $S$

A new kind of rule: it removes some real (wasteful) plans but always keeps an optimal one. An optimality cut.

Costs: 2(5.1) + 2(33.4) = 77.0 versus 5.1 + 30.4 + 33.4 = 68.9 (triangle inequality). One trip through all of S uses (|S| − 1) + 2 = |S| + 1 roads; two trips use one more. Switch between "two trips" (0 + 4 = 4 > 3, broken) and "one trip" (1 + 2 = 3 ≤ 3, satisfied). Caveat for Q&A: it needs the number of trucks to be free (variable m), or fixed at the minimum fleet.

4 · What the paper adds · new cut 3

A cap on the number of trucks

Same idea for bigger groups: in an optimal plan, any two routes together are heavier than $Q$. Otherwise we could merge them into one shorter route.

So on average a route carries more than $Q/2$, which caps the fleet (Corollary 2.5, from inequality (2.10)):

$$m \;\le\; \left\lfloor \frac{2\,(\htmlClass{tA}{q(\mathscr C)} + \delta)}{\htmlClass{tB}{Q} + 1 + \delta} \right\rfloor$$
  • total demand of all customers
  • truck capacity; $\delta = 1$ if $Q$ is even, $0$ if odd (makes the rounding exact)

Toy: $\lfloor 2(28 + 1)/(10 + 1 + 1) \rfloor = \lfloor 4.83 \rfloor = 4$. With at least $\lceil 28/10 \rceil = 3$ trucks needed, the fleet is 3 or 4.

(2.10) applies the same count to every group $S$ too heavy for one truck: $x(S) + x(1{:}S) \le |S| + \lfloor 2(q(S) + \delta)/(Q + 1 + \delta) \rfloor$.

On the right: loads 9, 10, 9. Every pair adds to more than 10, as the theorem says.

5 · The paper's results

What the paper reports

  • 1,650 random problems with 15–100 customers, plus 24 from the literature; 1 hour of CPU each on a 1990s workstation.
  • Three algorithms: NEW (all cuts, Searches 1–6), A = (1.4) + (1.7), B = (1.4) only.
  • NEW solves 1,369 of 1,590 problems, against 1,239 for A and 1,107 for B, in about half of B's total time.
  • The gain is largest when trucks are tight, and vanishes when one truck carries everything.
Key point: the new cuts let more problems finish within the hour. A is Achuthan et al. 1996, B is Laporte et al. 1985. Most of the time saved comes from solving more problems (an unsolved one costs the full hour). When one truck carries everything it is a travelling salesman problem, and the new cuts never fire.

  2. Run NEW, A and B on the same problems with identical settings (one Gurobi thread, same time limit).
  3. Check every answer: routes rebuilt and re-costed; the same optimum as CVRPLIB on all 18 benchmarks we solved; brute-force tests that no cut removes a real plan.
  4. Compare with the paper table by table.

7 · Wrap-up

Strengths and limits

Strengths

  • Optimality cuts (2.9)/(2.10): rules justified by what an optimal plan looks like, not only by feasibility. A new idea.
  • A large, systematic test, with a clear comparison against the two earlier algorithms.

Limits

  • The test problems were not published, so others cannot rerun them exactly.
  • (2.9)/(2.10) need triangle-inequality distances and a free (or minimal) fleet.
  • Later methods (Lysgaard et al. 2004; Fukasawa et al. 2006; Pecin et al. 2017) add more cuts and column generation and solve much larger problems.
Key point: a real contribution with clear conditions. Small slips in the text if asked: Q_j for q_j in (3.4), Search 3's formula labelled Z''_2, "Theorem 1" in Search 5. Baldacci et al. 2008 is another later method.

7 · Wrap-up

Three things to remember

  1. Model: one number per road. Rule 1: one truck in, one out. Rule 2: a group needs enough trucks, too many rules to write, so they are added as cuts.
  2. Method: the LP gives a lower bound, cuts push it up, branching finishes; stop when it meets the best plan.
  3. Paper: new cuts raise the bound, so trees shrink and more problems are solved. We reproduced this.

Backup · likely question

"(2.9) cuts off optimal solutions. Can the algorithm miss the optimum?"

No. (2.9) can remove some optimal plans (Remark 2.2), but Theorem 2.3 guarantees that at least one optimal plan satisfies all of them, so the optimal value is still found.

Proof idea (Theorem 2.3):

  1. Suppose an optimal plan breaks (2.9) for a group $S$ with $q(S) \le Q$. Then two whole routes stay inside $S$.
  2. Join them end to end: the load is at most $q(S) \le Q$, and by the triangle inequality the cost does not go up.
  3. The new plan is still optimal and has one fewer violation. Repeat until none is left.
Say it calmly: this is an existence theorem. "Some optimal solution survives; that is all an exact method needs." Our brute-force tests confirmed it on 60 random problems.

Backup · likely question

"What if distances break the triangle inequality, or the fleet is fixed?"

  • No triangle inequality: joining two routes can cost more, the proof breaks, and (2.9) could cut off every optimal plan. The paper assumes it throughout (p. 154); Euclidean distances satisfy it.
  • Fixed fleet $m$: safe only if $m$ is the minimum fleet. With more trucks than needed it can make the problem infeasible (Remark 2.3). Our code switches (2.9) on only in the safe cases.
  • Other dominance rules together with (2.9)? Not without a new proof: each rule keeps some optimal plan, possibly a different one (Remark 2.6).
The literature problems in Tables 4.5–4.6 have fixed m; that is where this matters.

Backup · likely questions

Questions about the model

  • Why two-index, not three-index or set partitioning? One variable per road and no symmetry between identical trucks. The price: capacity must be written as exponentially many group rules, added as cuts. Set partitioning gives stronger bounds but needs column generation.
  • Is $\ell(S) = \lceil q(S)/Q \rceil$ the true number of trucks? No, a lower bound. Three customers of demand 6 with $Q = 10$ need 3 trucks, not 2. The rule stays valid, just weaker.
  • (1.7) equals (1.4) for the other customers. What is new? Nothing in the model; the gain is in finding cuts: a small group $S$ can reveal a broken rule on its large complement.
Why x_1j may be 2: a one-customer route uses the depot road twice; this keeps the model undirected.

Backup · likely question

"Are the searches exact? How is branching done?"

  • Not exact. Searches 1–6 are greedy heuristics, each at most $O(n^3)$ (Remark 3.1). Exact separation of these capacity rules is NP-hard in general, so heuristics are standard.
  • If no cut is found, a broken rule may still exist; the algorithm then branches, so the answer is still exact.
  • Branching on a fractional road, as in Achuthan et al. (1996). It also branches after tailing off: six rounds in a row with almost no bound improvement.
Tailing-off threshold: improvement below 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ for six rounds (p. 159).

Backup · if asked how cuts are found

Finding broken rules fast: Searches 1–6

There are far too many groups to test them all, so the paper uses six quick greedy searches on the LP picture.

  • Start from the connected pieces of the LP answer (roads with $x \gt 0$). In our example: $\{2\}$, $\{3,4,5,6\}$, $\{7\}$, $\{8\}$. Each piece is a candidate $S$.
  • Searches 1–2: shrink a piece while the rule stays broken: (1.4), (2.1), (2.9).
  • Search 3: try unions of pieces for (1.7) and (2.9).
  • Searches 4–6 (new): grow groups by merging neighbours, and look for the "$S$ plus groups $T$" pattern of (2.3)/(2.8).

Fixed order of checks, with Searches 4–6 only when 1–3 find nothing. Each search costs at most $O(n^3)$. The searches are heuristics: they can miss a broken rule, which branching then covers.

Keep this short unless asked. The key message: separation is heuristic; the paper's contribution is both new inequalities and searches that find them.

Backup · likely question

"Where do your results differ from the paper's, and why?"

  • Times 10–1,000× smaller: about 30 years of hardware and LP-solver progress, not the method.
  • A and B close: with our fixed root loop, (1.7) adds little that (1.4) and Search 1 don't already find.
  • More nodes on the tightest problems: the paper re-separates at every node until no cut is left and adds Gomory cuts; Gurobi gives our callback one or two rounds per node, so we branch more.
  • Slightly lower root bounds when one truck carries all: we add no Gomory cuts.
Different random problems too: same recipe, different draws, so cell averages compare statistically. With 30 per cell our root averages match within a few tenths of a point.
