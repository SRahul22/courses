      Why hold stock at all

      Inventory is stock bought before it is needed, and it exists because buying exactly what is needed, exactly when it is needed, is either impossible or too expensive.

      Every model in this course answers one of two questions about it: how much to order at a time, and when to order.

      Your kitchen at home: you buy a 5 kg bag of rice once a month instead of 170 g every day, because each trip to the shop costs you time.

      Meet the Banyan Store, a stationery shop on a university campus. It sells about 200 notebooks a month, steadily, all year: 2400 notebooks per year. The notebooks come from a wholesaler in the city, by van.

      The shop could ask the van to bring 7 notebooks every day. It does not, because every delivery has a cost of its own: the van trip, the paperwork, someone at the counter to check and shelve the boxes. So the shop orders a bigger batch less often, and the notebooks sit on the shelf until students buy them. That shelf of notebooks is the shop's inventory.

      Four reasons to hold stock

      • Batching. Each order carries a fixed cost, so it pays to order many units at once. The stock that results is used up between orders; it is called cycle stock. This is the reason behind the EOQ (Part 2) and Wagner–Whitin (Part 3).
      • Uncertainty. Demand next week is not known exactly. Extra units kept against a surprise are safety stock. This is the world of the newsvendor (Part 4) and Part 5.
      • Seasons. A factory with limited capacity builds stock before a festival rush that it could not produce in the rush itself.
      • Lead time. Orders take time to arrive, so goods already ordered but still on the road are stock too.

      The picture every model starts from

      Take the simplest possible world: demand is constant and known, an order arrives the moment it is placed, and the shop never runs out. Suppose the shop orders $Q = 400$ notebooks at a time. On delivery day the shelf jumps to 400. Students take 200 a month, so the shelf falls in a straight line and is empty after 2 months. At that instant the next 400 arrive, and the pattern repeats.

      Drawn over time, the stock level looks like the teeth of a saw: the sawtooth pattern. Three numbers describe it:

      The three costs that pull against each other

      Every inventory decision balances a cost for placing orders against a cost for keeping stock, with a third cost lurking for running out.

      Once you can price each of them, "how much should we order?" stops being a matter of taste and becomes arithmetic.

      Shopping for groceries once a year would save trips but fill your flat with food that spoils; shopping every hour would keep the fridge empty but eat your whole day.

      1. The ordering cost $K$: paid per order

      Each time the Banyan Store orders, it pays for the van trip, the paperwork and an hour of staff time to receive the boxes: about ₹300, whether the order is for 50 notebooks or 2000. A cost paid once per order, independent of its size, is a fixed cost or ordering cost. In a factory the same role is played by the set-up cost of a production run.

      If the shop orders $Q$ at a time, it places $D/Q$ orders per year, so

      $$\text{ordering cost per year} = K\cdot\frac{D}{Q}.$$

      2. The holding cost $H$: paid per unit, per unit of time

      A notebook costs the shop $C=$ ₹40. While it sits on the shelf, that ₹40 is tied up instead of earning interest elsewhere; it also takes space, needs insurance, and may get damaged or go out of fashion. Shops usually express all of this as a percentage of the unit cost per year. At 10% per year, holding one notebook for a year costs

      $$H = 0.10 \times 40 = 4 \text{ rupees per notebook per year}.$$

      This is the holding cost. On average $Q/2$ notebooks sit on the shelf, so

      $$\text{holding cost per year} = H\cdot\frac{Q}{2}.$$

      3. The shortage cost: paid when you run out

      If a student asks for a notebook and the shelf is empty, two things can happen. The student may wait and be served when the next delivery comes (a backorder), which costs goodwill and perhaps a discount. Or the student may leave and buy elsewhere (a lost sale), and the shop loses the profit on that notebook. Either way, the price of being short is a shortage cost.

      In the EOQ of Part 2 demand is known exactly, so a sensible plan never runs out and this cost plays no role. It becomes central in Part 4, when demand is uncertain.

      From one cycle to a cost per year

      Under constant demand every cycle is a copy of the first, so we can price one cycle exactly and then turn it into a cost per unit of time.

      The per-time version is the one to minimise; minimising the cost of a single cycle gives a misleading answer, and seeing why is half of understanding the EOQ.

      A monthly phone plan and a daily plan cannot be compared by their price tags; you divide each by the days it covers first.

      The lecture states the assumptions of the economic order quantity (EOQ) model:

      • time is continuous and the horizon is infinite (the business runs forever);
      • demand arrives at a constant rate $D$, known in advance;
      • each order costs $K$ and arrives instantly (zero lead time), so it is placed exactly when stock hits zero;
      • holding one unit for one unit of time costs $H$; inventory starts at zero.

      Because demand is uniform, the best policy repeats itself: the same $Q$ every time, a new order every $T = Q/D$. So deciding $Q$ fixes $T$, and the reverse.

      Step 1: the holding cost of one cycle is an area

      During a cycle that starts at time 0, the stock at time $t$ is $Q - Dt$. Holding costs $H$ per unit per unit time, so over a short moment $dt$ we pay $H(Q-Dt)\,dt$. Adding up over the cycle:

      $$\int_0^T H(Q - Dt)\,dt = HQT - \tfrac12 HDT^2.$$

      This is $H$ times the area of the triangle under one tooth: base $T$, height $Q$, area $\tfrac12 QT$. Substituting $T = Q/D$:

      $$\text{holding per cycle} = HQ\frac{Q}{D} - \frac12 HD\frac{Q^2}{D^2} = \frac{HQ^2}{2D}.$$

      Adding the one order, the cost of one cycle is $\;K + \dfrac{HQ^2}{2D}.$

      Step 2: why not minimise the cost of one cycle?

      The cycle cost $K + HQ^2/(2D)$ is smallest when $Q$ is as small as possible. But a tiny $Q$ makes cycles tiny too, and then there are very many of them: you pay $K$ over and over. The lecture asks exactly this ("optimizing this makes less sense, why?"). Cycles of different lengths are not comparable; costs per unit of time are.

      Step 3: divide by the cycle length

      Dividing the cycle cost by $T = Q/D$ gives the average cost per unit time:

      $$z(Q) = \frac{K + \frac{HQ^2}{2D}}{Q/D} = \underbrace{\frac{H}{2}Q}_{\text{holding}} + \underbrace{\frac{KD}{Q}}_{\text{ordering}}.$$

      These are the two yearly costs of Part 1, now derived from the cycle.

      The square-root formula, and why it forgives mistakes

      Setting the derivative of $z(Q)$ to zero gives the most famous formula in inventory theory, $Q^* = \sqrt{2KD/H}$.

      It tells you the best order size, the cost at that size, and, through its flat bottom, how much a rough estimate of the inputs will cost you.

      Like the speed at which a car uses the least fuel per kilometre: drive a bit faster or slower and you barely notice the difference.

      Minimise $z(Q)$ with one derivative

      The holding term $\tfrac H2 Q$ grows in a straight line; the ordering term $KD/Q$ falls like a hyperbola. Their sum is a bowl. Differentiate:

      $$z'(Q) = \frac{H}{2} - \frac{KD}{Q^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q^2 = \frac{2KD}{H}.$$

      The second derivative $z''(Q) = 2KD/Q^3$ is positive for every $Q>0$, so $z$ is convex and this stationary point is the global minimum.

      $$Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2KD}{H}}, \qquad z(Q^*) = \sqrt{2HKD}, \qquad T^* = \frac{Q^*}{D}.$$

      At $Q^*$ the yearly holding cost and the yearly ordering cost are equal, each $\sqrt{HKD/2}$.

      Why equal? At $Q^*$, $z'(Q^*)=0$ says $\tfrac H2 = KD/Q^{*2}$; multiply both sides by $Q^*$ and you get $\tfrac H2 Q^* = KD/Q^*$: holding equals ordering. Each is half of the total, which is how $z^* = 2\sqrt{HKD/2} = \sqrt{2HKD}$ comes out.

      Banyan Store: $Q^* = \sqrt{2\cdot300\cdot2400/4} = \sqrt{360{,}000} = 600$ notebooks, $z^* = \sqrt{2\cdot4\cdot300\cdot2400} = 2400$ rupees a year, 4 orders a year, one every 3 months. Holding: $4\cdot300 = 1200$; ordering: $4 \cdot 300 = 1200$.

      Set-up

      Suppose we place $n$ orders, and order $i$ covers an interval of length $T_i$, with $T_1 + \dots + T_n = T$. Order $i$ must bring $Q_i = DT_i$ units. Its holding cost is the triangle again, $\tfrac12 H D T_i^2$. The average cost per unit time over the season is

      $$\frac1T\left(nK + \sum_{i=1}^n \tfrac12 HD\,T_i^2\right).$$

      For a fixed $n$, only the sum of squares depends on the $T_i$, so we must solve

      $$\min \sum_{i=1}^n T_i^2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{i=1}^n T_i = T,\; T_i \ge 0.$$

      The minimum is at $T_i = T/n$ for every $i$.

      Why: if two intervals differ, say $a \ne b$, replace both by their average $\tfrac{a+b}{2}$. The total length is unchanged and $a^2 + b^2 - 2\left(\tfrac{a+b}2\right)^2 = \tfrac12(a-b)^2 > 0$, so the sum of squares drops. Any unequal plan can be improved, so the best plan has all intervals equal. (Equivalently: the objective is convex and symmetric, or use a Lagrange multiplier: $2T_i = \lambda$ for all $i$.)

      Lot sizing as an integer program

      With demand that changes from period to period, we must decide in which periods to order and how much, trading the fixed cost of each order against the cost of carrying stock forward.

      Writing it as a mixed-integer program makes every cost and constraint explicit, and it is the model the rest of this part simplifies.

      $s_t$

      One shot at the order: the critical ratio by intuition

      A little probability

      For continuous demand, the density $f(y)$ describes how probability is spread: the area under $f$ between two values is the probability that demand falls between them. The CDF is the area to the left: $F(x) = \int_0^x f(y)\,dy$, also written $\int_0^x dF(y)$. The expected value of any function $g(D)$ is $E[g(D)] = \int g(y)\,dF(y)$: each possible value weighted by its probability.

      The objective

      The lecture minimises expected cost (the negative of expected profit). We buy $x$ units at $C$, sell $\min(x, D)$ of them at $R$, and salvage the leftover $\max(0, x - D)$ at $V$:

      $$z(x) = Cx - R\,E[\min(x, D)] - V\,E[\max(0, x - D)].$$

      Step 1: the two expectations

      Sales equal demand when $D \le x$, and equal $x$ when $D \gt x$:

      $$E[\min(x, D)] = \int_0^x y\,dF(y) + \int_x^\infty x\,dF(y).$$

      Leftovers are $x - D$ when $D \le x$, else zero:

      $$E[\max(0, x - D)] = \int_0^x (x - y)\,dF(y).$$

      Step 2: rewrite around the mean

      Add and subtract $R\int_x^\infty y\,dF(y)$. The two integrals of $y$ combine into $E[D]$:

      $$z(x) = Cx - R\,E[D] + R\int_x^\infty (y - x)\,dF(y) - V\int_0^x (x - y)\,dF(y).$$

      The first integral is the expected shortfall, the second the expected leftover.

      Step 3: differentiate with the Leibniz rule

      Both integrals have $x$ in a limit and in the integrand, so we need the Leibniz rule:

      $$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{a(x)}^{b(x)} g(x, y)\,dy = g\big(x, b(x)\big)\,b'(x) - g\big(x, a(x)\big)\,a'(x) + \int_{a(x)}^{b(x)} \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\,dy.$$

      For the shortfall, $g = (y - x)f(y)$: the boundary term at $y = x$ is $(x - x)f(x) = 0$, and $\partial g/\partial x = -f(y)$, so the derivative is $-\int_x^\infty f(y)\,dy = -(1 - F(x))$. For the leftover, $g = (x - y)f(y)$: the boundary term vanishes again and the derivative is $\int_0^x f(y)\,dy = F(x)$. Therefore

      $$z'(x) = C - R\big(1 - F(x)\big) - V F(x) = (C - R) + (R - V)F(x).$$

      And $z''(x) = (R - V)f(x) \ge 0$ since $R \gt V$: $z$ is convex, so the stationary point is the minimum.

      $$F(x^*) = \frac{R - C}{R - V} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x^* = F^{-1}\!\left(\frac{R - C}{R - V}\right).$$

      Uniform demand on $[a, b]$: $x^* = a + \alpha(b - a)$. Normal demand with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$: $x^* = \mu + z_\alpha \sigma$, where $\alpha$ is the critical ratio and $z_\alpha$ its standard normal quantile.

      Add a set-up cost: the $(s, S)$ policy

      If every production run costs $K$ and some stock $x_0$ is already on hand, it is not worth topping up a nearly full shelf: you order only when stock is low enough.

      The result is the $(s, S)$ policy: when stock is at or below $s$, order up to $S$; otherwise do nothing.

      You refill a car's tank to full when the gauge drops to a quarter, not every time it shows less than full, because each stop costs time.

      The lecture's extension: we start with $x_0$ units and may produce more, bringing stock up to $x \ge x_0$. Producing costs $K$ (if we produce at all) plus $C$ per unit, so producing $x - x_0$ units and then facing demand costs

      $$K + C(x - x_0) + \big(z(x) - Cx\big) = K + z(x) - Cx_0,$$

      where $z(x)$ is the newsvendor expected cost from Chapter 4.2 (it already contains $Cx$). Not producing costs $z(x_0) - Cx_0$. The term $-Cx_0$ appears in both, so it never affects the choice.

      Two cases

      Let $S$ minimise $z(x)$: this is the newsvendor quantity $x^*$, now called the order-up-to level.

      • Case I: $x_0 \ge S$. Producing can only move us further from the bottom of $z$. Do nothing.
      • Case II: $x_0 \lt S$. If we produce, we go all the way to $S$, the best level, at cost $K + z(S)$. If not, we pay $z(x_0)$. Produce when $z(x_0) \gt K + z(S)$.

      Let $S = \arg\min z(x)$ and let $s \lt S$ satisfy $z(s) = K + z(S)$. Then the optimal rule is

      $$\text{if } x_0 \le s:\ \text{produce } S - x_0; \qquad \text{if } x_0 \gt s:\ \text{produce nothing.}$$

      $S$ is the order-up-to level and $s$ the reorder point (the slide calls $s$ a reorder quantity, but it is a stock level). With $K = 0$, $s = S$ and the rule is "always top up to $S$".

      Lead times, reorder points and safety stock

      When an order takes $L$ days to arrive, you must order before the shelf is empty, at a reorder point that covers demand during those $L$ days, plus a cushion if that demand is uncertain.

      The EOQ answers "how much", the reorder point answers "when", and the cushion, safety stock, is where uncertainty costs money every day.

      You start boiling water for tea a few minutes before you want it, and a little earlier if the kettle is unpredictable.

      Known lead time, known demand

      Suppose a warehouse uses 50 units a week, steadily, and the supplier takes $L = 2$ weeks to deliver. If it orders when the shelf hits zero, it waits two weeks with nothing. Instead it orders when the stock falls to the demand during the lead time:

      $$r = D \cdot L = 50 \times 2 = 100 \text{ units}.$$

      When the shelf reaches 100, an order goes out; two weeks and 100 units later, the shelf is empty exactly as the order arrives. The sawtooth is unchanged; only the timing of the order moved earlier. The order size is still the EOQ.

      If the lead time is longer than a cycle, several orders are in transit at once. Then the rule uses the inventory position (on hand plus on order) instead of the shelf: order when the position falls to $r$.

      Uncertain demand during the lead time

      Now say weekly demand has mean 50 and standard deviation 10, independently from week to week. Over $L = 2$ weeks, demand has mean $50 \times 2 = 100$, and variances add: the variance is $2 \times 10^2$, so the standard deviation is $10\sqrt{2} = 14.14$. Ordering at $r = 100$ runs out in about half of all cycles, whenever lead-time demand comes in above average.

      To run out less often, order earlier: add safety stock $SS$ on top of the mean, $r = \mu_L + SS$. The probability of not running out during a lead time is the cycle service level. With normal lead-time demand, a service level $\alpha$ needs

      $$r = \mu_L + z_\alpha\, \sigma_L, \qquad \mu_L = \mu L, \qquad \sigma_L = \sigma\sqrt{L},$$

      where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of demand per period. The safety stock is $z_\alpha \sigma\sqrt L$.

      For 95% service: $z_{0.95} = 1.645$, $SS = 1.645 \times 14.14 = 23.26$, $r = 123.26$. For 99%: $z_{0.99} = 2.326$, $SS = 32.9$. Those last few percentage points are expensive: the safety stock sits on the shelf all year, costing $H \cdot SS$ every year.

      This is the newsvendor idea again. Each lead time is a one-shot bet: the reorder point is a quantile of lead-time demand, and the service level plays the role of the critical ratio.

      Three EOQ variants: backorders, gradual production, discounts

      Each variant changes one assumption of the EOQ and keeps the same method: draw the inventory picture, write the cost per unit time, differentiate.

      Knowing them lets you adapt the EOQ when an exam or a real problem bends one assumption.

      A recipe you know well can be adjusted for fewer eggs or a smaller oven; the method stays, the quantities shift.

      All three use the Banyan Store: $D = 2400$ per year, $K = 300$, $H = 4$, with EOQ $Q^* = 600$ and yearly cost ₹2400.

      1. Planned backorders

      Suppose students accept waiting for a notebook, at a cost of $p$ per notebook per year of waiting. Then it pays to let the stock dip below zero before each delivery: the delivery first clears the waiting list. Holding less stock saves $H$; making customers wait costs $p$. The results:

      $$Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2KD}{H}}\sqrt{\frac{H + p}{p}}, \qquad \text{maximum backorder } B^* = Q^*\frac{H}{H + p}.$$

      With $p = 16$: $Q^* = 600\sqrt{20/16} = 670.82$, $B^* = 134.16$, and the yearly cost falls to $2400\sqrt{16/20} = ₹2146.6$. As $p \to \infty$ (waiting is unacceptable), the factor goes to 1 and we are back at the EOQ.

      2. Gradual production (finite production rate)

      If the shop makes its own notebooks at $P = 9600$ a year while selling 2400 a year, stock builds up at $P - D$ during a run instead of jumping by $Q$. The peak is only $Q(1 - D/P)$, so holding is cheaper and runs can be longer:

      $$Q^* = \sqrt{\frac{2KD}{H(1 - D/P)}} = \frac{600}{\sqrt{0.75}} = 692.82, \qquad \text{peak stock } 519.6, \qquad \text{cost } ₹2078.5.$$

      This is the economic production quantity (EPQ). As $P \to \infty$, it becomes the EOQ.
