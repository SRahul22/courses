    Calculus for GATE DA: limits, slopes and the best possible answer

    The DA syllabus keeps calculus to a single variable, and the questions are very consistent: evaluate a limit, check whether a piecewise function is continuous and differentiable, use a Taylor series, and find maxima and minima, often on an interval. This module covers exactly that, and ends with the optimisation ideas that machine learning is built on.

    You need: the derivatives refresher (Refreshers 3.1) and functions (Refreshers 1.1).

    The route

      Limits describe what a function is heading towards; continuity and differentiability describe whether its graph has gaps or sharp corners. GATE DA asks a limit almost every year (often a 0/0 form) and regularly asks whether a piecewise function, or ReLU, is continuous and differentiable.

      You need: functions and logs (Refreshers Part 1) and derivatives (Refreshers 3.1).

      Limits: where a function is heading

      $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ means $f(x)$ gets as close as you like to $L$ when $x$ is close enough to $a$, whatever happens exactly at $a$.

      A limit appears in almost every GATE DA paper, usually a “0/0” form that needs a standard limit, a conjugate, L’Hôpital’s rule or a Taylor series. They’re quick marks once you know the toolkit.

      It’s like a run chase that ends exactly at the target: the run rate needed tells you where the scoreboard is heading even before the last ball.

      Look at $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$. At $x = 2$ it’s $\frac00$: undefined. But for every other $x$ you can cancel: $f(x) = x + 2$. So as $x$ approaches 2, $f(x)$ approaches 4. The function has a hole at $x = 2$, and the limit fills it in: $\lim_{x\to2} f(x) = 4$.

      • One-sided limits: $x \to a^-$ (from the left) and $x \to a^+$ (from the right). The limit exists exactly when both exist and are equal.
      • Limits at infinity: what happens as $x$ grows without bound. For a ratio of polynomials, only the leading terms matter: $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 5} = 3$.
      • If $f$ is continuous at $a$ (no hole, jump or blow-up), the limit is just $f(a)$: substitute.
      • $\dfrac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$,   $\dfrac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} \to \dfrac12$
      • $\dfrac{e^x - 1}{x} \to 1$,   $\dfrac{\ln(1 + x)}{x} \to 1$
      • $(1 + x)^{1/x} \to e$

      Variants follow by substitution: $\frac{\sin 3x}{x} = 3\cdot\frac{\sin 3x}{3x} \to 3$; $\frac{\ln(1 + x^2)}{x^2} \to 1$.

      1. Factor and cancel (polynomials).
      2. Conjugate for square roots: multiply by $\frac{\sqrt{A} + B}{\sqrt{A} + B}$.
      3. Standard limits above, after a substitution.

      Continuity and differentiability: no gaps, no corners

      A function is continuous at a point if its graph has no gap or jump there, and differentiable if it also has no sharp corner: there’s a single well-defined tangent.

      GATE asks this every year: piecewise functions whose pieces must join smoothly (2024 Q37), whether ReLU is differentiable (2025 Q48), and which combinations of differentiable functions stay differentiable (2025 Q24).

      It’s like a road: continuous means no missing bridges; differentiable means no hairpin corners either, so you could drive it at speed without a sudden change of direction.

      Piecewise functions, ReLU and absolute values, and “always differentiable?” MSQs.

      2024 Q37 · make a piecewise function smooth2025 Q48 · ReLU2025 Q24 · sums, products, compositions2025 Q59 · |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ (x − y)²
      Heat: every year, 1–4 marks

      $f$ is continuous at $a$ if $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)$: the limit exists, $f(a)$ exists, and they agree.

      $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if $f'(a) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$ exists. For that, the left slope (with $h \to 0^-$) must equal the right slope ($h \to 0^+$).

      Differentiable ⇒ continuous, but not the other way round: $|x|$ and ReLU are continuous at 0 but have corners there (left slope ≠ right slope).

      At each boundary point, write two equations:

      1. Values match (continuity): left piece = right piece at the boundary.
      2. Slopes match (differentiability): derivative of the left piece = derivative of the right piece there.

      Two boundaries give four equations, enough for the unknown coefficients (and to check the options).

      If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at $c$: $f \pm g$, $fg$, and $f/g$ (when $g(c) \ne 0$) are differentiable at $c$. A composition $f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $c$ if $g$ is differentiable at $c$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(c)$. Knowing $f$ only at $c$ isn’t enough for $f\circ g$.

      Taylor series: rebuilding a function from one point

      A Taylor series writes a function as a polynomial built from its derivatives at a single point: $f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \cdots$.

      It’s named in the syllabus, it powers quick limit calculations, and GATE has used it to ask for a 10th derivative in seconds (2025 Q14). In ML, Taylor’s second-order approximation is how Newton’s method and many optimisers work.

      It’s like predicting a car’s position from a single instant: where it is, how fast it’s going, how fast that’s changing, and so on. Each extra piece of information makes the prediction good further into the future.

      Maxima and minima: finding the turning points

      At a smooth peak or valley the slope is zero; the second derivative tells which it is; and on an interval the true largest and smallest values come from comparing those turning points with the endpoints.

      An extrema question has appeared in every GATE DA paper (2024 Q50, 2025 Q49, 2026 Q27), usually as an MSQ with subtle options about “local” vs “global” and about endpoints.

      It’s like hiking a ridge with a map: peaks and dips are where the path is momentarily flat, but the highest point of your hike might be where the trail starts or ends.

      • $x^*$ is a local maximum if $f(x^*) \ge f(x)$ for all $x$ near $x^*$; a global maximum if that holds for every $x$ in the domain. Same for minimum with $\le$.
      • A critical point is where $f'(x) = 0$ or $f'$ doesn’t exist. Interior local extrema can only happen at critical points.
      • First derivative test: $f'$ changes from + to − ⇒ local max; − to + ⇒ local min; no sign change ⇒ neither.
      • Second derivative test: $f'(x^*) = 0$ and $f''(x^*) \lt 0$ ⇒ local max; $f''(x^*) \gt 0$ ⇒ local min. If $f''(x^*) = 0$ the test says nothing: $x^4$ has a minimum at 0, $x^3$ has neither.
      1. Find the critical points inside $(a, b)$.
      2. Evaluate $f$ at those points and at the endpoints $a$ and $b$.
      3. The largest value is the global max; the smallest is the global min.

      On an open or half-open interval, an extreme value may not be attained at all: the function can approach a value at an excluded endpoint without ever reaching it.

      Convexity: when a minimum is guaranteed to be the minimum

      A convex function curves upward everywhere ($f'' \ge 0$), so any local minimum is automatically the global minimum, and slope-following methods can’t get stuck in the wrong valley.

      GATE 2025 Q51 tested exactly what $f'' \gt 0$ guarantees. In ML, convex losses (squared error, logistic loss) are why linear and logistic regression always train to the same best answer.

      It’s like a bowl: drop a ball anywhere inside and it rolls to the single lowest point. A non-convex landscape is an egg tray, where the ball can settle in any one of many dips.

      $f$ is convex if every chord lies on or above the graph: $f(tx + (1-t)y) \le tf(x) + (1-t)f(y)$ for $t\in[0, 1]$. For twice-differentiable $f$: convex ⇔ $f''(x) \ge 0$ everywhere. If $f'' \gt 0$ everywhere it’s strictly convex. Examples: $x^2$, $e^x$, $|x|$, $-\ln x$ (for $x \gt 0$). Sums of convex functions are convex.

      • Any local minimum of a convex function is a global minimum.
      • If $f'' \gt 0$ everywhere, then $f'$ is strictly increasing, so $f' = 0$ at at most one point: at most one minimiser, and no maxima.
      • Convexity doesn’t guarantee a minimum exists: $e^x$ is convex but keeps decreasing to the left, never reaching a lowest value.

      Gradient descent (1-D): $x \leftarrow x - \eta f'(x)$. Newton’s method: $x \leftarrow x - \dfrac{f'(x)}{f''(x)}$, which jumps to the minimum of the local quadratic (second-order Taylor) approximation. On a quadratic it lands exactly on the minimum in one step.
