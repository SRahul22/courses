    Go deeper: Pascal's rule and why C(n, r) = C(n, n − r)

    Choosing which $r$ players are in is the same as choosing which $n - r$ are out, so $\binom nr = \binom n{n-r}$. And $\binom nr = \binom{n-1}{r-1} + \binom{n-1}{r}$: a group of $r$ either includes a particular player (choose the other $r-1$ from $n-1$) or doesn't (choose all $r$ from $n-1$). This rule builds Pascal's triangle.

    Sample spaces, events and the rules of probability

    A probability assigns each event a number between 0 and 1, following three simple axioms; every other rule (complements, unions, "at least one") follows from them.

    These rules are used in almost every probability question, and "mutually exclusive vs independent" is a favourite source of trick options.

    It's like a 100-rupee budget spread over outcomes: every outcome gets a non-negative share, the shares total exactly 100, and the money for a group of outcomes is the sum of their shares.

    Where it shows up in GATE DA

    Inside nearly every probability question; sometimes on its own as a complement or independence question.

    An experiment is anything with an uncertain result: a toss, a match, a delivery. The sample space $S$ (sometimes $\Omega$) is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is a subset of $S$: a collection of outcomes we care about. For the Bengaluru Backprops' next two tosses, $S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$, and "at least one head" is the event $\{HH, HT, TH\}$.

    1. $P(A) \ge 0$ for every event $A$.
    2. $P(S) = 1$: something certainly happens.
    3. If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive (disjoint: $A \cap B = \varnothing$), then $P(A\cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$. (The same holds for any countable list of pairwise disjoint events.)
    • Complement: $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$. Your best friend for "at least one" questions.
    • Union: $P(A\cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A\cap B)$ (inclusion–exclusion again).
    • Equally likely outcomes: $P(A) = |A|/|S|$.
    • $P(\varnothing) = 0$ and $0 \le P(A) \le 1$.

    $A$ and $B$ are independent if $P(A\cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Intuitively: knowing that $A$ happened doesn't change the chance of $B$. Tosses of different coins, or throws of different dice, are independent.

    Careful: mutually exclusive and independent are very different. Mutually exclusive events can't happen together, so if one happens the other certainly didn't, which is the opposite of "no information". Two mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent: $P(A\cap B) = 0 \ne P(A)P(B)$.

    Go deeper: independence of three or more events

    Events $A, B, C$ are mutually independent if every pair satisfies the product rule and $P(A\cap B\cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C)$. Pairwise independence alone isn't enough. Classic example: toss two coins; $A$ = first is heads, $B$ = second is heads, $C$ = both match. Each pair is independent, but $P(A\cap B\cap C) = \tfrac14 \ne \tfrac18$.

    Conditional probability: updating on new information

    $P(A\mid B)$ is the probability of $A$ once you know $B$ happened: you shrink the world down to $B$ and ask what fraction of it is also $A$.

    It's the engine behind Bayes' theorem, Naive Bayes classifiers and Bayesian networks, three topics that between them appear in every GATE DA paper.

    It's like a cricket commentator updating predictions: "the Backprops win 50% of their matches, but when they win the toss it's 60%". Same team, new information, new probability.

    Where it shows up in GATE DA

    Inside every Bayes, Naive Bayes and Bayesian-network question, and in tree-style word problems.

    Throw two dice and suppose you're told the first die shows 3. The 36 outcomes shrink to six: (3,1), …, (3,6). Among those, only (3,5) has a sum of 8. So $P(\text{sum} = 8 \mid \text{first} = 3) = \tfrac16$, even though without the information $P(\text{sum} = 8) = \tfrac{5}{36}$.

    That's all conditional probability is: restrict to the outcomes where $B$ happened, then see what fraction of them are in $A$.

    For $P(B) \gt 0$, $$P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.$$ Read the bar "∣" as "given". Rearranged, this is the multiplication rule: $P(A\cap B) = P(B)\,P(A\mid B) = P(A)\,P(B\mid A)$.

    If $B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_k$ split the sample space into non-overlapping pieces (a partition), then $$P(A) = \sum_{i} P(B_i)\,P(A\mid B_i).$$ Work out the chance of $A$ in each scenario, weight each by how likely that scenario is, and add. With two scenarios: $P(A) = P(B)P(A\mid B) + P(B^c)P(A\mid B^c)$.

    A tree diagram makes both rules visual. The first branching is the scenario ($B$ or not $B$); the second is the outcome ($A$ or not $A$) with conditional probabilities on the branches. Multiply along a path to get the probability of that path; add the paths that end in $A$ to get $P(A)$.

    Independence, restated: $A$ and $B$ are independent exactly when $P(A\mid B) = P(A)$. Learning $B$ tells you nothing about $A$.

    Go deeper: conditional probability is a probability

    For a fixed $B$, the function $A \mapsto P(A\mid B)$ satisfies all three axioms: it's non-negative, $P(B\mid B) = 1$, and it adds over disjoint events. So every rule you know (complements, unions) also works "given $B$": $P(A^c\mid B) = 1 - P(A\mid B)$. This is why you can keep conditioning on more and more information, which is what Bayesian networks do.

    Bayes' theorem turns $P(\text{evidence}\mid\text{cause})$, which is usually easy to know, into $P(\text{cause}\mid\text{evidence})$, which is what you actually want.

    A random variable is a rule that assigns a number to each outcome. Toss three coins and let $X$ = number of heads; the outcome HTH gives $X = 2$. We use capital letters for random variables and lower-case for particular values: "$P(X = 2)$" means "the probability that the number of heads is 2".

    • Discrete random variables take separate values you can list: 0, 1, 2, 3 heads; number of wickets.
    • Continuous random variables take any value in an interval: a bowler's speed, the time until a battery dies.

    The probability mass function is $p(x) = P(X = x)$. Every $p(x) \ge 0$ and $\sum_x p(x) = 1$. For three coins: $p(0) = \tfrac18$, $p(1) = \tfrac38$, $p(2) = \tfrac38$, $p(3) = \tfrac18$.

    A probability density function $f(x) \ge 0$ gives probabilities as area: $$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1.$$ Two surprises: $P(X = a) = 0$ for any single value (a line has no area), and $f(x)$ can be bigger than 1 (it's a density, not a probability).

    The cumulative distribution function is $F(x) = P(X \le x)$. It is non-decreasing, goes from 0 (far left) to 1 (far right), and is right-continuous. For a discrete variable it's a staircase with a jump of $p(x)$ at each value; for a continuous one it's smooth, and $f(x) = F'(x)$.

    • $P(a \lt X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)$.
    • $P(X \gt a) = 1 - F(a)$.
    • Median: the $m$ with $F(m) = \tfrac12$.
    • For continuous $X$, "$\lt$" and "$\le$" give the same probabilities.

    Expectation, variance and covariance: the summary numbers

    The expectation is the long-run average of a random variable; the variance measures how far it typically strays from that average; the covariance measures whether two random variables move together.

    Means, variances, covariance and correlation appear in at least two questions every year, and they're the language of regression, PCA and every estimator in statistics.

    Measuring things: uniform, exponential and normal

    Uniform means every value in a range is equally likely; exponential models waiting times and forgets the past; the normal is the bell curve that averages of almost anything end up following.

    The exponential distribution has been in every GATE DA paper. The normal sits behind z-scores, the Central Limit Theorem, and the χ² and t distributions used in tests.

    The normal distribution and z-scores

    The normal $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the bell curve centred at $\mu$ with spread $\sigma$. About 68% of the probability lies within $\mu \pm \sigma$, 95% within $\mu \pm 2\sigma$, and 99.7% within $\mu \pm 3\sigma$. Any normal can be converted to the standard normal $Z \sim N(0, 1)$ by $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$, the z-score: how many standard deviations $X$ is from its mean. Its CDF is written $\Phi(z)$, and by symmetry $\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)$.

    The Central Limit Theorem (covered properly in Statistics 2.2) says a sum of many independent random variables is approximately normal. So a Binomial($n, p$) count is roughly $N(np,\ np(1-p))$ for large $n$.

    Two relatives you'll meet in statistics

    • Chi-squared $\chi^2_k$: the sum of squares of $k$ independent standard normals. Mean $k$, variance $2k$. $\chi^2_2$ happens to be exponential with mean 2.
    • Student's t: looks like the standard normal but with heavier tails (extreme values are more likely). With more degrees of freedom it approaches $N(0,1)$; with 1 degree of freedom it's the very heavy-tailed Cauchy.

    You need: Parts 2–4, and double integrals over simple regions (Refreshers 3.2).

    Two random variables at once

    A joint distribution gives the probability of every combination of values of $X$ and $Y$; summing out one variable gives the other's marginal distribution.

    Covariance, correlation, conditional probability and independence of random variables all start from the joint distribution. GATE uses joint tables and joint densities on triangles and squares.

    It's like a league table cross-tabulated by toss and result: each cell counts the matches with that combination, and the row and column totals are the marginals.

    For discrete $X, Y$: $p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)$, with $\sum_x\sum_y p(x, y) = 1$. The marginal PMFs are $$p_X(x) = \sum_y p(x, y),\qquad p_Y(y) = \sum_x p(x, y).$$ In a table: marginals are the row and column totals, written in the margins, hence the name.

    For continuous $X, Y$: a joint density $f(x, y) \ge 0$ with total volume 1, and $P((X,Y)\in R) = \iint_R f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$. Marginal: $f_X(x) = \int f(x, y)\,dy$ (integrate out $y$). Watch the support: if $f$ lives on a triangle like $0 \lt y \lt x \lt 2$, the limits of integration depend on $x$.

    $X$ and $Y$ are independent if $p(x, y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)$ for every pair $(x, y)$ (or $f(x,y) = f_X(x)f_Y(y)$). One cell that fails the test is enough to break independence. A quick warning sign for densities: if the support isn't a rectangle (e.g. a triangle), they can't be independent.

    (The four options are formulas; we derive the answer directly.)

    1. Marginal of $Y$: integrate out $x$. $f_Y(y) = \int_0^2 \frac{x(1+3y^2)}{4}\,dx = \frac{1+3y^2}{4}\cdot\Big[\frac{x^2}{2}\Big]_0^2 = \frac{1+3y^2}{2}$.

      $(1+3y^2)$ doesn't depend on $x$, so it comes out of the integral.

    2. Conditional density: $f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) = \dfrac{f(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} = \dfrac{x(1+3y^2)/4}{(1+3y^2)/2} = \dfrac{x}{2}$ for $0 \le x \le 2$.

      The same "joint ÷ marginal" rule as for events, now for densities.

    3. The answer doesn't depend on $y$ at all, and $f(x,y)$ factors as $\frac x2\cdot\frac{1+3y^2}{2}$ on a rectangle. So $X$ and $Y$ are independent, and $f_{X\mid Y} = f_X = x/2$.

      Shortcut worth remembering: if a joint density factors into an $x$-part times a $y$-part on a rectangle, the variables are independent and each part (normalised) is a marginal.

    Conditional expectation: the best guess of Y once you know X

    $E[Y\mid X = x]$ is the average of $Y$ among only those outcomes where $X = x$, and averaging those averages over $X$ gives back $E[Y]$ (the tower rule).

    It's in the syllabus by name ("conditional expectation and variance"), GATE has asked the tower rule directly, and regression is literally the estimation of $E[Y\mid X]$.

    It's like predicting a batter's score once you know the pitch type: you average only over past innings on that kind of pitch. Average those pitch-wise predictions, weighted by how often each pitch occurs, and you're back to the overall average.

    Given $X = x$, the conditional PMF/PDF of $Y$ is $p(y\mid x) = \frac{p(x,y)}{p_X(x)}$ (or $f(y\mid x) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f_X(x)}$). Its mean is the conditional expectation $$E[Y\mid X = x] = \sum_y y\,p(y\mid x)\quad\text{or}\quad\int y\,f(y\mid x)\,dy.$$ This is a function of $x$. Plugging in the random $X$ gives a random variable, written $E[Y\mid X]$.

    $$E\big[E[Y\mid X]\big] = E[Y].$$ Average the conditional averages, weighted by how likely each $x$ is, and you recover the overall average. It's the expectation version of the law of total probability.

    $$\mathrm{Var}(Y) = E\big[\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid X)\big] + \mathrm{Var}\big(E[Y\mid X]\big).$$ Total spread = average spread within each group + spread between the group averages. This split reappears as "explained vs unexplained variance" in regression.
