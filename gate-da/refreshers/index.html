    Functions: machines that turn one number into another

    A function takes an input number and returns exactly one output number, and its graph is a picture of every input–output pair at once.

    Probability distributions, loss functions, activation functions, eigenvalue equations: almost everything in GATE DA is a function. Reading one quickly is a skill you'll use in every module.

    It's like a vending machine: press button B4 and you always get the same snack. Same input, same output, every time.

    Suppose the Data Premier League charges ₹200 per ticket, plus a ₹50 booking fee per order. If you buy $x$ tickets, you pay $200x + 50$ rupees. That rule is a function. We give it a name, say $f$, and write

    $$f(x) = 200x + 50.$$

    Read $f(x)$ as "f of x": the output when the input is $x$. So $f(3) = 200\cdot 3 + 50 = 650$. The letter $x$ is just a placeholder; $f(t) = 200t + 50$ is the same function.

    The graph of $f$ is the set of all points $(x, f(x))$ drawn on a grid. The horizontal axis holds inputs and the vertical axis holds outputs. A graph lets you see a function's whole behaviour at once: where it rises, where it falls, where it crosses zero.

    A function $f$ assigns to each allowed input $x$ exactly one output $f(x)$. The set of allowed inputs is the domain. "$f:\R\to\R$" means: inputs are real numbers, outputs are real numbers.

    The five shapes you'll meet constantly

    • Line $f(x) = mx + c$. The slope $m$ says how much the output rises per unit step right; $c$ is where it crosses the vertical axis.
    • Parabola $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$. A U shape if $a \gt 0$, an upside-down U if $a \lt 0$. Its turning point (the vertex) sits at $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$.
    • Absolute value $|x|$: "drop the minus sign". $|{-3}| = 3$, $|3| = 3$. Its graph is a V. Geometrically, $|a - b|$ is the distance between $a$ and $b$ on the number line.
    • ReLU $\max(0, x)$: zero for negative inputs, the input itself otherwise. It's the most common activation function in neural networks.
    • Piecewise functions use different rules on different stretches of the input. ReLU is one; so is the ticket function above if bulk orders get a discount.

    Solving equations and inequalities

    "Solve $f(x) = k$" means: which inputs give output $k$? On the graph, draw a horizontal line at height $k$ and read off where it hits the curve.

    • Linear: $200x + 50 = 1050$ ⇒ $200x = 1000$ ⇒ $x = 5$. Do the same thing to both sides until $x$ is alone.
    • Quadratic: $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ has solutions $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. The part under the root, $b^2 - 4ac$, is the discriminant: positive means two real roots, zero means one, negative means none (the parabola never touches the axis). You'll meet this again with eigenvalues.
    • Inequalities work like equations, with one twist: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number flips the sign. $-2x \gt 6$ ⇒ $x \lt -3$.
    Careful: with fractions, cross-multiplying $\dfrac{p}{q} = \dfrac{r}{s}$ into $ps = qr$ is fine for equations. For inequalities it is only safe when you know the denominators are positive.

    Let $X$ be a continuous random variable whose cumulative distribution function (CDF) $F_X(x)$, for some $t$, is given as follows:

    $$F_X(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \le t \\ \dfrac{x-t}{4-t} & t \le x \le 4 \\ 1 & x \ge 4 \end{cases}$$

    If the median of $X$ is 3, then what is the value of $t$?

    1. 2
    2. 1
    3. −1
    4. 0

    You don't need to know what a CDF is yet (that's Probability, Part 3). All you need: the median is the input where $F_X$ equals ½. Everything else is reading a piecewise function and solving an equation.

    1. The median is 3, so $F_X(3) = \tfrac12$.

      This is the one fact we're given. It turns the question into an equation.

    2. Which piece applies at $x = 3$? The middle one, as long as $t \le 3 \le 4$. So $\dfrac{3-t}{4-t} = \dfrac12$.

      A piecewise function uses different formulas on different stretches. The outer pieces are flat at 0 and 1, and neither can equal ½, so it must be the middle piece.

    3. Cross-multiply: $2(3 - t) = 4 - t$ ⇒ $6 - 2t = 4 - t$ ⇒ $t = 2$.

      Safe for an equation. Collect the $t$ terms on one side: $6 - 4 = 2t - t$.

    4. Check: $t = 2 \le 3 \le 4$ ✓, and $F_X(3) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ ✓. Answer (A) 2.

      Always check that your answer is consistent with the assumption you made in step 2 (here, that 3 lies in the middle piece). On GATE, plugging your answer back in costs ten seconds and catches most slips. The official key agrees: (A).

    Exponentials and logarithms: growth, decay and undoing them

    An exponential multiplies by the same factor at every step; a logarithm undoes that, answering "how many steps does it take?"

    The exponential distribution has appeared in every GATE DA paper so far, and you can't touch it without $e^{-\lambda x}$ and $\ln$. The sigmoid in logistic regression and the entropy in decision trees are built from the same pieces.

    It's like a rumour: each person tells two more, so the count doubles every round. The exponential tells you how many people know after 10 rounds; the logarithm tells you how many rounds until a million people know.

    Where it shows up in GATE DA

    Every paper. Most often inside exponential-distribution questions, where you solve $e^{-\lambda x} = p$ for $\lambda$ or $x$.

    Powers first

    $2^5$ means five 2s multiplied together: $2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2 = 32$. The small raised number is the exponent. Five rules cover nearly everything:

    RuleExampleWhy

    $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$$(2^3)^2 = 2^6$two groups of three twos
    $a^0 = 1$$7^0 = 1$$a^n / a^n = a^{n-n}$
    $a^{-n} = 1/a^n$$2^{-3} = 1/8$dividing undoes multiplying

    The special number e

    Mathematicians favour one base above all others: $e \approx 2.71828$. It's the base where the slope of $e^x$ at any point equals its height. That makes calculus with $e^x$ unusually clean (you'll see why in the derivatives chapter). On GATE, almost every exponential is written with base $e$.

    • Growth $e^{x}$: shoots up quickly as $x$ increases.
    • Decay $e^{-\lambda x}$ with $\lambda \gt 0$: starts at 1 when $x = 0$ and slides towards 0, never reaching it. Bigger $\lambda$ means faster decay. In GATE, $e^{-\lambda x}$ is the probability that a waiting time is longer than $x$.

    $\log_b y$ is the exponent you put on $b$ to get $y$: $$\log_b y = x \iff b^x = y.$$ The natural log $\ln$ is $\log_e$. So $\ln e^3 = 3$, $e^{\ln 5} = 5$, $\log_2 32 = 5$, $\log_{10} 1000 = 3$. Logs exist only for positive $y$.

    • $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$ (multiplying adds exponents)
    • $\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b$
    • $\ln(a^k) = k\ln a$, so $\ln(1/4) = -\ln 4$
    • $\ln 1 = 0$, $\ln e = 1$
    • Change of base: $\log_2 x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln 2}$

    Sums and series: adding up many things at once

    $\sum$ is shorthand for "add these up", and a geometric series, where each term is a fixed fraction of the last, can add up to a finite number even with infinitely many terms.

    Means, variances and expected values are all sums. Several GATE questions are really "can you sum this series?" in disguise, and in 2026 one was literally a double sum.

    It's like eating half the remaining cake each day: you'll never quite finish it, but you'll never eat more than one cake in total.

    Where it shows up in GATE DA

    Directly in series questions, and indirectly in every mean, variance and expected-value calculation.

    Last season the Haryana Heuristics' opener scored $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{14}$ runs in 14 matches. The total is $x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_{14}$, which is tedious to write. Sigma notation compresses it:

    $$\sum_{i=1}^{14} x_i = x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_{14}.$$

    Read it as "add up $x_i$ for $i$ from 1 to 14". The letter $i$ is the index; it's a placeholder, so $\sum_{k=1}^{14} x_k$ means the same thing. The average is then $\bar x = \frac{1}{14}\sum_{i=1}^{14} x_i$.

    • Constants come out: $\sum c\,x_i = c\sum x_i$.
    • Sums split: $\sum (x_i + y_i) = \sum x_i + \sum y_i$.
    • Adding a constant $n$ times: $\sum_{i=1}^{n} c = nc$.
    • Products do not split: $\sum x_i y_i \ne \left(\sum x_i\right)\left(\sum y_i\right)$ in general.

    Two patterns you should know cold

    Arithmetic series (each term adds the same amount): $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$. The trick: pair the first and last terms, $1 + n$, then the second and second-last, $2 + (n-1)$, and so on. Every pair sums to $n+1$, and there are $n/2$ pairs.

    Geometric series (each term multiplies by the same ratio $r$): $$a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{n-1} = a\,\frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r}.$$ If $|r| \lt 1$, then $r^n \to 0$ as $n$ grows, and the infinite sum settles down to $$a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1-r}.$$ If $|r| \ge 1$, the terms don't shrink and the sum runs off to infinity (or never settles).

    Sets: the language of "and", "or" and "not"

    A set is a collection of distinct things, and union, intersection and complement are just "or", "and" and "not" made precise.

    Derivatives: the slope at a single point

    The derivative $f'(x)$ is the slope of the curve at the point $x$: how fast the output changes when you nudge the input.

    Finding maxima and minima, training neural networks with backpropagation, and the sigmoid's famous $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ all come down to derivatives. GATE asks one directly almost every year.

    It's like the speedometer in a car. The odometer tells you distance (the function); the speedometer tells you how fast that distance is changing right now (the derivative).

    Where it shows up in GATE DA

    Directly in calculus questions, and inside every optimisation and neural-network question.

    For a straight line the slope is easy: rise over run, the same everywhere. A curve's steepness changes from point to point. To find the slope at one point $x$, pick a second point a tiny step $h$ away, draw the line through both (a secant), and measure its slope:

    $$\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}.$$

    Now shrink $h$ towards 0. The secant swings round and settles on the tangent line, the line that just touches the curve at $x$. Its slope is the derivative.

    $$f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.$$ Other notations: $\dfrac{df}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}f(x)$. If $f'(x) \gt 0$ the function is rising at $x$; if $f'(x) \lt 0$ it is falling; if $f'(x) = 0$ it is momentarily flat (a candidate for a peak or valley).

    Integrals: adding up area under a curve

    The integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the area under the curve of $f$ between $a$ and $b$, and you compute it by running a derivative backwards.

    For continuous random variables, probability is area under a curve. Every exponential, normal or uniform question in GATE hides an integral, and many of them can be done with plain geometry.

    It's like filling a petrol tank: the pump's flow rate changes over time, and the total fuel is the area under the flow-rate curve.

    Gradients: which way is downhill?

    For a function of several variables, each partial derivative measures the slope in one direction, and the gradient collects them into an arrow pointing uphill.

    Machine learning trains models by walking downhill on a loss surface (gradient descent). GATE has asked you to perform one step of it, and to compute gradients through a neural network.

    It's like standing on a foggy hillside: you can't see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, and you step that way.

    A function can take two inputs: $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$. Its graph is a surface, like a landscape. To draw it on paper we use a contour map: curves joining points of equal height, exactly like the contour lines on a hiking map.

    To measure slope, freeze all inputs but one. The partial derivative $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ treats $y$ as a constant and differentiates in $x$ as usual. For $f = x^2 + 3y^2$: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x$ and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 6y$.

    $$\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right).$$ At each point, $\nabla f$ is an arrow pointing in the direction of steepest ascent, and it's perpendicular to the contour line through that point. Its length is how steep it is. So $-\nabla f$ points steepest downhill.

    Start at some point $\mathbf w$. Repeat: $$\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf w),$$ where $\eta \gt 0$ (eta) is the learning rate, the step size. Too small and progress is slow; too large and you overshoot the valley and may bounce out of it. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) uses the gradient computed on one data point at a time.

    A vector in $\R^n$ is a list of $n$ real numbers, written as a column: $\v = \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}$. In two dimensions you can draw it as an arrow from the origin to the point $(3, 1)$. In 3 or 100 dimensions you can't draw it, but every rule below still works.

    • Add entry by entry: $\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\3\end{bmatrix}$. As arrows: place the second at the tip of the first.
    • Scale by a number: $2\v = \begin{bmatrix}6\\2\end{bmatrix}$, the same direction and twice as long. A negative number flips the direction.
